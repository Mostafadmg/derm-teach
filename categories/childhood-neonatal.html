    Childhood & neonatal

    Module 6 Week 1: neonatal pustular rashes, infant acne, nappy rash, vascular and pigmented birthmarks, epidermolysis bullosa and aplasia cutis.

    Neonatal pustular rashes

    Erythema toxicum, TNPM and miliaria are benign and need reassurance. Neonatal HSV is the one you must never ‘wait and see’ on. Includes a one-table differential.

    Never miss: Neonatal HSV: grouped vesicles, or an unwell, febrile or poorly feeding baby. Same-day emergency pathway.

    Neonatal & infantile acne

    Neonatal acne (birth to 6 weeks) is common and harmless. Infantile acne (6 weeks to about 16 months) can scar and may need treatment. Mid-childhood acne needs an endocrine review.

    Never miss: Acne between ages 1 and 6, signs of hyperandrogenism, or nodular or scarring disease. Refer.

    Napkin (nappy) dermatitis

    Irritant rash on the convex skin with the folds spared, or Candida with fold involvement and satellites. Use barrier ointment at every change, with short-course hydrocortisone and/or an azole.

    Never miss: An unwell infant, an infant under 1 month, ulceration, non-response (zinc deficiency, LCH), or safeguarding concerns.

    Infantile haemangioma & port-wine stain

    A haemangioma is a tumour that grows fast, then involutes. A port-wine stain is a malformation that persists. Refer high-risk haemangiomas early, while propranolol works best.

    Never miss: Periocular, airway or beard, lip, ulcerated, large facial segmental (PHACE), or 5 or more lesions (liver).

    Pigmented & epidermal birthmarks

    Naevus of Ota, achromic and anaemic naevi, Becker naevus, Mongolian spots and linear epidermal naevi. Mostly benign and needing reassurance, with key exceptions: the eye in Ota, tuberous sclerosis, and documenting Mongolian spots for safeguarding.

    Never miss: Ocular Ota (glaucoma), multiple ash-leaf macules (tuberous sclerosis), extensive or syndromic epidermal naevus, and misreading a Mongolian spot as a bruise.

    Epidermolysis bullosa (EB)

    Inherited skin fragility in which minimal friction causes blisters. Recognise it, handle the baby gently, and refer urgently to a specialist EB centre. Primary care supports under the specialist plan.

    Never miss: Airway involvement (hoarse cry in JEB), sepsis, dehydration or failure to thrive, and SCC in chronic RDEB wounds.

    Aplasia cutis congenita

    Congenital focal absence of skin, usually on the vertex scalp. Small superficial lesions heal with gentle care. The hair collar sign, large size or a midline position means imaging and urgent specialist care.

    Never miss: Hair collar sign (possible neural tube defect), a large midline defect (sagittal sinus), and ACC with blistering (EB) or limb defects (Adams–Oliver).
