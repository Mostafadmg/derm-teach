    Ulcers & red leg

    Venous, arterial and neuropathic ulcers, pyoderma gangrenosum, and the red-leg differential, so you don’t treat every red leg as cellulitis.

    4 pages · 21 images

    Module 5 · Week 2

    Venous leg ulcer

    The commonest chronic leg ulcer: a shallow, sloping, exudative ulcer in the gaiter area. Compression after ABPI is the cornerstone. Colonised does not mean infected.

    Never missArterial disease before compressing, and a non-healing or atypical ulcer (malignancy, PG, vasculitis).

    Pyoderma gangrenosum

    A rare, very painful, rapidly enlarging neutrophilic ulcer with a purple undermined edge. It is not an infection. Avoid debridement and refer urgently to dermatology.

    Never missMisdiagnosis as an infected ulcer, with repeated antibiotics and surgical debridement that make it worse.

    Arterial & neuropathic ulcers

    Ischaemic ulcers are punched-out, painful and distal, and must never be compressed. Neuropathic ulcers are painless, on plantar pressure points and surrounded by callus. Any diabetic foot ulcer needs urgent referral (NG19).

    Never missCritical limb ischaemia, osteomyelitis (probe to bone), wet gangrene, and a ‘small’ plantar ulcer in diabetes.

    Red leg: differentials & referral

    A structured approach to the red leg: is it really cellulitis? Bilateral, chronic, itchy redness is usually venous. Plus a one-table guide to when to refer urgently.
