    Acne scars

    Atrophic acne scars (ice-pick, boxcar, rolling), how to tell scar from pigment, and which specialist modalities suit which scar. Prevention is treating active acne early; scarring is a NICE referral trigger.

    Also calledacne scarringice-pick scarsboxcar scarsrolling scarsatrophic scarspost-inflammatory hyperpigmentation+2
    Related pagesAcne vulgarisKeloids and hypertrophic scarsCosmetic procedures: peels, needling & lasersRetinoids (topical & oral)

    Key facts

    • Atrophic acne scars follow collagen loss after inflammatory acne: ice-pick, boxcar and rolling.
    • Ice-pick scars are the commonest (about 60–70%).
    • Separate true scars from post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation or erythema (flat colour change, which fades).
    • Prevention matters most: treat acne early; scarring is a referral trigger under NICE NG198.
    • Treatments (TCA CROSS, subcision, lasers, microneedling) are specialist or cosmetic; aim for improvement, not removal.

    Overview

    Note

    Scope: counselling and recognition

    For the pharmacist/IP, this page covers recognising scar types, setting expectations, safety and referral. It is not a procedure how-to.

    Acne scarring is fibrous remodelling after inflammatory acne. Atrophic scars follow collagen destruction; raised scars are covered on Keloids and hypertrophic scars. Scars are usually permanent but may soften with time or combined treatment. The psychological impact is often high, even when scarring looks ‘mild’.

    Epidemiology

    • Who
      • Some scarring affects many people with moderate to severe acne; risk rises with delayed treatment and picking.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    • Inflammatory nodules and cysts cause net collagen destruction, giving atrophic scars.
    • Risk rises with acne severity and duration, delay in treatment, picking (acne excoriée) and smoking.
    • Post-inflammatory erythema and hyper- or hypopigmentation are colour change, not true scarring, but patients often call both ‘scars’.

    Myths to address

    • "Scars will fade completely": atrophic scars are permanent; colour marks fade.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    Deep inflammation destroys collagen faster than it can be rebuilt.

    1. 1

      Deep inflammation

      Ruptured follicles release contents into the dermis, causing intense inflammation.

    2. 2

      Collagen destruction

      Enzymes (MMPs) break down dermal collagen.

    3. 3

      Imperfect repair

      Healing lays down too little collagen, leaving a depression.

    4. 4

      Tethering

      Fibrous bands pull the surface down in rolling scars (released by subcision).

    Clinical features

    TypeAppearanceTypical sites
    Ice-pickNarrow, deep, V-shaped; deeper than wide (about 60–70% of atrophic scars)Cheeks
    BoxcarRound or oval depressions with sharp vertical edgesCheeks, temples
    RollingWider, sloping edges, tethered by dermal bandsCheeks
    Perifollicular elastolysisSmall, pale, follicular atrophic scarsBack, chest
    PIH / PIE (not scar)Flat brown or red marks; no change in skin contourAnywhere acne occurred

    Special groups

    • Skin of colour
      • PIH is very common and often more distressing than scars; higher procedure risk.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    Clinical: history of acne plus scar morphology. Document Fitzpatrick skin type, whether acne is still active, and the patient’s goals.

    Differential diagnoses

    Look-alikeDistinguishing clues
    PIH or erythema aloneFlat colour change, with no depression
    Chickenpox scarsHistory of varicella; scattered
    Traumatic or surgical scarHistory of injury

    Management

    Prevent

    Treat active acne early and adequately (see Acne vulgaris). Scarring is a referral trigger under NICE NG198. Treat active acne first, before any scar procedures.

    Specialist modalities (overview)

    Scar typeOptions (specialist or cosmetic)
    Ice-pickTCA CROSS (chemical reconstruction of skin scars), punch excision or grafting
    BoxcarResurfacing (fractional lasers, peels), punch techniques, microneedling
    RollingSubcision, dermal fillers or fat, microneedling, fractional laser

    Combined, multi-session approaches are usual. Refer to dermatology, plastic surgery or a reputable aesthetic clinic, and explain that the aim is improvement, not removal.

    Treatment ladder

    1. 1

      Prevent

      Treat active acne early and adequately; scarring = refer (NG198).

    2. 2

      Specialist: atrophic

      Ice-pick: TCA CROSS, punch excision / grafting. Boxcar: fractional laser, peels, punch, microneedling.

    Caution

    Prescribing limits and pitfalls

    • Expectations: Improvement, not removal.
    • Isotretinoin: Check status before procedures.
    • Skin of colour: Higher PIH risk: experienced operator only.

    Referral

    • Routine

      Scarring or significant distress at any acne severity (NG198).

    • Routine

      Low mood or body dysmorphic concerns: mental-health support.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Scarring or significant distress at any acne severity: refer (NG198).
    • Low mood, anxiety or body dysmorphic concerns: signpost or refer for mental health support.

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Psychological distress, low self-esteem, depression.

    Prognosis

    • Permanent but improvable with combined treatment.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • Separate scar from pigment. The treatments are different.

    • Check isotretinoin status before procedures: see Cosmetic procedures.

    Self-check quiz

    4 questions · optional
    1. Q1Which atrophic scar type is commonest?Reveal

      Ice-pick (about 60–70%).

    2. Q2What treatment suits rolling scars?Reveal

      Subcision (± fillers, microneedling, laser).

    3. Q3How do you tell PIH from a scar?Reveal

      PIH is flat colour change with no contour change.

    4. Q4What does NICE say about scarring?Reveal

      Scarring is a referral trigger at any acne severity.
