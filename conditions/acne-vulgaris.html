    Acne vulgaris

    Comedones, papules, pustules and nodules of the pilosebaceous unit. Follow NICE NG198 combinations for 12 weeks, never use antibiotic monotherapy, and refer early for scarring.

    Step 1 of 9 · 1 min

    Overview

    Adolescents & young adultsPilosebaceous unitNICE NG19812-week courses

    • Spot it

      Comedones (the hallmark) with papules and pustules on face, chest and back.

    • Why

      Sebum + follicular plugging + C. acnes + inflammation.

    • Treat

      12-week fixed-combination topical; add an oral tetracycline if moderate–severe. Never antibiotic alone.

    • Never miss

      Acne fulminans (unwell, ulcerating): same-day dermatology. Scarring or distress: refer early.

    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    What it is

    Acne vulgaris is a chronic inflammatory disorder of the pilosebaceous unit. Most adolescents (around 85–95%) have it to some degree, and 20–35% develop moderate to severe disease. It can persist into, or start in, adult life, especially in women.

    Complications include scarring, pigment change and real psychological distress, which can be out of proportion to how the acne looks.

    Start: Why it happens
    2

    Step 2 of 9 · 1 min

    Why it happens

    Four factors acting in one hair follicle.

    • Sebum production rises (androgens).
    • Follicular plugging forms the comedone.
    • C. acnes colonises the plug.
    • Inflammation turns comedones into papules, pustules and nodules.
    • Aggravators: PCOS, comedogenic cosmetics, picking, steroids, lithium, EGFR inhibitors. Diet: NICE finds insufficient evidence.
    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    How it’s caused

    Four linked processes

    1. More sebum, driven by androgens.
    2. Follicular hyperkeratinisation, which plugs the follicle and forms a microcomedone.
    3. Cutibacterium acnes proliferation.
    4. Inflammation, producing papules, pustules and nodules.

    Aggravators

    • PCOS or other hyperandrogenism
    • Occlusive or comedogenic cosmetics, picking and humidity
    • Drugs: corticosteroids, lithium, EGFR inhibitors, anabolic steroids
    • Diet: links with high-glycaemic foods and dairy have been reported, but NICE finds insufficient evidence to recommend specific acne diets
    Next: What you’ll see
    3

    Step 3 of 9 · 3 min

    What you’ll see

    No comedones? Think again (rosacea, folliculitis).

    • Non-inflammatory: open (black) and closed (white) comedones.
    • Inflammatory: papules, pustules, nodules, pseudocysts.
    • Sites: face, neck, upper chest, back, shoulders.
    • Sequelae: post-inflammatory erythema or pigment; ice-pick, rolling, boxcar scars.

    Lesions to recognise

    • Open comedones

      Dilated follicles with dark keratin plugs.

      Nose, forehead

    • Closed comedones

      Small skin-coloured papules; use side lighting.

      Forehead

    • Papulopustular

      Inflamed papules and pustules with comedones.

      Cheeks, chin

    • Nodulocystic

      Deep nodules and cysts with scarring: refer.

      Face, trunk

    • Truncal

      Papules, pustules, nodules on back and chest.

      Back, shoulders

    • Pigment change

      Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, often the main worry.

      Skin of colour

    Clinical features

    • Sites: face, neck, upper chest, back and shoulders.
    • Non-inflammatory lesions: open comedones (blackheads) and closed comedones (whiteheads).
    • Inflammatory lesions: papules, pustules, nodules and pseudocysts.
    • Sequelae: post-inflammatory erythema or hyperpigmentation; ice-pick, rolling and boxcar scars.

    Severity (NICE NG198 continuum)

    CategoryDefinition
    Mild to moderateAny of: comedones; up to 34 inflammatory lesions; up to 2 nodules
    Moderate to severe35 or more inflammatory lesions, and/or 3 or more nodules

    Red flag

    Red-flag variants

    Acne fulminans (sudden ulcerating acne with fever and arthralgia), acne conglobata (interconnecting nodules and sinuses) and nodulocystic disease.

    Next: Confirm the diagnosis

    Step 4 of 9 · 1 min

    Confirm the diagnosis

    History

    • Duration; past treatments, how long, adherence.
    • Contraception and pregnancy plans; drugs.
    • PCOS clues: irregular periods, hirsutism.
    • Psychosocial impact and scarring.

    Examine

    • Lesion types and counts; nodules.
    • Trunk, scarring, pigment change.

    Severity

    Mild–moderate

    Any of: comedones; ≤ 34 inflammatory lesions; ≤ 2 nodules

    Moderate–severe

    ≥ 35 inflammatory lesions and/or ≥ 3 nodules

    Tests

    • Usually none.
    • Hormone profile if atypical or hyperandrogenism.
    • Swab if unusual pustular infection.

    Diagnosis

    Diagnosis is clinical. Comedones help separate acne from rosacea and folliculitis.

    • History: duration; previous treatments, including how long they were used and adherence; contraception and pregnancy plans; PCOS features (irregular periods, hirsutism); drugs; psychosocial impact; scarring.
    • Tests are usually unnecessary. Consider a hormone profile if the picture is atypical or suggests hyperandrogenism, and a swab if an unusual pustular infection is suspected.

    Safety limits

    Never

    Topical or oral antibiotic alone, or both antibiotics together.

    Review at 12 weeks

    Stop the oral antibiotic once clear; keep the topical.

    Max duration

    Antibiotic regimens beyond 6 months only exceptionally, with 3-monthly review.

    By site & situation

    Maintenance

    Adapalene + BPO, or adapalene, azelaic acid or BPO alone.

    Alternative

    BPO alone if avoiding a retinoid or antibiotic.

    Isotretinoin safety

    No tetracyclines (intracranial hypertension), no vitamin A; POP not enough for the PPP; 30-day supply, dispense within 7 days.

    More detailfull notes · 3 min

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    Aligned with NICE NG198 and CKS.

    Self-care and OTC

    • Wash twice daily with a syndet (non-alkaline) cleanser. Avoid harsh scrubbing and over-cleansing.
    • Use non-comedogenic moisturisers, sunscreen and make-up, and remove make-up at night.
    • Do not pick or squeeze, as this causes scarring.
    • OTC benzoyl peroxide (BPO), or adapalene where available, is useful while waiting for a prescription.
    • Set expectations: improvement takes 6–8 weeks.

    First-line 12-week options (offer one, after shared decision)

    SeverityOptions
    Any severityFixed adapalene + BPO once daily in the evening, or fixed tretinoin + clindamycin once daily in the evening
    Mild to moderate (also)Fixed BPO + clindamycin once daily
    Moderate to severeAdapalene + BPO plus oral lymecycline 408 mg or doxycycline 100 mg daily, or topical azelaic acid 15–20% twice daily plus lymecycline or doxycycline

    Alternatives: BPO alone if the patient wants to avoid a retinoid or antibiotic. If tetracyclines are unsuitable, consider trimethoprim or an oral macrolide in the combinations set out in NG198.

    UK guidance

    Antimicrobial stewardship

    • Never use a topical antibiotic alone, an oral antibiotic alone, or topical and oral antibiotics together. Always partner an antibiotic with a non-antibiotic topical.
    • Review at 12 weeks. Stop the oral antibiotic once clear and continue the topical.
    • If improved but not clear, the oral antibiotic can continue for up to another 12 weeks. Use antibiotic-containing regimens beyond 6 months only in exceptional cases, with 3-monthly review.

    Maintenance

    After clearance, consider adapalene + BPO, or adapalene, azelaic acid or BPO alone.

    Isotretinoin

    Isotretinoin is initiated by a specialist or GPwER under MHRA risk-minimisation measures: mental health and sexual function counselling, and the pregnancy prevention programme. It is usually considered after adequate courses of topical and systemic antibiotic treatment, where indicated.

    Treatment-resistant acne and the isotretinoin pathway (Module 3 Week 1)

    • Assessment of the 18-year-old woman: Three adequate regimens have failed (BPO/clindamycin → topical retinoid + COCP → oral doxycycline + adapalene/BPO); She has nodulocystic disease with scarring risk; This is treatment-resistant moderate–severe acne, which meets NG198 criteria for referral to a consultant dermatologist-led team for oral isotretinoin
    • While waiting: Stop the oral antibiotic if it is past 6 months, or continue it only exceptionally with review; Keep a non-antibiotic topical (adapalene/BPO); Consider whether the COCP choice suits her (co-cyprindiol is an option in selected cases; check VTE risk); Same-day referral only for acne fulminans (systemic symptoms, ulcerating nodules)
    • Isotretinoin: what the IP must know (MHRA 2023 safety measures, Pregnancy Prevention Programme): It is initiated only by or under a consultant dermatologist-led team; Two independent prescribers must agree before starting in under-18s. For an 18-year-old this specific rule does not apply, but every other measure does; Pregnancy prevention programme for anyone who could become pregnant:; highly effective contraception (user-independent, e.g. LARC; or two complementary methods) from 1 month before, throughout, and 1 month after; a medically supervised pregnancy test before, monthly during, and 1 month after; 30-day prescription limit, dispensed within 7 days; an annual risk-acknowledgement form; patient card; Mental health: a structured assessment of psychiatric history and mood at baseline and every visit, with a warning to report low mood or suicidal thoughts. Involve the family where appropriate; Sexual function: warn about possible erectile or libido dysfunction (including persistent cases); Monitoring: baseline and ~1 month LFT and fasting lipids. Watch for dry skin and lips, nosebleeds, myalgia and photosensitivity; Interactions: no tetracyclines (benign intracranial hypertension), no vitamin A supplements, caution with progestogen-only pills (not adequate alone for the PPP); Pharmacy role: check the PPP before every supply and do not dispense if the pregnancy test is outdated

    Red flags & referral

    • Same day

      Acne fulminans: on-call dermatology.

    • Urgent

      Low mood or suicidal thoughts: assess and escalate, especially on isotretinoin.

    • Routine

      Acne conglobata, nodulocystic acne, or diagnostic doubt.

    • Routine

      Mild–moderate failing two courses; moderate–severe failing a regimen with an oral antibiotic.

    • Routine

      Scarring, persistent pigment change or significant distress, at any severity.

    Red flags & referral

    • Acne fulminans: refer the same day to on-call dermatology.
    • Refer to dermatology or a GPwER for: diagnostic uncertainty; acne conglobata; nodulocystic acne; mild to moderate acne that has failed two completed courses; moderate to severe acne that has failed a regimen including an oral antibiotic.
    • Refer for: scarring, persistent pigment change, or significant psychological distress, at any severity.
    • Low mood or suicidal thoughts: assess and escalate, especially in the context of isotretinoin.
    Next: Prescriber pearls
    8

    Step 8 of 9 · 1 min

    Prescriber pearls

    • Start topicals on alternate days or short-contact, then build up.

    • BPO bleaches towels, pillowcases and hair.

    • Retinoids and tetracyclines cause photosensitivity.

    • Pregnancy: no topical retinoids or tetracyclines. BPO ± clindamycin with caution.

    • Combined pill preferred over POP; co-cyprindiol in selected women (VTE risk, review ~6 months).

    • Depression is common with acne itself: screen regardless of drug.

    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    Prescriber pearls

    • Start topicals on alternate days or as short-contact applications to reduce irritation, then build up.
    • BPO bleaches fabric and hair, so warn about towels and pillowcases.
    • Retinoids and tetracyclines cause photosensitivity, so give sun advice.
    • Pregnancy: topical retinoids and oral tetracyclines are contraindicated, so counsel on contraception. BPO with or without clindamycin can be used cautiously.
    • If hormonal contraception is wanted, a combined pill is preferred over a progestogen-only pill.
    • Co-cyprindiol is an option in selected women (e.g. with PCOS). Discuss VTE risk and review at about 6 months.
    • When referring for isotretinoin, document mental health history thoroughly.
    • In skin of colour, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation can bother patients more than the acne itself. Treat early, and consider azelaic acid.
    • Grading: NG198 uses a simple mild–moderate vs moderate–severe division (based on lesion types and counts plus nodules/cysts and scarring) to choose the regimen. Specialist tools (Leeds, IGA) are for documentation.
    • Combination therapy rules: always pair an oral antibiotic with BPO and/or a topical retinoid. Never use an oral or topical antibiotic alone, or oral + topical antibiotic together. Review at 12 weeks.
    • Maintenance after clearance: a topical retinoid ± BPO.
    • Safety-net: depression is common with acne itself, so screen regardless of the drug.
    • NICE NG198 combinations and the combined pill: acne prescribing and the combined pill; topical and oral retinoid safety: retinoids.

    4. Q4Which acne presentation needs same-day referral?Reveal

    5. Q5Why are tetracyclines and isotretinoin never combined?Reveal
