    Acne vulgaris

    Comedones, papules, pustules and nodules of the pilosebaceous unit. Follow NICE NG198 combinations for 12 weeks, never use antibiotic monotherapy, and refer early for scarring.

    Also calledacnespotscomedonesblackheadswhiteheadsnodulocystic acne+3

    Key facts

    • Chronic inflammatory disease of the pilosebaceous unit: comedones, papules, pustules, nodules.
    • Affects 85–95% of adolescents; 20–35% moderate–severe; often persists into adulthood, especially in women.
    • Four drivers: sebum, follicular plugging, C. acnes, inflammation.
    • UK care follows NICE NG198: 12-week fixed-combination topicals ± an oral tetracycline; never antibiotic alone.
    • Refer acne fulminans the same day; refer scarring or distress at any severity.

    Overview

    Acne vulgaris is the common form of acne: a chronic inflammatory disorder of the pilosebaceous unit (the hair follicle and its sebaceous gland). It produces a mixed eruption of non-inflammatory lesions (comedones) and inflammatory lesions (papules, pustules, nodules).

    • It favours areas with the most sebaceous follicles: face, neck, upper chest, shoulders and back. About half of patients have truncal acne as well as facial acne.
    • Diagnosis is clinical. Comedones are the hallmark and help separate acne from rosacea and folliculitis.
    • It matters beyond the skin: scarring, persistent pigment change and real psychological distress, often out of proportion to how the acne looks.
    • Severe variants (acne conglobata, acne fulminans) are uncommon but need specialist care.

    Epidemiology

    • Prevalence
      • Most adolescents (about 85–95%) have acne to some degree; 20–35% develop moderate to severe disease.
      • It is one of the most common skin conditions worldwide.
    • Age
      • Usually starts around puberty and peaks in the mid to late teens.
      • It can persist into, or begin in, adult life: roughly 50% of women in their 20s and 30–40% in their 30s have some acne.
      • About a quarter of adult women with acne had none as teenagers (adult-onset acne).
    • Sex
      • More common and often more severe in males during adolescence.
      • More common in females in adulthood (hormonal and PCOS-related acne).
    • Ethnicity
      • Affects all ethnic groups. In skin of colour, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation and keloid scarring are more frequent and are often the main concern.
    • Family history
      • Strongly heritable: twin studies attribute about 80% of the variation in acne risk to genetics.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    Acne results from a genetic predisposition acted on by androgens and local factors. No single cause is found in most patients.

    Causes and associated factors

    • Androgens: drive sebum production and follicular plugging.
      • Puberty; polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS); congenital adrenal hyperplasia and other hyperandrogenic states.
      • Acne often changes in pregnancy.
    • Drugs
      • Corticosteroids (systemic and topical), testosterone and anabolic steroids, lithium, EGFR inhibitors, JAK inhibitors, phenytoin, halogens (iodides, bromides).
    • Occlusion and friction (acne mechanica)
      • Headbands, helmets, chin straps, backpacks, sports pads, face masks.
    • Cosmetics and hair products
      • Occlusive or comedogenic creams and make-up; pomade acne along the hairline from greasy hair products.
    • Picking and squeezing, humid environments and heavy sweating.

    Myths to address

    • Diet: links with high-glycaemic foods and dairy (and whey protein supplements) have been reported, but NICE finds insufficient evidence to recommend any specific acne diet.
      • A balanced diet is reasonable; strict exclusion diets are not.
    • Dirt or poor hygiene does not cause acne.
      • The dark colour of a blackhead is oxidised keratin and melanin, not dirt. Over-washing and scrubbing make irritation worse.
    • Acne is not infectious and cannot be caught.
    • Sunlight does not cure acne: any improvement is temporary, and retinoids and tetracyclines increase sun sensitivity.

    Note

    Drug-induced acneiform eruptions

    A sudden, monomorphic papulopustular eruption without comedones after starting a drug (steroids, EGFR inhibitors) is an acneiform drug eruption rather than acne vulgaris.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    Four linked processes act in a single follicle. They explain each lesion type and which drug targets which step.

    1. 1

      Androgens drive excess sebum

      At puberty, androgens (testosterone, DHEAS) enlarge the sebaceous glands and increase sebum output. IGF-1 also stimulates the glands, which may explain the reported link with high-glycaemic diets.

    2. 2

      Follicular hyperkeratinisation forms a microcomedone

      Keratinocytes lining the upper follicle multiply and fail to shed. Sticky keratin and sebum plug the follicle, forming a microcomedone, the precursor of every acne lesion. A closed plug is a whitehead; a plug open to the surface is a blackhead.

    3. 3

      Cutibacterium acnes proliferates

      The plugged, lipid-rich, low-oxygen follicle lets the commensal C. acnes multiply. It activates Toll-like receptor 2 on immune cells, releasing IL-1β, IL-8, IL-12 and TNF-α; Th17 cells and IL-17 recruit neutrophils.

    4. 4

      Inflammation and rupture

      Inflammation turns comedones into papules and pustules. If the follicle wall ruptures, keratin, sebum and bacteria spill into the dermis, causing a deeper nodule and, when healing is disordered, scarring or pigment change.

    How treatments map onto the mechanism

    • Retinoids (adapalene, tretinoin; isotretinoin): normalise keratinisation and unplug follicles (step 2). Isotretinoin also shrinks sebaceous glands (step 1).
    • Benzoyl peroxide: kills C. acnes without resistance and is mildly comedolytic (steps 2–3).
    • Antibiotics (topical clindamycin, oral tetracyclines): reduce C. acnes and are anti-inflammatory (steps 3–4).
    • Azelaic acid: antibacterial, comedolytic and reduces pigmentation.
    • Combined pill and antiandrogens: reduce androgen-driven sebum (step 1).

    Histopathology

    • Microcomedone / comedone: dilated follicular infundibulum filled with compact keratin and sebum; a narrow (closed) or wide (open) opening.
    • Inflammatory papule / pustule: perifollicular lymphocytes, then neutrophils; follicular wall thinning.
    • Ruptured follicle / nodule: dermal abscess with a foreign-body granulomatous reaction to keratin and hair.
    • Scar: dermal fibrosis (hypertrophic or keloid) or loss of dermal tissue (atrophic ice-pick, rolling, boxcar).
    • Biopsy is rarely needed; histology is mainly used when another diagnosis is suspected.

    Clinical features

    Symptoms

    • Often few physical symptoms; inflamed papules and nodules can be tender or painful.
    • Oily skin (seborrhoea) is common.
    • Ask about the psychological impact: embarrassment, social withdrawal, low mood, bullying.
    • Systemic symptoms (fever, joint pain) are not part of ordinary acne: think acne fulminans.

    Lesions and morphology

    Several lesion types usually occur together. Individual lesions last under 2 weeks; deep nodules can persist for months.

    • Open comedones (blackheads)

      Dilated follicles with a dark keratin plug at the surface. Non-inflammatory.

    • Closed comedones (whiteheads)

      Small skin-coloured or white papules with no visible opening; easiest to see with side lighting.

    • Papules

      Small (< 5 mm), tender, red bumps arising from inflamed comedones.

    • Pustules

      Papules with a visible white or yellow centre of pus.

    • Nodules and pseudocysts

      Large, deep, painful red lumps (≥ 5 mm) or fluctuant cyst-like swellings. High risk of scarring: needs oral treatment or referral.

    • Secondary lesions

      Excoriations, red marks (post-inflammatory erythema), dark marks (post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation) and scars.

    Distribution

    • Face (most common): forehead, cheeks, nose, chin and jawline.
    • Trunk: upper chest, upper back and shoulders, in about half of patients.
    • Adult female acne favours the lower face and jawline.
    • Pomade acne follows the hairline; acne mechanica follows the area of friction.

    Variants and subtypes

    • Comedonal acne

      Mainly open and closed comedones with few inflamed lesions. Topical retinoid territory.

    • Papulopustular acne

      Inflamed papules and pustules with comedones; the commonest pattern.

    • Truncal acne

      Papules, pustules and nodules across the back and chest.

    • Nodulocystic acne

      Deep nodules and cysts with scarring. Refer for isotretinoin.

    • Acne conglobata

      Interconnecting nodules, abscesses and sinus tracts on the trunk, with severe scarring.

    • Acne fulminans

      Sudden painful ulcerating, haemorrhagic nodules with fever, malaise and joint pain, typically in young males; can follow starting isotretinoin. Same-day dermatology.

    • SAPHO-associated acne

      Synovitis, acne, pustulosis, hyperostosis, osteitis: severe acne with bone or joint pain.

    • Adult female acne

      Inflamed papules on the lower face and jawline; ask about the menstrual cycle and PCOS.

    • Neonatal and infantile acne

      A separate pattern in babies; see the dedicated page. Open page

    Special groups

    • Skin of colour
      • Redness is less visible; post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation is often the main reason to seek help and can last months.
      • Pomade acne is more common; keloid scars are more likely on the jawline, chest and upper back.
      • Treat inflammation early; azelaic acid helps pigmentation.
    • Pregnancy
      • Topical and oral retinoids and tetracyclines are contraindicated.
      • Benzoyl peroxide with or without clindamycin can be used cautiously.
    • Adult women and PCOS
      • Look for irregular periods, hirsutism, acanthosis nigricans and weight gain.
    • Children
      • Acne before age 8–9 is unusual: consider an endocrine cause and seek advice.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    Diagnosis is clinical, from the lesion mix and distribution. Comedones are the key sign.

    History

    • Duration and course; previous treatments, how long each was used and adherence.
    • Contraception and pregnancy plans; current drugs (steroids, lithium, anabolic steroids, EGFR inhibitors).
    • PCOS features: irregular periods, hirsutism, weight gain.
    • Cosmetics, hair products and occlusion; picking.
    • Psychosocial impact, mood and any self-harm thoughts; scarring.

    Examination

    • Lesion types and approximate counts; nodules; trunk involvement.
    • Scarring and pigment change.
    • Signs of hyperandrogenism (hirsutism, acanthosis nigricans, androgenic alopecia).

    Defining severity (NICE NG198)

    CategoryDefinition
    Mild to moderateAny of: comedones; up to 34 inflammatory lesions; up to 2 nodules
    Moderate to severe35 or more inflammatory lesions, and/or 3 or more nodules

    NG198 uses this two-level split to choose a regimen.

    • Other grading tools (Leeds, Investigator's Global Assessment, lesion counts) are mainly for specialist documentation and trials.
    • Record scarring, pigment change and psychological impact separately: they can justify referral at any severity.

    Investigations

    • Usually none.
    • Hormone profile (e.g. testosterone, SHBG, LH/FSH) if the picture is atypical or suggests hyperandrogenism or PCOS.
    • Skin swab if an unusual pustular infection is suspected (e.g. Gram-negative folliculitis after long antibiotic use).
    • Baseline bloods for isotretinoin are arranged by the specialist team.

    Management

    Principles

    • Follow NICE NG198: agree one 12-week course after shared decision-making (severity, preference, adverse effects, pregnancy plans).
    • Treat early to prevent scarring; set expectations that improvement takes 6–8 weeks and full effect up to 12.
    • Antimicrobial stewardship: never use an antibiotic alone; always pair it with BPO or a topical retinoid.
    • Review at 12 weeks; then step down to maintenance.
    • Ask about mood and offer support at every review.

    Defining severity

    Use the NG198 split above (mild to moderate vs moderate to severe) to choose a regimen. Scarring, pigment change or distress can justify escalation or referral at any severity.

    Conservative and self-care

    • Wash twice daily with a syndet (non-alkaline) cleanser; avoid harsh scrubbing and over-cleansing.
    • Use non-comedogenic moisturisers, sunscreen and make-up; remove make-up at night.
    • Do not pick or squeeze: it causes scarring.
    • OTC benzoyl peroxide (or adapalene where available) while waiting for a prescription.
    • No specific diet is recommended (NICE).

    Medical treatment: topical

    Adapalene + benzoyl peroxide

    Fixed-combination topical retinoid + antimicrobial
    Dose
    Once daily in the evening, 12 weeks.
    Use
    First line at any severity; also maintenance.
    Cautions
    Retinoid: avoid in pregnancy and when planning pregnancy. Irritation, dryness.
    Counselling
    Start on alternate nights or as short contact; pea-sized amount to the whole area, not spot-treating. BPO bleaches fabric and hair. Use sunscreen.

    Tretinoin + clindamycin

    Fixed-combination topical retinoid + antibiotic
    Dose
    Once daily in the evening, 12 weeks.
    Use
    First line at any severity.
    Cautions
    Retinoid: avoid in pregnancy. Contains an antibiotic: do not combine with an oral antibiotic.
    Counselling
    Irritation and photosensitivity: start gradually and use sunscreen.

    Benzoyl peroxide + clindamycin

    Fixed-combination antimicrobial + antibiotic
    Dose
    Once daily, 12 weeks.
    Use
    Mild to moderate acne.
    Cautions
    Contains an antibiotic: not with oral antibiotics; can be used cautiously in pregnancy.
    Counselling
    Bleaches fabric; dryness and irritation.

    Benzoyl peroxide alone

    Antimicrobial, comedolytic
    Use
    If the patient wants to avoid a retinoid or antibiotic; OTC while awaiting review; maintenance.
    Cautions
    Irritation; bleaching of towels, pillowcases and hair.
    Counselling
    No bacterial resistance develops, so it is the preferred antibiotic partner.

    Azelaic acid 15–20%

    Antibacterial, comedolytic, depigmenting
    Dose
    Twice daily.
    Use
    With an oral tetracycline for moderate to severe acne; alone for maintenance; helpful for post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation.
    Counselling
    Mild stinging at first.

    Medical treatment: systemic

    Lymecycline 408 mg

    Oral tetracycline
    Dose
    Once daily; review at 12 weeks.
    Use
    Moderate to severe acne, always with adapalene + BPO or azelaic acid.
    Cautions
    Contraindicated in pregnancy, breastfeeding and under 12s. Never with a topical antibiotic.
    Counselling
    Separate from antacids, iron, zinc and calcium. Photosensitivity: use sunscreen.

    Doxycycline 100 mg

    Oral tetracycline
    Dose
    Once daily; review at 12 weeks.
    Use
    As lymecycline.
    Cautions
    Contraindicated in pregnancy and under 12s. Photosensitivity is more marked.
    Counselling
    Swallow with plenty of water, sitting or standing upright (oesophageal irritation). Separate from antacids, iron and calcium.

    Trimethoprim or an oral macrolide

    Alternative oral antibiotics
    Use
    Only when tetracyclines are unsuitable, in the combinations set out in NG198.
    Cautions
    Stewardship: same rules as tetracyclines.

    Oral isotretinoin

    Systemic retinoidSpecialist only
    Use
    Severe, scarring or treatment-resistant acne. Initiated only by or under a consultant dermatologist-led team; two independent prescribers must agree in under-18s.
    Cautions
    • Highly teratogenic: Pregnancy Prevention Programme for anyone who could become pregnant (highly effective contraception from 1 month before to 1 month after; supervised pregnancy test before, monthly during and 1 month after; 30-day prescription limit, dispensed within 7 days).
    • No tetracyclines (benign intracranial hypertension); no vitamin A supplements; a progestogen-only pill alone is not adequate contraception for the PPP.
    • Acne may flare in the first weeks; a severe flare can be isotretinoin-triggered acne fulminans.
    Counselling
    • Mental health: report low mood or suicidal thoughts; structured assessment at baseline and every visit.
    • Sexual function: possible erectile or libido problems, rarely persistent.
    • Dry lips and skin (lip balm), nosebleeds, muscle aches, photosensitivity.
    Monitoring
    LFTs and fasting lipids at baseline and about 1 month. Pharmacy checks the PPP before every supply.

    Hormonal treatment and antiandrogens

    • If hormonal contraception is wanted, a combined oral contraceptive can be added to topical treatment; it is preferred to a progestogen-only pill, which can worsen acne.
    • Co-cyprindiol (cyproterone acetate with ethinylestradiol) is an option in selected women (e.g. with PCOS) when other treatments fail. Discuss VTE risk and review at about 6 months.
    • Spironolactone is not among the NG198 options; dermatologists sometimes use it off-label for adult women.
    • Prescribing detail: acne prescribing and the combined pill.

    Treatment ladder

    1. 1

      Everyone

      Skin care, non-comedogenic products, no picking; OTC benzoyl peroxide.

    2. 2

      Mild to moderate

      12 weeks of adapalene + BPO, tretinoin + clindamycin, or BPO + clindamycin.

    3. 3

      Moderate to severe

      12 weeks of adapalene + BPO (or azelaic acid) plus lymecycline 408 mg or doxycycline 100 mg daily.

    4. 4

      Maintenance

      Once clear: adapalene + BPO, or adapalene, azelaic acid or BPO alone. Stop the oral antibiotic.

    5. 5

      Specialist

      Isotretinoin, under MHRA measures and the Pregnancy Prevention Programme.

    UK guidance

    Antimicrobial stewardship

    • Never use a topical antibiotic alone, an oral antibiotic alone, or topical and oral antibiotics together.
    • Review at 12 weeks. Stop the oral antibiotic once clear and continue the topical.
    • If improved but not clear, the oral antibiotic can continue for up to another 12 weeks. Use antibiotic regimens beyond 6 months only in exceptional cases, with 3-monthly review.

    Psychological impact

    • Acne is linked with depression, anxiety, low self-esteem and, in adolescents, increased suicidal ideation, regardless of treatment.
    • Ask about mood at every visit, not only when isotretinoin is involved.
    • Significant distress or a mental health disorder is a reason to refer at any severity.
    • Signpost support (e.g. the British Skin Foundation, Changing Faces) and involve the GP or mental health services when needed.

    Referral (NICE NG198)

    • Same day

      Acne fulminans: refer the same day to on-call dermatology.

    • Urgent

      Low mood, self-harm or suicidal thoughts: assess and escalate (mental health), especially around isotretinoin.

    • Routine

      Diagnostic uncertainty; acne conglobata; nodulocystic acne.

    • Routine

      Mild to moderate acne that has failed two completed courses; moderate to severe acne that has failed a regimen including an oral antibiotic.

    • Routine

      Scarring, persistent pigment change, or psychological distress at any severity.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Same day

      Sudden ulcerating, haemorrhagic nodules with fever and joint pain (acne fulminans), including after starting isotretinoin.

    • Urgent

      Suicidal thoughts or severe low mood.

    • Routine

      Scarring or keloids starting at any severity; acne before age 8–9 or with virilisation (endocrine cause).

    • Routine

      Sudden monomorphic eruption after a new drug: review the drug.

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Scarring
      • Atrophic: ice-pick, rolling and boxcar scars.
      • Hypertrophic and keloid scars, especially on the jawline, chest and shoulders in skin of colour.
    • Post-inflammatory erythema (red marks, fair skin) and hyperpigmentation (dark marks, skin of colour).
    • Psychological: depression, anxiety, social withdrawal.
    • Treatment-related: antibiotic resistance, irritant dermatitis, isotretinoin adverse effects.

    Scar treatment (and the timing of procedures around isotretinoin) is covered on acne scars and keloids.

    Prognosis

    • Most adolescent acne improves by the early to mid-20s, but it can persist or start in adulthood.
    • Relapse after stopping treatment is common: maintenance treatment reduces it.
    • Early effective treatment is the best way to prevent permanent scarring.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • Start topicals on alternate days or as short-contact applications, then build up.

    • Apply topicals to the whole affected area, not just individual spots.

    • BPO bleaches fabric and hair: warn about towels and pillowcases.

    • Retinoids and tetracyclines cause photosensitivity: give sun advice.

    • Pregnancy: no topical or oral retinoids, no tetracyclines; BPO ± clindamycin can be used cautiously.

    • If hormonal contraception is wanted, a combined pill is preferred over a progestogen-only pill.

    • Combination rules: always pair an oral antibiotic with BPO and/or a topical retinoid; review at 12 weeks.

    • When referring for isotretinoin, document mental health history thoroughly.

    • In skin of colour, treat early and consider azelaic acid for pigmentation.

    • Depression is common with acne itself: screen regardless of the drug.

    • Related: retinoids, acne mimics, acne prescribing and the combined pill.

    Self-check quiz

    8 questions · optional
    1. Q1What is the hallmark lesion that separates acne from rosacea?Reveal

      Comedones (blackheads and whiteheads).

    2. Q2Name the four pathogenic factors in acne.Reveal

      Androgen-driven sebum, follicular hyperkeratinisation, C. acnes proliferation and inflammation.

    3. Q3How does NICE NG198 define moderate to severe acne?Reveal

      35 or more inflammatory lesions and/or 3 or more nodules.

    4. Q4Which first-line option suits any severity?Reveal

      Fixed adapalene + BPO, or fixed tretinoin + clindamycin, once daily in the evening for 12 weeks.

    5. Q5Name three antibiotic combinations to avoid.Reveal

      Topical antibiotic alone; oral antibiotic alone; topical and oral antibiotics together.

    6. Q6Why must tetracyclines not be given with isotretinoin?Reveal

      Risk of benign intracranial hypertension.

    7. Q7Which acne patient needs same-day referral?Reveal

      Acne fulminans: systemically unwell with ulcerating nodules.

    8. Q8Is a specific acne diet recommended?Reveal

      No: NICE finds insufficient evidence for any specific diet.
