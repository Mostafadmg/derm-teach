    Actinic keratoses (solar keratoses)

    Rough, scaly UV-induced dysplastic patches on sun-damaged skin: a marker of field cancerisation with a small risk of SCC. Sun protection, then lesion- or field-directed treatment, and when to refer.

    Also calledactinic keratosissolar keratosisAKactinic cheilitisfield changefield cancerisation+2
    Related pagesCutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC)Seborrhoeic keratosisPhotoageingSolar lentigines

    Key facts

    • Actinic (solar) keratoses are UV-induced patches of dysplastic keratinocytes on chronically sun-exposed skin.
    • Very common: up to 25% of people over 60 in northern England and Ireland; more in fair skin, outdoor workers and the immunosuppressed.
    • Rough, gritty scale on a pink base, often easier to feel than see; face, bald scalp, ears, forearms and backs of hands.
    • A single AK rarely becomes SCC, but people with many AKs have a 10–15% lifetime SCC risk (field cancerisation).
    • Sun protection for all; cryotherapy for few lesions; field treatment (5-FU, imiquimod, diclofenac 3%, PDT). Tender, thick or growing → exclude SCC.

    Overview

    Actinic keratoses (AKs) are common UV-induced dysplastic lesions on chronically sun-exposed skin of older adults. They mark field cancerisation and a small risk of progression to squamous cell carcinoma.

    Benign seborrhoeic keratoses are covered on their own page: seborrhoeic keratosis.

    Epidemiology

    • Who
      • Older adults with fair skin (Fitzpatrick I–II), chronic sun exposure and photoageing.
      • Organ transplant recipients and other immunosuppressed people have many more, and more aggressive, lesions.
    • Natural history
      • Individual AKs can regress (up to a quarter a year), persist or, rarely, progress to SCC.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    • AK: cumulative UVB/UVA; fair skin, outdoor work, immunosuppression (transplant) raise burden and SCC risk.
    • Actinic cheilitis is AK of the lip vermilion.

    Risk factors and associations

    • Cumulative UVB (and UVA) exposure: outdoor work, sunbeds, living in sunny climates.
    • Immunosuppression (transplant drugs), older age, previous skin cancer; HPV types may contribute.

    Myths to address

    • "It's just dry skin": a persistent gritty patch on sun-damaged skin is an AK until shown otherwise.
    • "Treat each spot": once cancer is excluded, treating the whole field matters more.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    UV damage to keratinocyte DNA creates a field of genetically abnormal cells; the AK is the visible tip.

    1. 1

      UV DNA damage

      UVB forms pyrimidine dimers; mutations in TP53 let damaged keratinocytes survive and divide.

    2. 2

      Clonal expansion

      Mutated clones spread along the basal layer, producing dysplasia of the lower epidermis.

    3. 3

      Abnormal keratinisation

      Dysplastic cells make abnormal keratin, giving adherent, rough parakeratotic scale.

    4. 4

      Field cancerisation

      Surrounding sun-damaged skin carries similar mutations, so new AKs and SCCs arise across the field.

    5. 5

      Progression

      If dysplasia becomes full-thickness (Bowen's disease) or invades the dermis, it has become SCC.

    Histopathology

    • Atypical keratinocytes in the basal layer (partial-thickness dysplasia), with parakeratosis that spares the follicular openings (alternating pink and blue).
    • Solar elastosis in the dermis.
    • Biopsy if thick, tender, ulcerated or not responding (to exclude SCC).

    Clinical features

    • AK: rough sandpaper scale on erythematous base; face, bald scalp, ears, forearms, hands; often multiple.
    • Hypertrophic AK and cutaneous horn need careful SCC exclusion.

    Special groups

    • Immunosuppressed (transplant)
      • Many AKs and high SCC risk: lower threshold for specialist referral and regular skin checks.
    • Lips (actinic cheilitis)
      • Blurred, scaly vermilion border of the lower lip; higher risk of SCC: lower threshold for specialist review.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    Clinical ± dermoscopy. Biopsy or 2WW/urgent skin cancer referral if SCC or melanoma cannot be excluded. Never destroy an undiagnosed pigmented lesion with cryotherapy or laser.

    Clinical

    • Fingertip + dermoscopy.

    Doubt

    • Biopsy or suspected cancer referral if SCC or melanoma can’t be excluded.

    Never

    • Destroy an undiagnosed pigmented lesion with cryo or laser.

    Management

    Everyone with AKs

    • Daily broad-spectrum SPF 30–50, hat, behaviour change; skin self-check education.
    • Emollient to reduce scale.

    Lesion- and field-directed options (local formulary / CKS themes)

    • Few discrete AKs: cryotherapy (light freeze) in trained hands.
    • Field change: topical 5-fluorouracil, imiquimod, diclofenac 3% gel, or photodynamic therapy via dermatology — expect an inflammatory treatment phase; counsel clearly.
    • Actinic cheilitis: lower threshold for specialist review.
    • Immunosuppressed: lower threshold for dermatology.

    Treatment ladder

    1. 1

      Everyone

      Daily broad-spectrum SPF 30–50, hat, behaviour change; self-check education. Emollient to reduce scale.

    2. 2

      Few discrete AKs

      Cryotherapy (light freeze) in trained hands.

    3. 3

      Field change

      Topical 5-fluorouracil, imiquimod, diclofenac 3% gel, or PDT via dermatology. Counsel: an inflammatory phase is expected.

    4. 4

      Lower threshold for dermatology

      Actinic cheilitis; immunosuppressed patients.

    5. 5

      SKs

      Reassure when typical; cryo / curettage only if symptomatic or doubtful (often not NHS-funded if cosmetic).

    Referral

    • Urgent

      Possible SCC or melanoma: suspected cancer referral (NG12).

    • Urgent

      Rapidly enlarging tender nodule in an AK field.

    • Urgent

      Lip AK with induration or ulceration.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Possible SCC or melanoma → urgent suspected-cancer referral (NG12).
    • Rapidly enlarging tender nodule in an AK field.
    • Lip AK with induration or ulceration.

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Progression to cutaneous SCC.
    • Cutaneous horn (may hide SCC).
    • Inflammatory reactions to field treatments; cosmetic concern.

    Prognosis

    • Chronic: new lesions keep appearing in sun-damaged skin.
    • Treatment clears most lesions; ongoing sun protection and surveillance are needed.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • Feel with a fingertip — AKs are gritty.

    • Treating the field matters more than chasing every spot once cancer is excluded.

    • Warn that 5-FU/imiquimod look worse before they look better.

    • Tender, thickened or growing AK, or an AK on the lip or ear: see cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma for when it may have become SCC.

    Self-check quiz

    5 questions · optional
    1. Q1Why treat a field of AKs rather than single spots?Reveal

      The whole sun-damaged area carries mutated cells (field cancerisation); treating it reduces new AKs and SCCs.

    2. Q2Which AK features suggest SCC?Reveal

      Tenderness, thickening, induration, rapid growth, ulceration or bleeding.

    3. Q3Name three field treatments.Reveal

      Topical 5-fluorouracil, imiquimod and diclofenac 3% gel; photodynamic therapy via dermatology.

    4. Q4What should patients expect from 5-FU or imiquimod?Reveal

      An inflammatory phase: redness, crusting and soreness before healing.

    5. Q5What is actinic cheilitis?Reveal

      Actinic keratosis of the lip vermilion; higher risk of SCC.
