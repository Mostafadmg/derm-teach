    Addison’s disease: skin signs

    Generalised bronzing, darker palmar creases, scars and gums, with tiredness, weight loss, salt craving and low blood pressure. Spotting it early can prevent a fatal adrenal crisis.

    Also calledprimary adrenal insufficiencyhyperpigmentationbuccal pigmentationpalmar crease pigmentationadrenal crisis
    Related pagesCushing’s syndrome: skin signsDrug-induced pigmentation (amiodarone, minocycline)Melasma & facial melanosis

    Key facts

    • Primary adrenal insufficiency: failure of the adrenal cortex, with low cortisol and aldosterone. About 100–140 per million in the UK.
    • 70–90% is autoimmune in the UK; other causes include TB, metastases, haemorrhage and drugs.
    • Skin clue: hyperpigmentation of creases, scars, gums, knuckles and pressure areas; vitiligo may coexist.
    • Adrenal crisis (vomiting, hypotension, collapse) → 999 and hydrocortisone 100 mg IM/IV immediately (NICE NG243).
    • Every patient needs an NHS Steroid Emergency Card, injection kit and sick-day rules.

    Overview

    Primary adrenal insufficiency: failure of the adrenal cortex → cortisol and aldosterone deficiency (and adrenal androgens). About 100–140 per million in the UK.

    • In the UK ~70–90% is autoimmune (21-hydroxylase antibodies), often with other autoimmune diseases (thyroid, T1DM, vitiligo, coeliac, pernicious anaemia, premature ovarian insufficiency — autoimmune polyendocrine syndromes).
    • Other causes: TB, adrenal metastases/haemorrhage (anticoagulants, meningococcal — Waterhouse–Friderichsen), HIV/CMV, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, adrenoleukodystrophy (young men), and drugs (ketoconazole, etomidate, checkpoint inhibitors, mitotane).
    • Secondary/tertiary adrenal insufficiency (pituitary disease or suppression by exogenous glucocorticoids) is far commoner in practice.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    Myths to address

    • "Pigmentation happens in all adrenal insufficiency": only primary (high ACTH); secondary causes are pale.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    Pigmentation comes from excess ACTH, which also stimulates melanocytes.

    1. 1

      Adrenal failure

      Autoimmune destruction stops cortisol production.

    2. 2

      Loss of feedback

      Low cortisol removes negative feedback, so the pituitary makes more POMC.

    3. 3

      POMC cleavage

      POMC is cut into ACTH and α-MSH.

    4. 4

      Melanocyte stimulation

      ACTH and α-MSH stimulate MC1R on melanocytes, increasing melanin, most visibly in creases and mucosa.

    How it is caused — why the hyperpigmentation?

    Low cortisol removes negative feedback → the pituitary makes more pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC), which is cleaved into ACTH and α-MSH. Both bind the melanocortin-1 receptor (MC1R) on melanocytes → more eumelanin → hyperpigmentation. This occurs only in primary AI. In secondary AI, ACTH is low and patients look pale.

    Clinical features

    Clinical features & sites

    • Skin and mucosa: generalised bronzing, accentuated in sun-exposed skin, palmar creases, knuckles, friction/pressure sites (belt line, elbows, knees, bra strap), recent scars, nipples, axillae, perineum; buccal mucosa, gums, tongue and lips (blue-black patches); longitudinal nail bands; darkening of hair and naevi; vitiligo (~10–20%, autoimmune); loss of axillary/pubic hair in women (adrenal androgen loss)
    • NICE NG243: hyperpigmentation may not be visible on black or brown skin — ask whether the patient has noticed a change and check the buccal mucosa and scars.
    • Systemic: fatigue, weakness, weight loss, anorexia, nausea/vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhoea, salt craving, postural dizziness/hypotension, myalgia/arthralgia, low mood; hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia, hypoglycaemia (especially in children), raised urea, eosinophilia, mild anaemia, raised TSH.
    • Adrenal crisis: vomiting, abdominal pain, confusion, hypotension/shock — often triggered by intercurrent illness; can be fatal (the press cases).

    Diagnosis and investigations

    • NICE NG243 (2024): offer an 8 am–9 am serum cortisol to people with suspected AI (course teaching: 8 am cortisol). Interpretation for adults (modern immunoassays):
    8–9 am serum cortisolMeaning (adults ≥16)Action
    <150 nmol/LMay have adrenal insufficiencyRefer to endocrinology; consider starting treatment; if acutely unwell → treat as adrenal crisis
    150–300 nmol/LUncertainConsider repeating; if it stays in this range, seek endocrinology advice/referral
    >300 nmol/LAI very unlikely—
    • Measure U&E (Na⁺, K⁺), glucose, FBC, and ACTH with the cortisol sample if possible (local lab rules — EDTA, on ice). Check TFT and renin/aldosterone.
    • Don't test people on oral glucocorticoids at physiological doses or above. Inhaled, injected or topical steroids can lower the 9 am cortisol. Wait 4 weeks after IM/intra-articular steroid injections.
    • Confirmation (secondary care): short Synacthen (ACTH stimulation) test, ACTH, renin, 21-hydroxylase antibodies, adrenal imaging if antibody-negative, very-long-chain fatty acids in young men.

    Differential diagnoses

    (hyperpigmentation)

    Haemochromatosis (bronze diabetes), drugs (minocycline, amiodarone, antimalarials, chemotherapy — busulfan, bleomycin; zidovudine), ectopic ACTH/Nelson syndrome, PCT, malabsorption/B12 or folate deficiency, pregnancy/melasma, chronic kidney disease, primary biliary cholangitis, acanthosis nigricans, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, constitutional/racial pigmentation, arsenic, hyperthyroidism, POEMS.

    Management

    • Suspected adrenal crisis (vomiting, hypotension, collapse, confusion) → 999. Give hydrocortisone 100 mg IM/IV immediately — do not wait for tests (NG243; adults; children by age). Then IV fluids and hydrocortisone 200 mg/24 h infusion or 50 mg IM/IV 6-hourly in hospital.
    • Maintenance (specialist-initiated): hydrocortisone ~15–25 mg/day in 2–3 divided doses (largest dose on waking), or prednisolone in selected patients; fludrocortisone 50–300 micrograms daily for mineralocorticoid replacement; ± DHEA in women (specialist)
    • Pharmacist/IP core tasks (NG243 + NatPSA 2020): Ensure the patient has an NHS Steroid Emergency Card, medical alert ID and a hydrocortisone emergency injection kit (100 mg IM + needles/syringes + instructions), and that patient and carers are trained to use it; Sick-day rules: during significant illness (fever, infection needing antibiotics, injury), take at least 40 mg oral hydrocortisone daily in 2–4 divided doses (or ≥10 mg prednisolone) until recovered. If vomiting within 30 minutes of a dose, repeat at double the dose. If vomiting recurs → IM hydrocortisone and go to ED; Interactions: enzyme inducers (rifampicin, carbamazepine, phenytoin, St John's wort) increase hydrocortisone requirement; CYP3A4 inhibitors increase exposure; Plan steroid cover for surgery/procedures (Woodcock 2020) and dental extractions; Recognise iatrogenic adrenal suppression in people on long-term glucocorticoids by any route: emergency card if oral prednisolone ≥5 mg/day (or equivalent) for ≥4 weeks, or high-dose inhaled/topical (NatPSA criteria); Annual review: BP (lying/standing), electrolytes, weight, over-/under-replacement signs; screen for other autoimmune diseases (TFT, B12, HbA1c, coeliac serology)

    IP scope

    Suspect, test (9 am cortisol, U&E), refer, and — vitally — steroid safety (cards, sick-day rules, emergency kits, interactions, never stopping steroids abruptly). Emergency IM hydrocortisone can be given by trained staff under emergency provisions.

    Referral

    • 999 / A&E

      Vomiting, abdominal pain, confusion or collapse with suspected adrenal insufficiency: adrenal crisis, hydrocortisone 100 mg IM and 999.

    • Same day

      Unexplained pigmentation with low sodium or high potassium: same-day 9am (or random) cortisol and endocrine advice.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Vomiting, abdominal pain, confusion or collapse with suspected adrenal insufficiency: adrenal crisis, hydrocortisone 100 mg IM and 999.
    • Unexplained pigmentation with low sodium or high potassium: same-day 9am (or random) cortisol and endocrine advice.

    Red flags / refer

    Unexplained hyperpigmentation + fatigue/weight loss/postural hypotension; hyponatraemia/hyperkalaemia; a 9 am cortisol <150 nmol/L; vomiting or collapse in a person on steroid replacement or long-term glucocorticoids (crisis — 999 + IM hydrocortisone).

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Adrenal crisis (fatal if untreated), hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia, hypoglycaemia.

    Prognosis

    • Normal life expectancy with replacement and crisis prevention.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • "Pigmented palmar creases and gums + salt craving + postural dizziness = check a 9 am cortisol today."

    • "Sick-day rules save lives" — rehearse them at every supply.

    • Pale patient with AI features on long-term steroids = secondary/tertiary AI — no pigmentation expected.

    Self-check quiz

    5 questions · optional
    1. Q1When should you think of Addison’s disease: skin signs?Reveal

      Gradual tanning not explained by sun, pigmented creases, scars and buccal mucosa, with fatigue, weight loss and dizziness.

    2. Q2What drives it?Reveal

      Autoimmune destruction of the adrenal cortex; low cortisol drives high ACTH and MSH, which stimulate melanocytes.

    3. Q3What is the backbone of care?Reveal

      U&E (low sodium, high potassium), 9am cortisol and urgent endocrine referral; steroid emergency card and sick-day rules once diagnosed.

    4. Q4What must you never miss?Reveal

      Adrenal crisis (vomiting, hypotension, collapse): hydrocortisone 100 mg IM/IV and 999.

    5. Q5Name a red flag that needs escalation.Reveal

      Vomiting, abdominal pain, confusion or collapse with suspected adrenal insufficiency: adrenal crisis, hydrocortisone 100 mg IM and 999.
