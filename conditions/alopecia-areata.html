    Hair & scalp

    Alopecia areata

    Autoimmune non-scarring bald patches with exclamation-mark hairs. Many limited patches regrow; refer extensive, childhood or distressed disease.

    Module 3 · Week 3 · 16 images

    Also coversalopecia areatapatchy hair lossexclamation mark hairsalopecia totalisalopecia universalisophiasis
    Think of it when

    Sudden smooth round bald patch without scale or scarring; exclamation-mark hairs at the active edge; nails may be pitted.

    What drives it

    CD8+ T-cell attack on the anagen bulb (JAK–STAT); stem-cell bulge spared → non-scarring and potentially reversible.

    Backbone of care

    Explain and support; watchful waiting or potent scalp steroid for 3 months in adults; camouflage/wigs; refer severe, childhood or non-responding disease.

    Never miss

    Scaly childhood patch = tinea until proven otherwise. Loss of follicular ostia = scarring alopecia — urgent dermatology.

    01

    What it is

    Alopecia areata (AA) is a common autoimmune non-scarring hair loss causing sudden smooth bald patches on any hair-bearing site. Lifetime risk is about 2%. Spectrum: patchy AA, ophiasis, diffuse AA, alopecia totalis (all scalp) and universalis (all body hair).

    02

    How it’s caused

    • Collapse of hair-follicle immune privilege → peribulbar T-cell attack; IFN-γ/IL-15 via JAK–STAT (rationale for specialist JAK inhibitors).
    • Genetic predisposition (~1 in 5 family history). Triggers sometimes cited: stress, illness — often none found.
    • Associations: autoimmune thyroid disease, vitiligo, type 1 diabetes, atopy, Down syndrome; higher anxiety/depression rates.
    03

    Clinical features

    • Round/oval smooth skin-coloured patches; no scale, erythema or scarring; ostia preserved.
    • Exclamation-mark hairs at active margins; pull test positive at the edge.
    • Grey/white hairs often spared; regrowth may start as fine white vellus hairs.
    • Nail pitting (thimble pits); also trachyonychia.
    • May involve brows, lashes and beard — avoid potent topical steroids on the face.
    04

    Diagnosis

    • Clinical: extent, speed, prior episodes, autoimmune/atopy history, psychosocial impact.
    • Trichoscopy: exclamation marks, yellow dots (ostia present), black dots, tapered/regrowing hairs.
    • Mycology if scale or childhood doubt (exclude tinea). Syphilis serology if moth-eaten. ANA if lupus possible.
    • Thyroid: consider TSH ± TPO especially in totalis/universalis or symptoms; check TSH is in range if already hypothyroid (coexistent telogen effluvium).
    • Routine iron/zinc/vitamin D supplementation is not evidence-based for AA (BAD).
    05

    Differentials

    Look-alikeClues
    TrichotillomaniaIrregular patches; hairs of different lengths; pull test negative — see trichotillomania
    Tinea capitisScale, black dots, nodes; children; mycology
    Telogen effluviumDiffuse shed; trigger 2–3 months earlier — see telogen effluvium
    Pattern hair lossGradual miniaturisation — see androgenetic alopecia
    Scarring alopeciaLost ostia, shiny scalp, symptoms — see scarring alopecia
    06

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    Pharmacist / IP

    Primary care (CKS / BAD themes)

    • Explain unpredictability; ~80% of limited short-duration patches regrow within a year, but relapses are common. Extensive/AT/AU: full recovery ~1 in 10.
    • Sun protection on bald patches; signpost Alopecia UK / BAD leaflets; screen psychological distress.
    • No treatment / watchful waiting if already regrowing or small non-distressing patches.
    • Adults: potent or very potent scalp steroid once daily for about 3 months (off-label); reassess 3–6 months. Never potent steroid on face/brows.
    • Camouflage, fibres, brows/lashes cosmetics; NHS wigs need a dermatologist’s prescription.

    Refer

    • Child, pregnancy/breastfeeding, diagnostic doubt, severe/rapid progression (ophiasis, AT/AU), no response at 3–6 months, major psychosocial impact, facial-site treatment wanted, or wig prescription needed.
    • Specialist options (counsel only): intralesional steroid, contact immunotherapy, JAK inhibitors, systemic agents — do not initiate without training/pathway.
    07

    Red flags & referral

    Escalate
    • Childhood scaly patch → treat/investigate as tinea.
    • Lost follicular openings / symptoms of scarring → urgent derm.
    • Significant distress, school withdrawal, self-harm risk → mental-health pathways.
    08

    Prescriber pearls

    • Yellow dots on trichoscopy prove follicles are still there — hope for regrowth.
    • Regrowth takes months and may be white at first — set expectations.
    • Check TSH is optimised if the patient is already on thyroxine.
