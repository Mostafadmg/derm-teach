    Basal cell carcinoma (BCC)

    The commonest skin cancer: a slowly growing pearly papule or non-healing sore, usually on the face. Learn the subtypes, the dermoscopy clues, the NICE NG12 referral speed, and the treatment ladder (surgery first-line for nodular BCC, BAD 2021).

    Also calledBCCrodent ulcerpearly papulenodular BCCsuperficial BCCmorphoeic BCC+3
    Related pagesCutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC)Melanoma: recognition & referralActinic keratoses (solar keratoses)Skin surgery procedures: excision, curettage, cryotherapy & Mohs

    Key facts

    • The commonest cancer in the UK: a slow-growing, locally invasive tumour of basal keratinocytes that almost never metastasises.
    • Mostly older, fair-skinned adults with sun damage; 80% on the head and neck.
    • Classic nodular BCC: pearly papule with rolled edge and arborising telangiectasia, often with central ulceration (rodent ulcer).
    • Caused by hedgehog pathway mutations (PTCH1, SMO), usually from UV exposure.
    • UK (NICE NG12): routine (not 2WW) referral for most suspected BCC; urgent if site or size threatens function. Surgery is the mainstay.

    Overview

    The commonest human cancer. It is a slow-growing, locally invasive malignancy of basal keratinocyte-like cells. Metastasis is very rare (<0.1%), but neglected lesions destroy tissue (nose, eye, ear.

    Epidemiology

    • Incidence
      • The commonest cancer in white populations; well over 100,000 new cases a year in the UK and rising.
      • Lifetime risk about 1 in 5 in some fair-skinned populations.
    • Who
      • Usually over 60, more men; rare in people with dark skin but does occur (more often pigmented).

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    • Intermittent intense and cumulative UV, with a latency of decades (course teaching gave 20–50 years). Fair skin (Fitzpatrick I–II), age, male sex.
    • Other risks: previous BCC (about 40% get another within 3 years), radiotherapy, arsenic, immunosuppression (transplant), and hydrochlorothiazide (MHRA 2018: dose-dependent increased NMSC risk, mainly SCC).
    • Hedgehog pathway: most BCCs have loss of PTCH1 or activating SMO mutations, which is why hedgehog inhibitors (vismodegib, sonidegib) work in advanced disease.
    • Gorlin (naevoid BCC) syndrome: autosomal dominant PTCH1 (or SUFU) mutation. Multiple BCCs from youth, palmar/plantar pits, odontogenic keratocysts of the jaw, calcified falx, bifid ribs, macrocephaly, frontal bossing, and medulloblastoma in childhood (SUFU). Avoid radiotherapy (it induces many new BCCs) and minimise CT/X-ray.
    • Histology: nests of basaloid cells with peripheral palisading, retraction artefact (clefting) and mucinous stroma.

    Risk factors and associations

    • Intermittent intense UV and childhood sunburn; fair skin, red or blond hair, freckling.
    • Previous BCC (about 40% get another within 3 years), sunbeds, radiotherapy, arsenic.
    • Immunosuppression, hydrochlorothiazide (possible), syndromes: Gorlin (basal cell naevus) syndrome, xeroderma pigmentosum.

    Myths to address

    • "Skin cancer always spreads": BCC rarely metastasises but can destroy local tissue if neglected.
    • "It's a spot that keeps bleeding and healing": a non-healing or recurrently bleeding spot needs review.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    BCC grows from basal-layer cells when the hedgehog signalling pathway is switched on permanently.

    1. 1

      UV mutation

      UV damages PTCH1 (a hedgehog-pathway brake) or activates SMO; TP53 mutations often coexist.

    2. 2

      Uncontrolled hedgehog signalling

      GLI transcription factors stay on, so basaloid cells keep proliferating.

    3. 3

      Local invasion

      Tumour nests depend on surrounding stroma, which is why BCC invades locally but rarely spreads by blood or lymph.

    4. 4

      Growth patterns

      Nodular, superficial, infiltrative and morphoeic patterns differ in how far they extend beyond what is visible.

    5. 5

      Ulceration

      Central necrosis gives the classic rodent ulcer, with a rolled pearly edge.

    Histopathology

    • Nests of basaloid cells with peripheral palisading, retraction artefact and mucinous stroma.
    • Subtype (nodular, superficial, infiltrative, morphoeic, micronodular) guides treatment and recurrence risk.

    Clinical features

    About 80–85% occur on the head and neck. Main subtypes (the course "5"):

    Subtypes to recognise

    • Nodular

      Commonest. Pearly nodule, rolled edge, telangiectasia, central ulcer. Site: Face.

    • Superficial

      Pink, scaly thin plaque with a thread-like pearly edge. Site: Trunk, limbs; younger.

    • Morphoeic

      Scar-like, waxy, ill-defined; spreads unseen. High-risk. Site: Face.

    • Pigmented

      Brown-blue-grey; commoner in skin of colour; melanoma DDx. Site: Face.

    Special groups

    • Skin of colour
      • Less common; often pigmented and mistaken for a naevus or melanoma.
    • Young patients or many BCCs
      • Consider Gorlin syndrome (jaw cysts, palmar pits) and refer.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    • Clinical + dermoscopy: arborising (tree-like) vessels in sharp focus; blue-grey ovoid nests/globules; spoke-wheel and leaf-like structures; ulceration; shiny white structures.
    • The skin cancer team confirms with punch or excision biopsy. Histology determines the subtype and risk.

    History

    • Slow growth; bleeds, crusts, never fully heals.
    • UV exposure, fair skin, previous BCC (~40% get another in 3 years).
    • Immunosuppression, radiotherapy, hydrochlorothiazide (MHRA).

    Examine

    • Site and size (H-zone?), edge definition.
    • Whole-skin check for other lesions.

    Dermoscopy

    • Arborising vessels in sharp focus.
    • Blue-grey ovoid nests; spoke-wheel and leaf-like areas; shiny white structures.

    Tests

    • Skin cancer team: punch or excision biopsy.
    • Histology: basaloid nests, peripheral palisading, retraction clefts.

    Sebaceous hyperplasia (the skin lumps notes see Where course teaching differs from UK guidance), intradermal naevus (soft, symmetrical, vessels out of focus; see the naevi notes), amelanotic melanoma, SCC/KA, Bowen's (for superficial BCC), eczema/psoriasis plaque (for superficial BCC), morphoea or scar (for morphoeic), DFSP (the skin lumps notes see Trigeminal trophic syndrome (TTS) and Case 1 care-home context), trigeminal trophic syndrome (see Trigeminal trophic syndrome (TTS) and Case 1 care-home context), molluscum, CCA.

    What it is

    A rare cause of self-inflicted ulceration of the nasal ala after damage to the trigeminal nerve or its central pathways.

    How it is caused / pathophysiology

    Trigeminal sensory loss or paraesthesia (after a brainstem/lateral medullary stroke, trigeminal ablation, herpes zoster, acoustic neuroma surgery, or vertebrobasilar ischaemia) leads to unconscious rubbing, scratching and picking. The result is a chronic, non-healing ulcer. Vascular dementia with brainstem infarction, plus picking behaviour, fits this picture.

    Clinical features & typical sites

    A crescent-shaped ulcer of the nasal ala that often spares the nasal tip (the tip is supplied by the external nasal branch, which is often spared). It is unilateral and progressive, and can destroy the ala. The area is numb or tingly.

    How to diagnose

    History of trigeminal injury, sensory testing, and biopsy to exclude BCC/SCC (and infection: granulomatous disease, mycobacterial or fungal infection; vasculitis/GPA).

    Key differentials

    BCC (main), SCC, granulomatosis with polyangiitis, lupus vulgaris, leishmaniasis, NK/T-cell lymphoma, pyoderma gangrenosum, and dermatitis artefacta.

    Management for pharmacist/IP (UK)

    • Refer for biopsy (the course answer). The IP role is to arrange the referral and not to treat the ulcer as infected or as eczema without a diagnosis.
    • TTS treatment (specialist): behavioural protection (occlusive dressings, nail care, mittens or protective devices in dementia), treat paraesthesia (carbamazepine, gabapentin or amitriptyline at low dose, with careful weighing of anticholinergic burden and falls risk in an 85-year-old on donepezil), and reconstructive surgery once the picking stops.
    • Capacity and consent (MCA 2005): assess capacity for each decision (biopsy, surgery). If capacity is lacking, decide in the person's best interests, consulting family or an LPA for health and welfare (or an IMCA if no one is appropriate). Weigh the burden of travel and procedures against benefit. For a frail resident with a slow-growing BCC, options include surgery under local anaesthetic, radiotherapy, or active monitoring with palliative wound care. This is an MDT decision.
    • Medicines for Case 1: continue aspirin for biopsy or skin surgery (BSDS 2023). Donepezil plus beta-blockers or other bradycardic drugs carries a bradycardia/syncope risk, which matters if sedation is considered. No interaction issue with local anaesthetic at standard doses.

    Prescriber pearls & counselling

    Care-home staff should report non-healing lesions on the face for more than 4 weeks. An "infected scab" on the nose of an older person that won't heal is a BCC until proved otherwise.

    Management

    Recognition and referral (IP role)

    • NICE NG12 1.7.5: suspected BCC means a routine (non-urgent) referral, usually to dermatology.
    • NG12 1.7.6: consider the suspected cancer pathway only if delay could have an unacceptable impact because of site or size, e.g. near the eye or nose, or a large or rapidly growing lesion. Case 1 nasal ulcer qualifies for discussion.
    • Who excises: the NICE Improving Outcomes Guidance for skin tumours (and its 2010 update) allows suitably trained, accredited primary-care clinicians to excise only low-risk BCCs (e.g. a small nodular BCC on the trunk or limbs in a non-immunosuppressed patient). Anything high-risk goes to a specialist skin cancer team. IPs do not biopsy, curette, freeze or apply topical treatment to an undiagnosed suspected BCC.

    BAD 2021 high-risk features (determine margins/Mohs):

    • Site and size: area H (central face, eyelids, nose, lips, ears, temple), area M lesions >10 mm, area L lesions >20 mm.
    • Poorly defined borders.
    • Recurrence.
    • Immunosuppression.
    • Prior radiotherapy at the site.
    • Histology: infiltrative, micronodular, morphoeic or basosquamous; perineural invasion; depth >6 mm or beyond the subcutis.

    Prognosis: excellent for treated primary low-risk BCC (5-year recurrence under 5% after surgery). Recurrence is higher for high-risk subtypes and sites. Mortality is negligible except in neglected or metastatic disease. About 40% develop another BCC, so self-examination and sun protection matter.

    The BCC treatment ladder

    See the skin cancer notes:

    • surgery (standard excision 4 mm / ≥5 mm margins) → Mohs for high-risk/recurrent
    • C&C or cryo for small low-risk lesions
    • imiquimod, 5-FU or PDT for superficial BCC
    • radiotherapy if unfit
    • hedgehog inhibitors for advanced disease.

    Worked cases

    Case 1: 85-year-old care-home resident, enlarging picked nasal ulcer, vascular dementia

    • Diagnosis: nodular BCC (ulcerated "rodent ulcer").
    • Key differential: trigeminal trophic syndrome. Also SCC and GPA.
    • Investigation: biopsy (dermatology). Histology: nests of basaloid cells with peripheral palisading and retraction clefts.
    • Referral: NG12 1.7.5 says routine, but nose = H-zone, so consider the suspected cancer pathway (1.7.6) if it is large or close to vital structures.
    • Capacity / best-interests decisions under the MCA. Continue aspirin for any procedure.
    • Treatment (specialist): surgical excision (or Mohs for a high-risk facial BCC) is first-line; radiotherapy if unfit. Not PDT (it is for superficial/thin nodular BCC) (see Where course teaching differs from UK guidance).

    Case 2: BCC related questions

    • Aetiology: UV, PTCH1/SMO, Gorlin, immunosuppression, radiation, arsenic.
    • Surgery: standard excision, 4 mm (low-risk) / ≥5 mm (high-risk) margins; Mohs for high-risk/recurrent/H-zone.
    • Variants: nodular, superficial, morphoeic/infiltrative, micronodular, pigmented, basosquamous.
    • Prognosis: excellent; recurrence is the issue, not mortality.

    Case 3: 68-year-old outdoor worker, indurated bleeding lower-lip ulcer for 6 weeks

    • Diagnosis: SCC of the lip (on actinic cheilitis).
    • Why worrying: the lip is a high-risk site with a higher metastatic rate.
    • Treatment: surgical excision (6 mm margin for high-risk per BAD 2020), with nodal assessment.
    • Referral: NG12 1.7.4 suspected cancer pathway, plus a lip ulcer >3 weeks meets the oral cancer criteria.

    Case 4: SCC related questions

    • Precursors: AK, actinic cheilitis, Bowen's, KA, horn, chronic wounds (Marjolin), lichen sclerosus.
    • Investigations: biopsy, node examination, ultrasound ± FNA, CT/MRI for very high-risk.
    • Management: excision, Mohs (selected), RT, cemiplimab for advanced disease, follow-up and photoprotection.

    Case 5: 62-year-old mechanic, steroid-unresponsive rash for 12 months

    • Diagnosis: mycosis fungoides. Rule out occupational contact dermatitis (patch test) and drug-induced pseudolymphoma.
    • Plan: refer for (repeat) biopsies with TCR clonality, then staging. Skin-directed therapy (potent topical steroid, nbUVB/PUVA phototherapy, chlormethine) for early stage.
    • Histology: epidermotropism with Pautrier microabscesses.
    • Stage: cannot be assigned from the vignette. Tumours = IIB, erythroderma = III, blood involvement = IV.

    Caution

    Prescribing limits and pitfalls

    • High-risk features: H-zone; M-zone > 10 mm, L-zone > 20 mm; poorly defined; recurrent; immunosuppressed; prior RT; aggressive histology; perineural; depth > 6 mm.
    • Prognosis: 5-year recurrence < 5% after surgery for low-risk primary BCC.
    • Primary-care excision: Only low-risk BCCs by accredited clinicians (NICE IOG).

    Referral

    • Urgent

      Near the eye, nose or ear, large, fast-growing or recurrent: consider the suspected cancer pathway (NG12 1.7.6).

    • Routine

      Typical BCC at a low-risk site: routine dermatology referral (NG12 1.7.5).

    • Routine

      Multiple BCCs young, palmar pits or jaw cysts: think Gorlin syndrome.

    • Don’t

      Never treat an undiagnosed pigmented or nodular lesion: refer for diagnosis first.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • BCC near the eye, nose or ear, large, rapidly growing or recurrent: consider the suspected cancer pathway because delay could matter (NG12 1.7.6).
    • Do not freeze, curette or apply imiquimod to an undiagnosed pigmented or nodular lesion: refer for diagnosis first.
    • Multiple BCCs in a young person, palmar pits or jaw cysts: think Gorlin syndrome and refer.

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Local tissue destruction (eye, nose, ear) if neglected.
    • Recurrence, especially infiltrative/morphoeic BCC and H-zone sites.
    • Further BCCs and other skin cancers.
    • Metastasis is very rare (under 0.1%).

    Prognosis

    • Excellent: over 95% cure with standard excision.
    • Annual skin self-checks and sun protection are needed because new BCCs are common.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • Medicines review for keratinocyte cancer patients: Hydrochlorothiazide: MHRA advises informing patients of the NMSC risk and considering alternatives in those with previous skin cancer; Long-term voriconazole: phototoxicity and SCC; Azathioprine and other transplant immunosuppressants

    • Imiquimod counselling: apply at bedtime, wash off after about 8 h; expect redness, crusting and erosion (it means the drug is working); stop and seek advice if there is severe reaction or flu-like illness.

    • Sun protection: SPF 30+ with high UVA rating, shade, hats (NICE NG34). Advise a self-check every month.

    • Hedgehog inhibitors: pregnancy prevention for women (and condoms for men on vismodegib); cramps, taste loss and weight loss are common.

    Self-check quiz

    5 questions · optional
    1. Q1What is the NICE NG12 referral speed for a typical BCC?Reveal

      Routine referral (1.7.5); consider the suspected cancer pathway only if site or size makes delay matter (1.7.6).

    2. Q2Classic dermoscopy clue?Reveal

      Arborising (tree-like) vessels in sharp focus; also blue-grey ovoid nests.

    3. Q3First-line treatment for most BCCs, including nodular BCC on the face?Reveal

      Standard surgical excision (4 mm margin low-risk, ≥ 5 mm high-risk); Mohs for high-risk or recurrent.

    4. Q4Which mimic causes a crescent ulcer of the nasal ala that spares the tip?Reveal

      Trigeminal trophic syndrome. Biopsy to exclude BCC.

    5. Q5What must an IP never do with a suspected BCC?Reveal

      Freeze, curette or apply imiquimod to an undiagnosed lesion. Refer for diagnosis first.
