    Blistering diseases

    Approach to blistering diseases

    Module 4 · Week 6

    Sort a blistering rash by where the skin splits (subcorneal, intraepidermal, subepidermal), whether it is immunobullous, and how fast to refer. Biopsy for immunofluorescence is done by specialists, but the referral speed is decided in primary care.

    Coversblistersbullaesplit levelimmunobullousNikolskydirect immunofluorescenceDIFbiopsy
    Think of it when

    Blisters or erosions without an obvious burn, bite, friction or infection.

    What drives it

    Autoantibodies against desmosomes (intraepidermal, flaccid) or hemidesmosomes (subepidermal, tense); drugs; infection; genetics (EB).

    Backbone of care

    Identify the pattern, refer at the right speed, look for a drug cause, and prescribe and monitor within the specialist plan.

    Never miss

    SJS/TEN, pemphigus with mouth erosions, eye involvement, pemphigoid in pregnancy, eczema herpeticum and SSSS in children.

    What it is

    A vesicle is a fluid-filled lesion under 5 mm (some texts say 10 mm) and a bulla is a larger one. An erosion is loss of epidermis only, often all that remains of a fragile, superficial blister. The depth of the split explains what you see. The higher the split, the more flaccid the blister and the more erosions there are. The deeper the split, the more tense the blister, and haemorrhage, scarring and milia can follow.

    Clinical features

    • Flaccid blisters or erosions only, with oral ulcers suggest pemphigus (PV), or SJS/TEN if acute and the patient is unwell.
    • Tense blisters, sometimes haemorrhagic, on an urticarial or eczematous base in an elderly person with itch suggest BP.
    • Excoriations and tiny grouped vesicles on extensors and buttocks, intensely itchy suggest DH.
    • Fragile blisters, scars and milia on the backs of the hands with hypertrichosis suggest PCT (or pseudoporphyria, or EBA).
    • A "string of pearls" annular arrangement suggests linear IgA disease (often drug-induced, e.g. vancomycin).
    • Flexural erosions with fissured maceration suggest Hailey-Hailey. Sudden, painful, targetoid or dusky skin with mucosal involvement suggests SJS/TEN.

    The Nikolsky sign (shearing the epidermis off with lateral pressure) is positive in PV, SSSS and TEN, and usually negative in BP. The Asboe-Hansen sign (pressure spreads the blister edge sideways) points to PV.

    4

    Diagnosis

    (specialist workup that the IP should know)

    • Two biopsies (this is the tutor's summary point): Lesional, from the edge of a fresh (<48 h) blister, for H&E (split level, acantholysis, eosinophils/neutrophils), sent in formalin; Perilesional, from normal-looking skin about 1 cm from a blister (or uninvolved skin for DH), for direct immunofluorescence (DIF). Send it in Michel's medium or fresh on saline gauze, never formalin. Biopsy of an eroded or ulcerated site gives a false negative
    • DIF patterns (use photos A06 and C05): intercellular "chicken-wire/fishnet" IgG ± C3 = pemphigus; linear IgG/C3 at the BMZ = BP/MMP/PG; linear IgA = LAD; granular IgA in the dermal papillae = DH; perivascular IgG/C3 and "doughnut" deposits = PCT
    • Salt-split skin separates BP (epidermal roof) from EBA (dermal floor).
    • Serology: ELISA for anti-Dsg1/Dsg3 (titres roughly follow activity) and anti-BP180/BP230; indirect IMF; for DH, tTG-IgA + total IgA (± EMA); for paraneoplastic pemphigus, anti-envoplakin/periplakin
    • Baseline for systemic therapy (the IP will be asked about these): FBC, U&E, LFT, glucose/HbA1c, BP, weight, bone profile + vitamin D, TPMT (azathioprine), G6PD (dapsone), hepatitis B/C, HIV, VZV serology, TB screen (IGRA/chest X-ray) before rituximab or high-dose steroids, a pregnancy test, and immunoglobulins before rituximab.
    5

    Differentials

    "Not everything that blisters is immunobullous":

    • bullous impetigo and SSSS
    • HSV/VZV (eczema herpeticum)
    • bullous insect bites (Module 4 exam Q15)
    • acute contact dermatitis and phytophotodermatitis
    • pompholyx
    • friction, burns and oedema blisters
    • bullous drug reactions
    • EM
    • bullous tinea
    • bullous LP
    • genodermatoses (EB, Hailey-Hailey)
    6

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    • Do not start oral steroids "to see" before biopsy unless the specialist advises it. They reduce the diagnostic yield, and in DH/coeliac a gluten-free diet would do the same to serology.
    • Supportive care while waiting for an urgent appointment: bland emollients and a soap substitute; puncture large tense blisters at the base with a sterile needle but leave the roof on; non-adherent dressings (silicone/paraffin gauze) and no adhesive tape on fragile skin; a potent or superpotent TCS only for suspected BP once dermatology agrees; analgesia, plus benzydamine/chlorhexidine mouthwash or an antiseptic mouthwash for oral erosions
    • Medication review is the IP's core skill: gliptins, loop diuretics, spironolactone, PD-1 inhibitors, thiol drugs (penicillamine, captopril), vancomycin, NSAIDs, and recent new drugs (SJS/TEN window 4–28 days).

    • IP don'ts: don't start oral prednisolone for undiagnosed blistering without a specialist discussion; don't put adhesive dressings on fragile skin; don't de-roof tense blisters; don't advise a gluten-free diet before coeliac tests.
    8

    Prescriber pearls

    • Ask about mouth, eyes and genitals at every blistering consultation. Mucosal involvement changes the urgency and the referral route.
    • This helps triage by teledermatology.
    • Infection risk: crusting, pain, fever or a sudden spread of erosions may mean secondary S. aureus or HSV. Swab and treat.

    Common misconceptions corrected

    PostStatementCorrection
    Day 1 (yours)Split levels: "dermolytic: EB"EB occurs at three levels. EB simplex is intraepidermal (basal keratinocyte, keratin 5/14). Junctional EB is in the lamina lucida (laminin-332, collagen XVII). Only dystrophic EB (collagen VII) is dermolytic, below the lamina densa. EBA (acquired, collagen VII) is also dermolytic. See the infant skin pages notes
    Day 1 (yours)Pemphigoid drug triggers: etanercept, furosemideFurosemide is a fair example, and etanercept is a case-report association. Add DPP-4 inhibitors (gliptins), the best-established and most relevant to primary care, plus spironolactone and PD-1 inhibitors
    Day 1 (yours)Subcorneal: impetigo, PFCorrect. SSSS also belongs here (exfoliative toxin cleaving Dsg1, the same target as PF).
    Short notes (yours)Pemphigus = vulgaris, foliaceus, erythematosus, vegetans, paraneoplasticGood. Add IgA pemphigus and drug-induced pemphigus. Erythematosus is a localised form of PF, and vegetans is a variant of PV
    Formative quiz (peers)QA answered "intraepidermal acantholysis" or "granular IgA"That is PV or DH. BP is subepidermal with linear BMZ IgG/C3
    Overview (tutor)"DPP-4 inhibitors are a common cause of drug-induced bullous pemphigus; lisinopril can prevent it"Gliptins cause pemphigoid. ACE inhibitors (including lisinopril) are a weak cause of pemphigus, not prevention. There is no evidence that lisinopril prevents gliptin-associated BP
    Overview (tutor)IVIG works by blocking CD95 (Fas) in TENThat was the in-vitro rationale. Clinical evidence is mixed, and the UK 2016 guideline does not routinely recommend IVIG. Ciclosporin is often preferred
