    Cellulitis & erysipelas

    Spreading dermal infection, usually in one leg. Exclude the mimics, draw the border, give flucloxacillin for 5–7 days and safety-net hard. Bilateral red legs are rarely cellulitis.

    Also calledcellulitiserysipelasred legskin and soft tissue infectionSSTIperiorbital cellulitis+7
    Related pagesRed leg: differentials & referralVenous leg ulcerTinea: corporis, cruris & pedisBoils & carbunclesImpetigo

    Key facts

    • Acute bacterial infection of the dermis and subcutis (cellulitis) or upper dermis and lymphatics (erysipelas).
    • Usually Streptococcus pyogenes (about two thirds) or Staphylococcus aureus (about one third).
    • Typically one hot, red, swollen, tender leg; bilateral red legs are rarely cellulitis.
    • UK: NICE NG141; flucloxacillin 500 mg–1 g QDS for 5–7 days for most adults; mark the border and review at 2–3 days.
    • Emergency if necrotising fasciitis or sepsis is possible.

    Overview

    ConditionDepthEdgeUsual organism
    ErysipelasSuperficial dermis and lymphaticsSharp, raised, well-demarcatedUsually streptococci
    CellulitisDeeper dermis and subcutisLess distinctS. pyogenes, S. aureus

    In practice the two overlap and are managed the same way in primary care.

    Epidemiology

    • Common
      • One of the commonest reasons for antibiotic prescribing in primary care and for emergency admission with skin disease.
    • Who
      • All ages; more common in middle-aged and older adults.
      • Erysipelas is more common in older adults and infants.
    • Recurrence
      • Up to about a third have a further episode, especially with lymphoedema, venous disease or untreated tinea pedis.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    • A breach in the skin barrier lets bacteria in. Common entry points are tinea pedis, eczema, ulcers, trauma, bites, lymphoedema and previous surgery.
    • Bacteria spread through the dermis and subcutaneous tissue, with or without lymphangitis.
    • The host inflammatory response causes the redness, heat, swelling and systemic symptoms.
    • Risk factors: obesity, venous insufficiency, lymphoedema, diabetes, and previous cellulitis.
    PatientUsual organisms (Module 5)
    ImmunocompetentS. pyogenes (about two-thirds) and S. aureus (about one-third)
    ImmunocompromisedA broader range, including Gram-negatives, so take advice

    Risk factors and associations

    • Portal of entry: toe-web tinea pedis or fissured heels, eczema, ulcers, trauma, bites, surgical wounds, IV drug use.
    • Host factors: previous cellulitis, lymphoedema, venous insufficiency, obesity, diabetes, immunosuppression, chronic kidney or liver disease, pregnancy, alcohol excess.
    • Organisms in special settings: Pasteurella (cat or dog bites), Pseudomonas (puncture wounds of the foot), Vibrio or Aeromonas (salt or fresh water), MRSA, Gram-negatives in the immunocompromised.

    Myths to address

    • "It was a spider bite": documented spider bites rarely cause cellulitis; look for tinea pedis or a skin break.
    • "Still red on day 3, so the antibiotic failed": redness takes days to settle; check the border and the patient.
    • "Cellulitis is contagious": it is not usually spread person to person.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    Bacteria cross a breach in the skin, multiply in the dermis and trigger an acute inflammatory response.

    1. 1

      A breach in the barrier

      A fissure, cut, bite, ulcer or macerated toe web lets skin flora into the dermis; lymphoedema and venous stasis impair clearance.

    2. 2

      Bacteria multiply and spread

      Streptococci release hyaluronidase, streptokinase and DNases that help them spread through tissue planes; staphylococci form local abscesses more often.

    3. 3

      Depth decides the edge

      Erysipelas involves the upper dermis and lymphatics, giving a raised, sharp edge; cellulitis extends into deep dermis and fat, so the edge is flat and ill-defined.

    4. 4

      Inflammation

      Cytokines (TNF, IL-1, IL-6) cause the redness, heat, swelling and pain, fever and malaise; toxins can cause systemic illness.

    5. 5

      Healing and damage

      Lymphatics damaged by each episode drain less well, increasing oedema and the risk of recurrence.

    Histopathology

    • Dermal oedema, dilated lymphatics and a diffuse neutrophilic infiltrate extending into subcutaneous fat.
    • Organisms are often scanty; biopsy is not used for diagnosis.

    Clinical features

    • Cellulitis: expanding redness, heat, swelling and tenderness, usually in one leg. May have fever, malaise, lymphadenopathy and lymphangitic streaking. Can have blisters.
    • Erysipelas: bright red, hot, raised, sharply demarcated plaque, often on the face or leg. Fever and chills are common and may come before the rash.
    • Skin of colour: redness is much less obvious, so rely on swelling, warmth, pain and induration.
    • Look for peau d’orange, bullae, purpura, lymphangitic streaks and tender regional lymph nodes.
    • Fever and rigors can come before the rash.

    Prescriber tip

    Milian’s ear sign

    Redness involving the pinna favours erysipelas over cellulitis. The ear has no deep dermis or subcutaneous fat, so deep cellulitis cannot really involve it.

    Look for

    • Peau d’orange

      Dimpled swollen skin: mark and reassess. Site: Leg.

    • Bullous cellulitis

      Still a soft-tissue infection; escalate if severe. Site: Leg.

    • Facial erysipelas

      Bright, well-demarcated plaque. Site: Face.

    • Milian’s ear sign

      Pinna involved → erysipelas. Site: Ear.

    • Portal of entry

      Macerated toe-web tinea. Site: Toe webs.

    Special groups

    • Skin of colour
      • Redness may be hard to see: rely on warmth, swelling, tenderness and induration, and compare with the other leg.
    • Children
      • Periorbital or facial cellulitis needs a low threshold for same-day assessment.
    • Pregnancy
      • Erythromycin if penicillin allergic (NICE NG141).

    Diagnosis and investigations

    • Clinical. Exclude non-infective mimics before giving antibiotics.
    • Mark the border with a skin pen and record the date and time. Photograph if possible, to monitor spread.
    • Assess severity: observations, NEWS2 or sepsis screen, comorbidities and site (face or periorbital).
    • Swab only if there is broken skin with a penetrating injury, water exposure, or infection acquired outside the UK.
    • Bloods and blood cultures belong in the hospital pathway for unwell patients.

    Clinical

    • Exclude mimics before antibiotics.
    • Bilateral? Rethink.

    Mark it

    • Pen the border, date and time; photograph.

    Severity

    • Obs, NEWS2 / sepsis screen, comorbidities, face or periorbital site.

    Tests

    Red flag

    Necrotising fasciitis

    Severe pain out of proportion to the signs, rapid spread, systemic toxicity, dusky or purple skin, bullae or crepitus. This is an emergency: 999 or ED.

    See also: Red leg: differentials & referral

    Management

    Aligned with NICE NG141 and CKS.

    Supportive

    • Draw around the edge, elevate the limb, give analgesia and encourage fluids.
    • Use emollient for dry skin. Avoid compression during acute cellulitis.
    • Treat the portal of entry: tinea pedis, eczema, ulcers, lymphoedema.

    Antibiotics (adults, oral)

    SituationChoice
    First lineFlucloxacillin 500 mg–1 g four times daily for 5–7 days (longer if clinically needed)
    Penicillin allergyClarithromycin, doxycycline or erythromycin (erythromycin in pregnancy)
    Near the eyes or nose (periorbital / ‘danger triangle’)Co-amoxiclav (penicillin allergy: clarithromycin + metronidazole). Consider specialist advice
    Severe, or unable to take oralIV in hospital or the community IV service; review switch to oral by 48 hours
    • Reassess if worse at any time, or not improving after 2–3 days.
    • Recurrent cellulitis (2 or more episodes in 12 months): a specialist may consider prophylaxis (e.g. phenoxymethylpenicillin). This is not routine in primary care.

    Note

    Recurrent cellulitis

    Two or more episodes: treat the portals of entry (tinea pedis, eczema), manage oedema with compression once the acute episode has settled, and discuss prophylactic penicillin with a clinician (based on the PATCH trials).

    Caution

    Prescribing limits and pitfalls

    • Day 3 redness: Skin takes days to settle: not automatic failure.
    • Not better at 48–72 h: Swab open wounds, consider MRSA, revisit the diagnosis.
    • Recurrent (≥ 2 in 12 months): Treat portals, compression once settled; specialist may consider penicillin prophylaxis.

    Referral

    • 999 / A&E

      Necrotising fasciitis: pain out of proportion, rapid spread, purple bullae, crepitus, very unwell.

    • 999 / A&E

      Sepsis: low BP, raised HR or RR, confusion, mottled skin.

    • Same day

      Periorbital / orbital: pain on eye movement, proptosis, reduced vision.

    • Urgent

      Spread beyond the border, or failure after 48–72 h oral therapy.

    • Routine

      Infants, frail elderly, immunocompromised, lymphoedema: lower threshold.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Necrotising fasciitis signs: pain out of proportion, rapid spread, purple bullae, crepitus, or a very unwell patient. Emergency.
    • Sepsis: low blood pressure, raised heart or breathing rate, confusion, mottled skin.
    • Periorbital or orbital cellulitis: pain on eye movement, proptosis, reduced vision. Same-day ophthalmology or ED.
    • Rapid spread beyond the marked border, or failure after 48–72 hours of oral therapy.
    • Infants, frail older adults, the immunocompromised, or significant lymphoedema: lower threshold to refer.

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Necrotising fasciitis, gas gangrene and streptococcal toxic shock.
    • Sepsis; bacteraemia with seeding to bone, joints or heart valves (endocarditis).
    • Abscess formation; lymphangitis.
    • Recurrent cellulitis and secondary lymphoedema.
    • Orbital cellulitis with visual loss or cavernous sinus thrombosis (facial infection).

    Prognosis

    • Most cases respond to oral antibiotics without lasting harm; skin can take weeks to look normal.
    • Recurrence is common; treating portals of entry and oedema reduces it.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • Bilateral red legs are not cellulitis until proven otherwise. Think venous eczema or lipodermatosclerosis.

    • The skin takes days to look normal after the bacteria are controlled. Residual redness on day 3 is not automatic failure, so avoid reflexively extending the course.

    • Always treat athlete’s foot to reduce recurrent leg cellulitis.

    • On the face, have a lower threshold for review or admission.

    • Safety-net: rapid worsening, severe pain, purple blisters or feeling faint means same-day emergency care.

    • At 48–72 hours, if it is not better: swab any open wound, consider MRSA, and revisit the diagnosis.
