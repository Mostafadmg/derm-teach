    Infections — bacterial

    Cellulitis & erysipelas

    Spreading dermal infection, usually in one leg. Exclude the mimics, draw the border, give flucloxacillin for 5–7 days and safety-net hard. Bilateral red legs are rarely cellulitis.

    Adult core · deepened with Module 5 Week 2 · 10 images

    Also coverscellulitiserysipelasred legskin and soft tissue infectionSSTIperiorbital cellulitisnecrotising fasciitisflucloxacillinNG141Milian's ear signpeau d'orangelymphangitisbullous cellulitis
    Think of it when

    Acute, unilateral, hot, red, swollen, tender skin, often on the lower leg, with or without fever. Erysipelas has a sharp, raised edge.

    What drives it

    Streptococci (± S. aureus) entering through a skin break such as a fissured toe web, ulcer or eczema.

    Backbone of care

    Mark the border, elevate, give analgesia, flucloxacillin 5–7 days, and treat the portal of entry.

    Never miss

    Necrotising fasciitis (pain out of proportion), sepsis and orbital cellulitis.

    01

    What it is

    ConditionDepthEdgeUsual organism
    ErysipelasSuperficial dermis and lymphaticsSharp, raised, well-demarcatedUsually streptococci
    CellulitisDeeper dermis and subcutisLess distinctS. pyogenes, S. aureus

    In practice the two overlap and are managed the same way in primary care.

    02

    How it’s caused

    • A breach in the skin barrier lets bacteria in. Common entry points are tinea pedis, eczema, ulcers, trauma, bites, lymphoedema and previous surgery.
    • Bacteria spread through the dermis and subcutaneous tissue, with or without lymphangitis.
    • The host inflammatory response causes the redness, heat, swelling and systemic symptoms.
    • Risk factors: obesity, venous insufficiency, lymphoedema, diabetes, and previous cellulitis.
    PatientUsual organisms (Module 5)
    ImmunocompetentS. pyogenes (about two-thirds) and S. aureus (about one-third)
    ImmunocompromisedA broader range, including Gram-negatives, so take advice
    03

    Clinical features

    • Cellulitis: expanding redness, heat, swelling and tenderness, usually in one leg. May have fever, malaise, lymphadenopathy and lymphangitic streaking. Can have blisters.
    • Erysipelas: bright red, hot, raised, sharply demarcated plaque, often on the face or leg. Fever and chills are common and may come before the rash.
    • Skin of colour: redness is much less obvious, so rely on swelling, warmth, pain and induration.
    • Look for peau d’orange, bullae, purpura, lymphangitic streaks and tender regional lymph nodes.
    • Fever and rigors can come before the rash.

    Prescriber tip

    Milian’s ear sign

    Redness involving the pinna favours erysipelas over cellulitis. The ear has no deep dermis or subcutaneous fat, so deep cellulitis cannot really involve it.

    04

    Diagnosis

    • Clinical. Exclude non-infective mimics before giving antibiotics.
    • Mark the border with a skin pen and record the date and time. Photograph if possible, to monitor spread.
    • Assess severity: observations, NEWS2 or sepsis screen, comorbidities and site (face or periorbital).
    • Swab only if there is broken skin with a penetrating injury, water exposure, or infection acquired outside the UK.
    • Bloods and blood cultures belong in the hospital pathway for unwell patients.
    05

    Differentials

    MimicDistinguishing clues
    Venous (stasis) eczema / lipodermatosclerosisOften bilateral, chronic, itchy or scaly, little pain, no fever
    DVTCalf swelling and tenderness, less skin redness; Wells score, D-dimer or Doppler
    GoutJoint-centred; very tender; history of attacks
    Insect bite reactionItch more than pain; central punctum; rapid onset
    Contact dermatitisItchy, vesicular or scaly; matches exposure; no fever
    ErysipeloidFish or meat handlers; finger or hand; violaceous
    Acute lipodermatosclerosisPainful, woody, red gaiter skin, often bilateral or recurrent; frequently misdiagnosed as cellulitis
    Lymphoedema flareLong history of swelling; less acute

    Red flag

    Necrotising fasciitis

    Severe pain out of proportion to the signs, rapid spread, systemic toxicity, dusky or purple skin, bullae or crepitus. This is an emergency: 999 or ED.

    See also: Red leg: differentials & referral

    06

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    Pharmacist / IP

    Aligned with NICE NG141 and CKS.

    Supportive

    • Draw around the edge, elevate the limb, give analgesia and encourage fluids.
    • Use emollient for dry skin. Avoid compression during acute cellulitis.
    • Treat the portal of entry: tinea pedis, eczema, ulcers, lymphoedema.

    Antibiotics (adults, oral)

    SituationChoice
    First lineFlucloxacillin 500 mg–1 g four times daily for 5–7 days (longer if clinically needed)
    Penicillin allergyClarithromycin, doxycycline or erythromycin (erythromycin in pregnancy)
    Near the eyes or nose (periorbital / ‘danger triangle’)Co-amoxiclav (penicillin allergy: clarithromycin + metronidazole). Consider specialist advice
    Severe, or unable to take oralIV in hospital or the community IV service; review switch to oral by 48 hours
    • Reassess if worse at any time, or not improving after 2–3 days.
    • Recurrent cellulitis (2 or more episodes in 12 months): a specialist may consider prophylaxis (e.g. phenoxymethylpenicillin). This is not routine in primary care.

    Note

    Recurrent cellulitis

    Two or more episodes: treat the portals of entry (tinea pedis, eczema), manage oedema with compression once the acute episode has settled, and discuss prophylactic penicillin with a clinician (based on the PATCH trials).

    07

    Red flags & referral

    Escalate
    • Necrotising fasciitis signs: pain out of proportion, rapid spread, purple bullae, crepitus, or a very unwell patient. Emergency.
    • Sepsis: low blood pressure, raised heart or breathing rate, confusion, mottled skin.
    • Periorbital or orbital cellulitis: pain on eye movement, proptosis, reduced vision. Same-day ophthalmology or ED.
    • Rapid spread beyond the marked border, or failure after 48–72 hours of oral therapy.
    • Infants, frail older adults, the immunocompromised, or significant lymphoedema: lower threshold to refer.
    08

    Prescriber pearls

    • Bilateral red legs are not cellulitis until proven otherwise. Think venous eczema or lipodermatosclerosis.
    • The skin takes days to look normal after the bacteria are controlled. Residual redness on day 3 is not automatic failure, so avoid reflexively extending the course.
    • Always treat athlete’s foot to reduce recurrent leg cellulitis.
    • On the face, have a lower threshold for review or admission.
    • Safety-net: rapid worsening, severe pain, purple blisters or feeling faint means same-day emergency care.
    • At 48–72 hours, if it is not better: swab any open wound, consider MRSA, and revisit the diagnosis.
