    Contact dermatitis

    Irritant vs allergic contact eczema — avoidance, emollients, stepped topical steroids, and when to refer for patch testing. Deepens the contact themes on the eczema page.

    Also calledallergic contact dermatitisirritant contact dermatitisACDICDpatch testingoccupational dermatitis+2
    Related pagesHand eczema (chronic, occupational & pompholyx)Eczema / dermatitisEmollients & soap substitutesTopical corticosteroids: potency, quantities & safety

    Key facts

    • Eczema caused by something touching the skin: irritant (ICD, about 80%) or allergic (ACD, type IV delayed hypersensitivity).
    • Hands and face are the commonest sites; occupational exposure is frequent (hairdressers, healthcare, cleaners, builders).
    • ACD: about 25 allergens cause half of cases; nickel is the leading cause worldwide.
    • Identify and avoid the cause (patch testing for suspected ACD), emollients and soap substitutes, topical steroid.
    • Persistent occupational dermatitis may need specialist and occupational health input.

    Overview

    Contact dermatitis is eczema caused by something touching the skin. Irritant contact dermatitis (ICD) is commoner and dose-dependent. Allergic contact dermatitis (ACD) needs prior sensitisation and is confirmed by patch testing.

    Atopic eczema pathways and general steroid/emollient skills sit on eczema / dermatitis. This page deepens ICD vs ACD, occupational disease, cosmetics, tattoos and patch-testing referral.

    Epidemiology

    • Common
      • Contact dermatitis accounts for most occupational skin disease; irritant contact dermatitis is about four times commoner than allergic.
    • Who
      • Any age; more common in adults with wet work; atopic people are at higher risk of irritant dermatitis.
      • Nickel allergy is commonest in women (jewellery, piercings) but falling in Europe since the EU Nickel Directive.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    ICDACD
    MechanismDirect toxic/physical barrier injuryType IV T-cell hypersensitivity
    SensitisationNot requiredRequired
    OnsetMinutes–hours or cumulativeTypically 24–72 h after re-exposure
    DoseConcentration/duration matterTiny amounts can elicit once sensitised
    • Common irritants: water, detergents, solvents, cutting oils, friction, heat.
    • Common allergens: nickel, fragrance mixes, rubber accelerators, preservatives (MI/MCI), PPD hair dye, topical antibiotics/steroids, epoxy resins.
    • Mixed ICD+ACD is frequent on hands. About four-fifths of occupational hand dermatitis starts as irritant.

    Risk factors and associations

    • Irritants: water and wet work, soaps and detergents, solvents, friction, cutting oils, alkalis and acids.
    • Common allergens: nickel, cobalt, chromate (cement, leather), fragrances, preservatives (methylisothiazolinone, formaldehyde releasers), PPD hair dye, rubber accelerators, topical medicines (neomycin, corticosteroids), plants (Primula, Compositae).
    • A damaged barrier (e.g. atopic eczema, leg ulcers, perianal disease) increases sensitisation to topical medicines.

    Myths to address

    • "I've used it for years, so I can't be allergic": sensitisation can develop after years of exposure.
    • "Hypoallergenic products are safe": they can still contain relevant allergens.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    Irritant dermatitis is direct damage; allergic dermatitis is a learned T-cell response.

    1. 1

      Irritant damage

      Irritants strip lipids and damage keratinocytes directly, causing barrier loss and non-specific inflammation in anyone exposed enough; no prior sensitisation needed.

    2. 2

      Sensitisation phase (allergic)

      Small reactive chemicals (haptens, under 500 Da) bind skin proteins; Langerhans cells carry them to lymph nodes and prime specific T cells (10–15 days).

    3. 3

      Elicitation phase

      On re-exposure, memory T cells release cytokines at the contact site within 24–72 hours, causing eczema (type IV hypersensitivity).

    4. 4

      Spread and chronicity

      Eczema can spread beyond the contact area (autosensitisation); continued exposure leads to chronic, lichenified, fissured dermatitis.

    Histopathology

    • Spongiotic dermatitis (intercellular oedema, vesicles) with lymphocytes; eosinophils favour allergic dermatitis.
    • Irritant: keratinocyte necrosis and neutrophils in acute damage. Histology cannot identify the cause: patch testing does.

    Clinical features

    • Both look eczematous: erythema, vesicles, scale, fissures, lichenification.
    • ICD: burning/stinging often > itch; sharp borders early; interdigital “sentinel” wet-work dermatitis.
    • ACD: itch dominates; geometric patterns (glove, plaster, jewellery); may spread beyond contact.
    • Facial/cosmetic ACD: eyelids, hairline; airborne fragrance can affect exposed face.
    • Tattoo ink allergy: localised eczema or lichenoid reaction in a colour distribution.
    • Skin of colour: erythema less obvious; violaceous pigment and lichenification more prominent.

    Distribution

    • Hands (wet work, gloves, occupational).
    • Face, eyelids and neck (cosmetics, airborne allergens, nail varnish).
    • Earlobes, wrists and umbilicus (nickel).
    • Scalp margin and face (hair dye PPD).
    • Feet (shoes: chromate, rubber).

    Diagnosis and investigations

    • History: occupation, hobbies, cosmetics, topical medicines, holiday improvement.
    • ICD: clinical exposure story; no confirmatory lab test.
    • Suspected ACD that is chronic, recurrent, occupational, facial or treatment-resistant → refer for patch testing (British baseline ± relevant series + patient’s own products).
    • Photopatch testing when sunscreen filters or photoallergens are suspected.
    • Prick testing / specific IgE is for immediate contact urticaria (e.g. latex), not delayed ACD.

    Management

    1. Identify and avoid the irritant/allergen — improvement can take weeks.
    2. Soap substitute + liberal emollient; cotton liners under gloves for wet work.
    3. Short topical corticosteroid matched to site and severity (face/flexures: mild–moderate).
    4. Refer for patch testing when indicated (CKS/BAD/PCDS).
    5. Occupational cases: fit-note/workplace adjustments; RIDDOR awareness is employer-led — IPs recognise and signpost.

    Caution

    Emollient cautions

    Do not use aqueous cream as a leave-on. Counsel paraffin fire risk and slippery bathroom floors.

    Note

    Pigmented contact dermatitis

    Grey-brown facial pigment from cosmetics/fragrances in darker skin — see contact & lichenoid pigmentation.

    Treatment ladder

    1. 1

      Every patient

      Identify and avoid the irritant or allergen. Soap substitute + liberal emollient.

    2. 2

      Calm the flare

      Short topical corticosteroid matched to site and severity. Face and flexures: mild to moderate potency.

    3. 3

      Find the allergen

      Refer for patch testing when indicated (CKS / BAD / PCDS).

    4. 4

      Occupational

      Fit note and workplace adjustments. RIDDOR reporting is employer-led: recognise and signpost.

    Caution

    Prescribing limits and pitfalls

    • Aqueous cream: Not as a leave-on emollient.
    • Paraffin emollients: Fire risk on fabrics; slippery floors.
    • Facial pigment: Grey-brown pigment from cosmetics in darker skin: see contact & lichenoid pigmentation.

    Referral

    • Urgent

      Severe facial or eyelid swelling, or systemic contact dermatitis.

    • Routine

      Occupational disease threatening livelihood: early patch testing and workplace advice.

    • Routine

      Suspected airborne allergy continuing despite stopping perfume.

    • Routine

      Not healing on treatment: think allergy to the medicament itself.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Occupational disease threatening livelihood — early patch testing and workplace advice.
    • Severe facial/eyelid swelling or systemic contact dermatitis.
    • Suspected airborne allergy continuing despite “stopping perfume”.
    • Non-healing dermatitis on medicaments — think contact allergy to the treatment itself.

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Chronic hand dermatitis with loss of work; secondary bacterial infection.
    • Severe facial and scalp swelling after PPD hair dye.
    • Spread (autosensitisation) and erythroderma (rare).

    Prognosis

    • ICD improves when exposure stops, but barrier recovery takes weeks to months.
    • ACD is lifelong: avoidance prevents recurrence; occupational ACD can persist even after avoidance.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • Many “allergies” on the hands are irritant — still treat the barrier aggressively.

    • Atopics get ICD more easily and can still develop ACD.

    • Hair-dye (PPD) and nail-varnish eyelid patterns are classic teaching cases.

    • Always check BNF/SmPC for topical steroid site limits.

    Self-check quiz

    5 questions · optional
    1. Q1Irritant or allergic: which needs prior sensitisation?Reveal

      Allergic contact dermatitis (type IV). Irritant needs no sensitisation and is dose-dependent.

    2. Q2When does allergic contact dermatitis appear after re-exposure?Reveal

      Typically 24–72 hours.

    3. Q3Which test confirms allergic contact dermatitis?Reveal

      Patch testing (prick tests / IgE are for immediate contact urticaria).

    4. Q4Who should be referred for patch testing?Reveal

      Chronic, recurrent, occupational, facial or treatment-resistant suspected allergic contact dermatitis.

    5. Q5A rash worsens under a cream you prescribed. Think?Reveal

      Contact allergy to the medicament itself (e.g. topical antibiotic or steroid).
