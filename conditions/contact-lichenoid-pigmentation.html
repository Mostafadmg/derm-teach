    Pigment & colour change

    Contact & lichenoid pigmentation

    Module 6 · Week 3

    Grey-brown facial or flexural pigment from contact allergens (Riehl/PCD) or lichenoid interface dermatitis (LPP, ashy dermatosis). Allergen avoidance and patch testing are key.

    CoversRiehl melanosispigmented contact dermatitislichen planus pigmentosusashy dermatosiserythema dyschromicum perstanspatch testing
    Think of it when

    Slow grey-brown reticulate facial/neck pigment in darker skin, sometimes with brief itchy flares after cosmetics or fragrances.

    What drives it

    Type IV hypersensitivity or lichenoid basal-layer attack → pigment incontinence (dermal melanophages).

    Backbone of care

    Stop fragrances/essential oils/non-essential cosmetics; fragrance-free regimen; refer for patch (± photopatch) testing.

    Never miss

    Do not keep borrowing potent facial steroids — they mask dermatitis and damage the face.

    What it is

    Pigmented contact dermatitis (PCD) / Riehl melanosis presents mainly as grey-brown to slate facial and neck pigment with little visible eczema, typically in Fitzpatrick IV–VI skin, often linked to cosmetics (“pigmented cosmetic dermatitis”).

    Lichen planus pigmentosus (LPP) and erythema dyschromicum perstans (ashy dermatosis) are related acquired dermal macular hyperpigmentation patterns with lichenoid histology. Overlap is common; specialist diagnosis matters.

    2

    How it’s caused

    • PCD: repeated low-dose allergens (fragrances, essential oils, PPD hair dye, kumkum/bindi dyes, textile dyes, preservatives) → interface dermatitis → dermal pigment.
    • LPP: T-cell lichenoid attack; triggers may include UV, hair dyes, oils; association with frontal fibrosing alopecia — check the hairline.
    • Ashy dermatosis: idiopathic ash-grey ovals on trunk/proximal limbs.
    3

    Clinical features

    • Gradual asymptomatic pigment; brief red/itchy flares after product use.
    • Forehead, temples, hairline, lateral cheeks, neck; may spare nose and perioral skin in cosmetic patterns.
    • LPP: face, neck, flexures (LPP inversus); slate-grey macules.
    • Ashy dermatosis: oval ash-grey patches on trunk.
    4

    Diagnosis

    • Detailed cosmetic/fragrance/hair-dye/textile history.
    • Patch testing (British baseline + cosmetic/fragrance/hair-dye + patient’s own products) at a contact clinic; readings day 2 and 3–4 (± day 7).
    • Photopatch testing when sunscreen filters, NSAIDs or photoallergens are suspected.
    • Biopsy when LPP / ashy dermatosis / ochronosis need separating.

    6

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    • Allergen avoidance first: stop non-essential cosmetics, fragrances and essential oils; bland fragrance-free cleanser, emollient and mineral sunscreen.
    • Refer for patch (± photopatch) testing — the key next step.
    • Short mild/moderate topical steroid or topical calcineurin inhibitor for itchy flares only (avoid potent facial steroids).
    • Specialist lightening and lasers are slow; pigment may take many months to fade after avoidance.
    7

    Red flags & referral

    • Ongoing potent facial steroid from borrowed/imported creams.
    • Diagnostic uncertainty — refer before escalating lighteners.
    • Frontal fibrosing alopecia signs with facial pigment — dermatology.
    8

    Prescriber pearls

    • Ask about borrowed steroid creams and imported cosmetics.
    • Antihistamines do not block patch-test readings (type IV).
    • Pharmacists can help patients find fragrance-free, patch-test–compatible products.
    • Cross-link atopic/contact eczema pathways: eczema / dermatitis.
