    Cosmetic & aesthetic

    Cosmetic procedures: peels, needling & lasers

    A counselling guide to peel depths, microneedling and laser classes, and an evidence-based view on procedures during or after isotretinoin (the old blanket 6-month wait is outdated).

    Module 6 · Week 6 · 4 images

    Also coverschemical peelmicroneedlingskin needlingcollagen inductionlaserfractional laserablative laserpulsed dye laserPDLisotretinoin and procedures6-month rulefrosting
    Think of it when

    A patient asking about peels, needling or laser for scars, pigment or ageing, or a patient on isotretinoin asking ‘can I have this done?’

    What drives it

    Controlled injury (chemical, mechanical or thermal) leading to re-epithelialisation and collagen remodelling.

    Backbone of care

    Match depth and risk to the goal and skin type, refer to trained operators, and counsel on SPF and aftercare.

    Never miss

    PIH and keloid risk in skin of colour, HSV reactivation, and unregulated providers. Do not ‘clear’ isotretinoin patients yourself.

    01

    What it is

    Note

    Scope: counselling and recognition

    For the pharmacist/IP, this page covers recognising types, setting expectations, safety and referral. It is not a procedure how-to (no injection technique, laser settings or peel recipes).

    ProcedureWhat it is
    Chemical peelControlled chemical injury to the epidermis ± dermis to resurface skin (photoageing, pigment, fine lines, acne, superficial scars)
    MicroneedlingCollagen induction therapy: fine needles (professional about 0.5–3 mm; home devices much shorter) create micro-injuries that trigger new collagen
    LasersSelective photothermolysis: the wavelength targets a chromophore (water, haemoglobin or melanin)
    02

    How it’s caused

    Each procedure injures the skin in a controlled way, triggering inflammation, re-epithelialisation and collagen remodelling. ‘Frosting’ (whitening) with some peel agents, such as TCA, reflects protein coagulation and indicates depth. This is for recognition only.

    Microneedling microchannels close within minutes and the epidermis is largely preserved, so it often suits darker skin better than ablative options.

    04

    Diagnosis

    There is no disease to diagnose. Instead, the pre-procedure assessment covers: Fitzpatrick type, HSV history, isotretinoin status, keloid tendency, pregnancy, sun exposure, active acne or infection, and realistic goals.

    05

    Differentials

    Isotretinoin and procedures: the evidence

    The old rule was to delay elective procedures for about 6 months after isotretinoin, because of theoretical impaired healing and scarring. A task force review (Mysore et al., Standard Guidelines of Care), cited in Module 6, found insufficient evidence to support a blanket delay for many procedures.

    Generally considered acceptable (in experienced hands)Still a higher-risk discussion
    Laser hair removal; fractional lasers; pigment lasers; fractional RF microneedling; superficial and medium peels; microdermabrasion; dermaroller; biopsies; superficial excisionsDeep phenol peels; fully ablative resurfacing; mechanical dermabrasion
    06

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    Pharmacist / IP
    • Do not perform medium or deep peels or procedures without the appropriate training, governance and insurance.
    • Isotretinoin: do not clear patients for procedures yourself. Document isotretinoin use and refer the decision to the operating clinician. Some UK centres remain conservative. Continue standard isotretinoin safety (pregnancy prevention programme, blood tests, mucocutaneous effects).
    • Aftercare counselling: gentle cleansing, SPF, moisturiser, no picking, and expect temporary redness, peeling or swelling.
    • Antiviral prophylaxis may be advised by the operator if there is a history of HSV.
    • Home microneedling rollers: warn about infection and poor hygiene or quality.

    Adverse effects to recognise

    • Infection (bacterial, candida, HSV reactivation)
    • PIH or hypopigmentation (especially darker skin), scarring, burns, blistering
    • Acne flare, persistent redness, granulomatous reactions (products driven into the skin with needling)
    • Paradoxical hypertrichosis (hair lasers)
    07

    Red flags & referral

    Escalate
    • Pain, spreading redness, pus or fever after a procedure: infection.
    • Grouped vesicles after a facial procedure: HSV, needing prompt antivirals.
    • Blistering, burns or dyspigmentation: refer back to the operator or dermatology.
    • Deep peels: systemic toxicity risk. Specialist settings only.
    08

    Prescriber pearls

    • More ablative is not always better. Match downtime and risk to the goal and skin type.
    • Darker skin: start cautiously, as there is a higher risk of dyspigmentation and keloid.
    • Pregnancy: most elective peels and lasers are deferred.
    • Advise patients to check the provider’s qualifications and insurance.
    • Patient phrase: “Many modern guidelines no longer insist on a blanket 6-month wait for milder procedures, but your dermatologist or cosmetic clinician decides case by case.”
