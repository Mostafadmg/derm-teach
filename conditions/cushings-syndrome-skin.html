    Cushing’s syndrome: skin signs

    Wide purple striae, thin bruising skin, a round red face and central weight gain from too much cortisol, most often from steroid medicines (oral, inhaled, topical or injected).

    Also calledstriaemoon facesteroid excessiatrogenic Cushing’spurple striaebuffalo hump

    Key facts

    • Wide purple striae, easy bruising, thin skin, moon face, acne and central weight gain, often in someone on steroids.
    • Excess glucocorticoid breaks down collagen and redistributes fat; usually iatrogenic, sometimes pituitary, adrenal or ectopic ACTH.
    • Total steroid review (all routes); if not iatrogenic, overnight dexamethasone suppression test or late-night salivary cortisol and endocrine referral; never stop long-term steroids abruptly.
    • Adrenal crisis from stopping steroids suddenly; hypertension, diabetes, osteoporosis; ectopic ACTH from lung cancer.

    Overview

    The clinical state caused by prolonged glucocorticoid excess.

    • Exogenous (iatrogenic) — by far the commonest: oral, inhaled, injected, potent topical steroids, and unlicensed skin-lightening creams (link to the therapeutics notes see Dermatomyositis).
    • Endogenous (~2–3 per million/year): ACTH-dependent (~80%): pituitary adenoma = Cushing's disease (~70%) or ectopic ACTH (small-cell lung cancer, carcinoid); ACTH-independent: adrenal adenoma/carcinoma, bilateral adrenal hyperplasia

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    How it is caused

    Cortisol is catabolic to protein → thin skin, striae, bruising, proximal myopathy, osteoporosis. It causes insulin resistance → diabetes and central fat redistribution (moon face, dorsocervical and supraclavicular fat pads). Mineralocorticoid effect → hypertension, hypokalaemia. Gonadotrophin suppression and adrenal androgens → amenorrhoea/oligomenorrhoea, hirsutism, acne. Immunosuppression → infections, poor healing.

    Clinical features

    Clinical features & sites

    • Most discriminating features (Endocrine Society): easy bruising, facial plethora, proximal myopathy, and wide (>1 cm), purple/"livid" striae on the abdomen, flanks, axillae and thighs.
    • Others: moon face, dorsocervical "buffalo hump", supraclavicular fat pads, central obesity with thin limbs, thin skin with purpura, acne, hirsutism, acanthosis nigricans, fungal infections (tinea versicolor), poor wound healing, oedema, depression/psychosis, insomnia, kidney stones, osteoporotic fractures. Children: weight gain with slowed growth.
    • The case: a 40-year-old woman with abdominal striae, amenorrhoea, weight gain and BP 142/92 → most likely Cushing's syndrome (after excluding exogenous steroids). Why: wide purple striae + menstrual disturbance + hypertension + weight gain is a combination of discriminatory and common features; at 40 (not pregnant, no recent large weight change) striae should not be dismissed as physiological.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    • First, exclude exogenous glucocorticoids: prescribed, OTC (hydrocortisone creams), inhaled/nasal, joint injections, skin-lightening products, herbal remedies adulterated with dexamethasone. Ritonavir/cobicistat + inhaled/nasal fluticasone or budesonide (CYP3A4) → iatrogenic Cushing's — a key pharmacist check.
    • Bedside/clinical tests: BP; capillary glucose/urinalysis for glycosuria; weight, BMI and waist; Proximal muscle strength — rise from a chair or squat without using the arms; climb a step; Skin thickness (skin fold on the back of the hand <2 mm — Liddle's sign); look for bruises, striae width and colour, plethora, fat pads; Visual fields (pituitary macroadenoma — bitemporal hemianopia), mood, and signs of infection
    • Single primary-care investigation: Course teaching suggests 24-h urinary free cortisol (≥2 collections), or late-night salivary cortisol (≥2) where available; UK practice: the 1 mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test (dexamethasone 1 mg at 23:00, serum cortisol at 08:00–09:00; normal suppression <50 nmol/L) is the most commonly used first-line screen. Many ICB pathways advise referring to endocrinology for testing rather than testing in primary care — follow the local pathway; Pitfalls: oestrogen-containing COCs raise CBG (false-positive DST — stop the COC 6 weeks before or use UFC/salivary cortisol); enzyme inducers (carbamazepine, phenytoin, rifampicin) speed dexamethasone clearance (false positive); pseudo-Cushing's states (alcohol, depression, severe obesity, poorly controlled diabetes)
    • Secondary care: ACTH (dependent vs independent), high-dose DST/CRH test, pituitary MRI, inferior petrosal sinus sampling, adrenal CT, K⁺, HbA1c, DEXA.

    Differential diagnoses

    Physiological striae (pregnancy, adolescence, rapid weight gain/bodybuilding — usually narrower, pink then silvery-white), iatrogenic Cushing's (topical/inhaled/oral steroids), metabolic syndrome/obesity, PCOS (oligomenorrhoea, hirsutism, acne, AN — no myopathy/bruising), pseudo-Cushing's (alcohol, depression), hypothyroidism, Marfan/Ehlers–Danlos (striae).

    Management

    • Refer to endocrinology (urgently if hypokalaemia, severe hypertension, rapid progression, psychosis or suspected ectopic ACTH/adrenal carcinoma).
    • Iatrogenic Cushing's: Do not stop steroids abruptly — the HPA axis is suppressed. Taper to the lowest effective dose; use steroid-sparing therapy; Steroid Emergency Card and sick-day advice (NatPSA 2020; NICE NG243); Bone protection; glucose and BP monitoring; Review inhaled/nasal steroid + CYP3A4 inhibitor combinations
    • Meanwhile, manage the comorbidities: BP (NICE NG136), diabetes, VTE risk (Cushing's is prothrombotic), osteoporosis, infection, mood.
    • Definitive treatment (specialist): transsphenoidal surgery, adrenalectomy, or medical therapy (metyrapone, osilodrostat, ketoconazole — interaction-heavy; levoketoconazole), radiotherapy.

    IP scope

    History and medicine review (crucial — iatrogenic causes), bedside assessment, bloods/first-line screen per local pathway, steroid-taper planning and emergency cards, comorbidity management, and referral.

    Referral

    • 999 / A&E

      Never stop long-term systemic steroids suddenly: taper, issue a steroid emergency card and sick-day rules.

    • Urgent

      Cushingoid features without steroid exposure, especially with rapid onset, weakness or low potassium: urgent endocrine referral.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Never stop long-term systemic steroids suddenly: taper, issue a steroid emergency card and sick-day rules.
    • Cushingoid features without steroid exposure, especially with rapid onset, weakness or low potassium: urgent endocrine referral.

    Red flags / refer

    Rapid onset with hypokalaemia, hyperpigmentation, muscle wasting (ectopic ACTH — urgent); virilisation (adrenal carcinoma); visual field loss; severe hypertension, psychosis or fractures.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • "Before you test for Cushing's, take the steroid history — every route."

    • "Wide, purple, and with weakness or bruising — not just stretch marks."

    • Fluticasone (inhaled/nasal) + ritonavir = a classic, preventable cause.
