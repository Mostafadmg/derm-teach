    Cutaneous amyloidosis (macular and lichen)

    Localised keratin-derived amyloid in the papillary dermis: rippled grey-brown patches on the upper back (macular) or very itchy firm papules on the shins (lichen), driven by chronic friction and scratching.

    Also calledmacular amyloidosislichen amyloidosisprimary localised cutaneous amyloidosisPLCAfriction amyloidosisamyloid
    Related pagesPhytophotodermatitisLichen planusContact & lichenoid pigmentationAcanthosis nigricans

    Key facts

    • Primary localised cutaneous amyloidosis: amyloid made from damaged keratinocytes builds up in the papillary dermis; it is not systemic amyloidosis.
    • Two common forms: macular (rippled grey-brown patches, upper back) and lichen (very itchy firm papules, shins); they coexist in about a quarter of cases.
    • Most common in people of Asian, Middle Eastern and South American descent with skin types IV–V.
    • Driven by chronic friction and scratching (nylon scrubs, brushes, notalgia paraesthetica); about 10% are familial.
    • Clinical diagnosis ± biopsy (Congo red, apple-green birefringence). Stop friction, potent steroid ± occlusion, treat itch; refer if refractory.

    Overview

    Cutaneous amyloidosis is a group of conditions in which amyloid (abnormally folded protein) is deposited in the skin. In the common primary localised forms, the amyloid comes from keratin released by damaged keratinocytes, and it stays in the skin.

    • Macular amyloidosis: itchy or symptomless, grey-brown, rippled (reticulate) pigmentation, usually between the shoulder blades.
    • Lichen amyloidosis: intensely itchy, firm, brown, scaly papules on the shins, often in rows, merging into plaques.
    • Biphasic amyloidosis: both forms together.
    • Rare forms (nodular amyloidosis, which can link to systemic disease) and systemic amyloidosis are different conditions needing specialist assessment.

    Epidemiology

    • Ethnicity and skin type
      • Most common in people from Asia, the Middle East and South America, and in skin types IV–V.
    • Age and sex
      • Usually adults; macular amyloidosis is more common in women; lichen amyloidosis is more common in men aged 50–60.
    • Family history
      • Up to 10% are familial (autosomal dominant, OSMR or IL31RA genes, part of the itch pathway).
    • Overlap
      • Macular and lichen amyloidosis coexist in about 25%.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    Chronic friction and scratching damage keratinocytes; their keratin is processed into amyloid in the upper dermis.

    Causes and associations

    • Friction
      • Nylon towels, bath brushes, loofahs, back scrubbers and rough clothing ("friction amyloidosis").
    • Chronic itch
      • Notalgia paraesthetica (itchy patch below the shoulder blade) often leads to macular amyloidosis.
      • Atopic dermatitis and lichen simplex are linked with lichen amyloidosis.
    • Genetic
      • Familial forms (OSMR, IL31RA); rarely multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2A (RET mutations) with interscapular amyloidosis.
    • Sun exposure and darker skin types are risk factors.
    • Occasionally associated with autoimmune conditions.

    Myths to address

    • "It's dirt, so scrub harder": scrubbing is the cause and makes it worse.
    • "Amyloid in the skin means organ disease": localised cutaneous amyloidosis is not systemic amyloidosis.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    1. 1

      Friction and scratching injure keratinocytes

      Repeated rubbing or scratching damages basal keratinocytes, which undergo apoptosis.

    2. 2

      Keratin becomes amyloid

      Keratin filaments from dying cells form colloid (keratin) bodies that drop into the papillary dermis, where macrophages and fibroblasts modify them into amyloid.

    3. 3

      Deposits and pigment build up

      Small globules of amyloid collect in the dermal papillae with melanin-laden macrophages, giving the grey-brown colour. In lichen amyloidosis the epidermis thickens over them, forming papules.

    4. 4

      Itch keeps the cycle going

      Amyloid and the thickened skin itch, so scratching continues and more amyloid forms. Faults in the OSMR / IL-31 itch pathway explain familial cases.

    Histopathology

    • Small globules of pink amorphous amyloid in the dermal papillae, just beneath the epidermis.
    • Pigment incontinence with melanophages.
    • Lichen amyloidosis: acanthosis and hyperkeratosis over the deposits.
    • Congo red stains amyloid, with apple-green birefringence in polarised light; crystal violet and thioflavin T also help; immunostaining shows keratin origin.
    • Deposits can be sparse, so multiple sections or a deeper biopsy may be needed.

    Clinical features

    Symptoms

    • Itch: mild or absent in macular amyloidosis, often severe and persistent in lichen amyloidosis.
    • Cosmetic concern about pigmentation.

    Lesions and morphology

    • Rippled grey-brown macules

      Small dusky macules merge into a rippled or reticulate pattern.

    • Interscapular patch

      Ill-defined pigmentation between the shoulder blades.

    • Firm itchy papules

      Tan-brown, scaly, dome-shaped papules, often in rows, on the shins.

    • Confluent plaques

      Papules merge into thickened, ridged plaques.

    Distribution

    • Macular: interscapular back, upper chest, shoulders and outer upper arms.
    • Lichen: shins, then thighs, forearms and the backs of the hands.

    Variants and subtypes

    • Macular amyloidosis

      Rippled grey-brown patches, upper back; women; friction.

    • Notalgia-linked macular amyloidosis

      Pigmented patch below one shoulder blade from chronic scratching of notalgia paraesthetica.

    • Lichen amyloidosis

      Intensely itchy papules on the shins; atopy.

    • Biphasic

      Both forms together in about a quarter.

    • Nodular amyloidosis

      Waxy nodules; light-chain amyloid; may relate to plasma cell disorders: specialist work-up.

    Special groups

    • Skin of colour
      • The most affected group; pigmentation is often the main concern and can be confused with post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation.
    • Families
      • Ask about relatives; consider MEN2A in familial interscapular amyloidosis (specialist).

    Diagnosis and investigations

    Diagnosis is usually clinical from the rippled pattern, site and history of friction or itch.

    History

    • Scrubbing habits, brushes, towels; chronic itch; atopy; family history.
    • Systemic symptoms (to exclude systemic amyloidosis): tiredness, weight loss, purpura, enlarged tongue, swelling, frothy urine.

    Investigations

    • Dermoscopy: a central white or brown hub with surrounding pigment.
    • Skin biopsy with Congo red when the diagnosis is uncertain.
    • If systemic or nodular amyloidosis is suspected: specialist investigations (serum and urine protein electrophoresis, free light chains).

    Differential diagnoses

    PhotoConditionDistinguishing features
    		Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentationFollows a known rash; no ripple; no papules.

    Management

    Principles

    • Break the itch–scratch–friction cycle; set realistic expectations: pigment improves slowly and recurs if friction restarts.
    • Treatment is often difficult; many options are specialist and evidence is limited.

    Conservative and self-care

    • Stop scrubbing: no loofahs, brushes or nylon towels; soft, loose clothing.
    • Regular emollients; keep nails short.
    • Sun protection for pigmentation.

    Medical treatment

    Potent topical corticosteroid

    Anti-itch, anti-inflammatory
    Use
    Itch and lichen amyloidosis; occlusion improves effect.
    Cautions
    Limit courses and monitor for atrophy, especially under occlusion.

    Topical calcineurin inhibitor

    Steroid-sparing (off-label)
    Use
    Itch where long-term steroid is unwanted.

    Sedating antihistamine at night

    Sleep
    Use
    To help sleep when itch is severe; does not treat the itch mechanism.

    Specialist options

    DermatologySpecialist only
    Use
    Intralesional steroid, topical capsaicin or DMSO, phototherapy (NB-UVB, PUVA), oral acitretin, laser or dermabrasion for lichen amyloidosis.

    Treatment ladder

    1. 1

      Everyone

      Stop friction, emollients, explanation.

    2. 2

      Itchy or papular

      Potent topical steroid ± occlusion, or calcineurin inhibitor; night sedating antihistamine if sleep is affected.

    3. 3

      Refractory

      Refer: phototherapy, retinoids, procedures.

    Referral

    • Urgent

      Features of systemic amyloidosis or nodular amyloidosis.

    • Routine

      Severe or refractory itch, diagnostic doubt (biopsy), or for specialist treatments.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Urgent

      Purpura (especially around the eyes), enlarged tongue, nerve symptoms, heart or kidney symptoms: possible systemic amyloidosis.

    • Routine

      Waxy nodules (nodular amyloidosis): screen for plasma cell disease.

    Complications and prognosis

    • Persistent itch, sleep disturbance and lichenification.
    • Long-lasting cosmetic pigmentation and distress.
    • Steroid side effects with long courses under occlusion.

    Prognosis

    • Chronic and benign; it does not progress to systemic amyloidosis.
    • Improves slowly once friction and scratching stop; tends to recur.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • Ask about back scrubbers, brushes and nylon towels: stopping them is the key treatment.

    • Rippled interscapular pigmentation in skin of colour is often macular amyloidosis, not dirt or PIH.

    • An itchy patch under one shoulder blade suggests notalgia paraesthetica.

    • Potent steroid under occlusion helps lichen amyloidosis, but time-limit courses.

    • Localised cutaneous amyloid is not systemic amyloidosis; refer if systemic features appear.

    Self-check quiz

    6 questions · optional
    1. Q1Where does the amyloid in macular and lichen amyloidosis come from?Reveal

      Keratin from damaged keratinocytes.

    2. Q2Typical site of macular amyloidosis?Reveal

      Between the shoulder blades (interscapular back).

    3. Q3Typical site of lichen amyloidosis?Reveal

      Shins.

    4. Q4Which stain confirms amyloid?Reveal

      Congo red, with apple-green birefringence under polarised light.

    5. Q5Key self-care advice?Reveal

      Stop scrubbing with brushes, loofahs or nylon towels.

    6. Q6Does it progress to systemic amyloidosis?Reveal

      No; systemic features need separate specialist assessment.
