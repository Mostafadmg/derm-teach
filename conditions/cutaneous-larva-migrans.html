    Cutaneous larva migrans

    Module 5 · Week 4

    An intensely itchy, red, snake-like track that moves a few millimetres to centimetres a day, usually on the feet after walking barefoot on a tropical beach. Treat with oral ivermectin or albendazole.

    CoversCLMcreeping eruptionhookworm rashbeach rashserpiginous trackivermectinalbendazole
    Think of it when

    A raised, wandering, itchy serpiginous line on the foot, buttock or hand after tropical beach or soil exposure.

    What drives it

    Animal (dog or cat) hookworm larvae burrow into human skin but cannot complete their life cycle, so wander in the epidermis.

    Backbone of care

    Single-dose oral ivermectin or a 3-day course of albendazole (unlicensed; specialist or tropical-medicine advice); antihistamine for itch.

    Never miss

    Secondary bacterial infection, and other migratory larvae (larva currens, Strongyloides) in the immunosuppressed.

    What it is

    Cutaneous larva migrans (CLM), also called hookworm-related CLM or "creeping eruption", is a self-limiting skin infestation. Animal hookworm larvae (mostly from dogs and cats) burrow into the epidermis and wander there, leaving an intensely itchy, serpiginous track. It is one of the most common dermatoses in travellers returning from tropical beaches (Caribbean, Central/South America, South-East Asia, Africa). Occasional UK-acquired cases have been reported in gardeners and runners.

    2

    How it’s caused

    • Usual causes are Ancylostoma braziliense (dog/cat hookworm in the Americas and Caribbean) and A. caninum, plus Uncinaria stenocephala (European dog hookworm) and Bunostomum phlebotomum (cattle).
    • Transmission (Case 2 Q2): infected animals pass eggs in their faeces onto warm, moist, sandy soil or beaches. The eggs hatch into infective filariform larvae, which penetrate human skin through follicles, cracks or intact skin when someone walks barefoot, sits or lies on sand, or plays in sandpits.
    • Humans are an accidental "dead-end" host. The larvae lack the enzymes to cross the basement membrane, so they stay in the epidermis and cannot complete their life cycle. They die within weeks to months. The track is an inflammatory/allergic reaction to the moving larva and its secretions.
    • Not spread person to person.
    3

    Clinical features

    • There may be tingling or prickling at the entry site within hours. The track appears days to a few weeks later. The larvae can lie dormant first.
    • The track is 2–3 mm wide, raised, serpiginous ("snake-like") and red or skin-coloured (on darker skin it can look hypochromic or pigmented, as in Case 2). It is intensely itchy and moves a few mm to a few cm a day. The larva usually lies 1–2 cm beyond the visible end of the track.
    • Sites: feet (soles, toe webs, dorsum) most often, then hands (sand play, as in Case 2), buttocks, knees and trunk (sunbathing).
    • Variants: several tracks or loops, vesicles and bullae, and hookworm folliculitis (itchy follicular papulopustules, often on the buttocks). Look for eczematised or impetiginised skin from scratching.
    • The patient is systemically well. Rarely there is eosinophilia, or (with heavy infestation) Löffler-type pulmonary infiltrates.
    4

    Diagnosis

    • The diagnosis is clinical: the typical migrating serpiginous itchy track plus barefoot exposure in a warm-climate setting.
    • No routine tests are needed. A biopsy is usually unhelpful because the larva is ahead of the track. Dermoscopy may show translucent segmental structures (the larval body) beyond the track.
    • Check for eosinophilia if the picture is atypical. Think of Strongyloides serology if the eruption is fast-moving, perianal/buttock or recurrent.

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    Primary care can manage most cases. It is self-limiting (usually 2–8 weeks), but treatment brings rapid relief and lowers the risk of infection.

    • Self-care / OTC (interim): reassure (not contagious, the larva will die). Relieve itch with a non-sedating oral antihistamine (or sedating at night for older children/adults per licence) and emollients. A mild–moderate topical steroid helps eczematous change (only once CLM is confidently diagnosed and not larva currens). Cut nails short and avoid scratching. Treat secondary impetigo per NICE NG153. Do not attempt cryotherapy: the larva is ahead of the track, it is painful and it does not work.
    • Specific treatment (prescriber, off-label in UK; check local formulary and ID advice): First line: ivermectin 200 micrograms/kg orally as a single dose (rounded to the nearest 3 mg tablet). Repeat the next day or after 1–2 weeks if it fails. In the UK this is usually unlicensed/imported (named-patient), but it is listed for CLM on several NHS formularies (e.g. SE London JMF) and in NHS Tayside guidance. Avoid in pregnancy and breastfeeding. Safety in children under 15 kg is not established; Alternative: albendazole 400 mg once daily for 3 days (up to 5–7 days for extensive disease or folliculitis). It is unlicensed for CLM in the UK and can be used in children over about 2 years. Take with fatty food. Avoid in pregnancy.; Topical option for single early tracks, or when oral drugs are unsuitable (young children, pregnancy, on specialist advice): 10–15% thiabendazole or albendazole ointment, applied three times daily for 1–2 weeks. These are extemporaneous or specialist products in the UK. Topical ivermectin 1% cream (licensed for rosacea) has been used off-label; Mebendazole is poorly absorbed and works less well. The UK OTC threadworm pack (single 100 mg dose) is not adequate for CLM, so do not sell it as a "cure"
    • Case 2 (a 6-year-old): if he weighs at least 15 kg, single-dose ivermectin or albendazole 400 mg daily for 3 days are both reasonable, prescribed off-label by a competent prescriber or via GP/paediatrics. Add an antihistamine. Itch usually settles within 24–48 hours, and tracks fade within about a week.
    • Refer / seek advice: diagnostic doubt; features of larva currens (fast, urticarial, perianal, recurrent, especially if steroids or immunosuppression are planned); widespread disease, folliculitis or treatment failure; pregnancy or a child under 15 kg; systemic symptoms (wheeze, eosinophilia).
    7

    Red flags & referral

    • Rapidly moving perianal or buttock tracks (larva currens) or a patient on steroids: think Strongyloides hyperinfection.
    • Spreading cellulitis from scratching.
    8

    Prescriber pearls

    • The tutor's prevention point: avoid walking barefoot on beaches and soil where dogs and cats roam. Wear shoes or sandals, lie on a mat or sunbed rather than directly on sand, and choose beaches that are tide-washed or animal-free. Supervise children in sandpits. Dry beach towels and clothing indoors rather than on sand.
    • Ivermectin tablets: give on an empty stomach with water (follow the product information). Counsel on mild transient GI upset and dizziness.
    • The two "larva" names: larva migrans creeps slowly from an animal hookworm and is a dead end in humans. Larva currens "runs" fast and comes from the human Strongyloides, which can persist for decades and disseminate if steroids are given.
