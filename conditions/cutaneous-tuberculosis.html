    Cutaneous tuberculosis (scrofuloderma & others)

    Skin TB is uncommon in the UK but important: a cold neck abscess or sinus over a lymph node (scrofuloderma), a slowly spreading reddish-brown plaque (lupus vulgaris), or warty or nodular forms. Refer to TB services; treatment follows NICE NG33.

    Step 1 of 9 · 1 min

    Overview

    Uncommon in UKCold neck abscessRefer to TB serviceNotifiable

    • Spot it

      A painless lump, sinus or scar over neck nodes, or a chronic plaque, in someone from a high-incidence area or with TB contact.

    • Why

      M. tuberculosis reaching skin from a deeper focus, by inoculation or blood spread; tuberculids are immune reactions.

    • Treat

      Refer to the TB service for biopsy, culture and treatment (NICE NG33); contact screening; HIV test.

    • Never miss

      Pulmonary or disseminated TB, HIV, and rifampicin interactions.

    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    What it is

    Skin infection by Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (rarely M. bovis / BCG). Uncommon in the UK but seen in people from high-incidence countries, the immunosuppressed (HIV, anti-TNF, steroids), homeless people and those with prior TB contact. Scrofuloderma is the commonest form in many series: TB of an underlying structure (usually cervical lymph node, also bone/joint/epididymis) breaks through to the skin. Case 1 is a classic scrofuloderma with active pulmonary TB.

    Start: Why it happens
    2

    Step 2 of 9 · 1 min

    Why it happens

    Route and immunity decide the form.

    • Scrofuloderma (commonest): TB in a node, bone or joint breaks through to skin (contiguous spread).
    • Lupus vulgaris: blood / lymph spread, good immunity, few bacilli.
    • TB verrucosa cutis: re-inoculation in a sensitised person (‘prosector’s wart’).
    • Miliary skin TB / gumma: poor immunity (HIV, infants).
    • Tuberculids (erythema induratum, papulonecrotic): hypersensitivity, no bacilli in the lesion.
    • At risk: high-incidence countries, HIV, anti-TNF, steroids, homelessness, TB contacts.

    How it’s caused

    Classify by route and host immunity:

    RouteFormHost immunity / bacillary load
    Exogenous — first inoculation (non-immune)Tuberculous chancre (primary inoculation TB)Naïve → multibacillary, becomes paucibacillary as immunity develops
    Exogenous — re-inoculation (sensitised)TB verrucosa cutis (warty TB; "prosector's wart")Good immunity, paucibacillary
    Endogenous — contiguous spreadScrofuloderma (node/bone → skin); periorificial TB (autoinoculation at mouth/anus)Scrofuloderma: moderate/variable, multibacillary; periorificial: poor immunity, advanced internal TB
    Endogenous — haematogenous/lymphaticLupus vulgarisGood immunity (strongly Mantoux+), paucibacillary
    HaematogenousAcute miliary TB of skin; tuberculous gumma (metastatic abscess)Poor immunity (HIV, infants), multibacillary
    Hypersensitivity, no bacilli in lesionTuberculids: papulonecrotic tuberculid, lichen scrofulosorum, erythema induratum (Bazin)Strong immunity; TB focus elsewhere; PCR may detect DNA; resolve with anti-TB therapy

    Mechanism in Case 1: pulmonary TB → lymphatic spread to cervical nodes → caseating necrosis → cold abscess → direct (contiguous) extension through overlying dermis → nodules, ulcers, sinuses.

    Next: What you’ll see
    3

    Step 3 of 9 · 2 min

    What you’ll see

    Cold, painless and slow.

    • Scrofuloderma: firm painless nodules over the neck (also axilla, groin), skin-coloured then dusky red-blue; matted ‘cold’ nodes.
    • Nodules soften and discharge → undermined ulcers, sinus tracts, cord-like puckered scars.
    • Lupus vulgaris: slowly enlarging red-brown plaque on head / neck; ‘apple-jelly’ on diascopy; SCC risk.
    • Systemic: cough, weight loss, night sweats, fever.

    Clinical features

    • Scrofuloderma: firm, painless, initially skin-coloured then dusky red/blue subcutaneous nodules over the neck (submandibular, supraclavicular, anterior triangle), axilla, groin, chest wall; nodes matted, non-tender ("cold"). Nodules soften, discharge cheesy/watery material, form undermined ulcers and sinus tracts, healing slowly with cord-like, puckered scars and skin bridges.
    • Systemic: chronic cough, weight loss, night sweats, fever (Case 1).
    • Lupus vulgaris: slowly enlarging red-brown plaque on head/neck; diascopy shows "apple-jelly" nodules; scarring, tissue destruction; risk of SCC in long-standing lesions.
    • TB verrucosa cutis: warty plaque on hands, knees, buttocks.
    • Tuberculids: crops of necrotic papules on extensor limbs (papulonecrotic); tiny lichenoid follicular papules on trunk in children (lichen scrofulosorum); tender violaceous nodules on posterior calves of women that may ulcerate (erythema induratum).
    Next: Confirm the diagnosis
    4

    Step 4 of 9 · 1 min

    Confirm the diagnosis

    Refer

    • TB service + dermatology for biopsy, ZN stain, culture, PCR.

    Screen

    • CXR, HIV test, IGRA / Mantoux as directed.

    Infection control

    • Cough? Mask, avoid waiting rooms, same-day advice.
    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    Diagnosis

    Specialist-led (dermatology + TB/respiratory/ID):

    • Skin/node biopsy: caseating granulomas; Ziehl–Neelsen stain for AFB; mycobacterial culture (gold standard, weeks; gives drug sensitivity); TB PCR / GeneXpert (rapid, rifampicin resistance).
    • Node FNA/excision; sputum smear/culture (AFB+ in Case 1); chest X-ray.
    • Mantoux / IGRA support infection but do not separate latent from active disease (may be false-negative in HIV/miliary TB).
    • HIV test for every patient with TB (NICE NG33); also hepatitis B/C, LFTs, renal function, visual acuity (ethambutol) baselines.

    Next: Treat
    6

    Step 6 of 9 · 1 min

    Treat

    Not managed in primary care.

    Treatment ladder

    1. 1

      FoundationRefer

      • Urgent TB service referral; TB is notifiable; contact tracing via the TB team.
    2. 2

      SpecialistStandard regimen (specialist)

      • 2 months RHZE then 4 months RH (NICE NG33); longer for CNS / bone or resistance.
      • DOT if adherence risk; pyridoxine with isoniazid if at risk.
    3. 3

      Step 1Your role

      • Interaction check for rifampicin: contraception, warfarin / DOACs, antiretrovirals, antiepileptics, methadone, steroids, azoles.
      • Counsel: orange urine and tears (stains soft lenses); report jaundice, dark urine, vomiting; ethambutol → report colour-vision change.

    Safety limits

    Contraception

    Rifampicin defeats combined hormonal contraception: non-hormonal / IUD (FSRH).

    Don’t

    Repeatedly treat a chronic neck sinus with flucloxacillin.

    Doses as in the source notes. Always confirm in the current BNF and local formulary.

    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    • Not managed in primary care — refer urgently to the local TB service (respiratory/ID) and dermatology; suspected pulmonary TB with cough needs same-day advice and infection-control (mask, avoid waiting rooms).
    • Standard regimen (NICE NG33, specialist-initiated): 2 months RHZE (rifampicin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, ethambutol) then 4 months RH (longer for CNS/bone, drug resistance). Directly observed therapy where adherence risk. Pyridoxine with isoniazid in at-risk patients.
    • TB is a notifiable disease (the diagnosing clinician notifies UKHSA); contact tracing via the TB service.
    • Surgery (excision/drainage) is adjunctive only.

    Step 7 of 9 · 1 min

    Red flags & referral

    • Same day

      Cough, haemoptysis, weight loss, night sweats: urgent chest assessment with infection control.

    • Urgent

      Chronic painless neck lump or sinus, especially from a high-incidence country: TB until proven otherwise.

    • Routine

      Notify TB to UKHSA.

    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    Red flags & referral

    • Cough, weight loss, night sweats or haemoptysis: urgent chest assessment.
    • Rifampicin is a strong enzyme inducer: review every interacting drug (contraceptives, anticoagulants, antiretrovirals).
    • Notify TB to UKHSA.
    Next: Prescriber pearls
    8

    Step 8 of 9 · 1 min

    Prescriber pearls

    • Rifampicin is a potent enzyme inducer: run the interaction checker.

    • Ethambutol: any visual change → report immediately.

    • Tuberculids clear only when the hidden TB focus is treated.

    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    Prescriber pearls

    • Interaction checker is essential: rifampicin is a potent enzyme inducer — reduces efficacy of combined hormonal contraception (advise non-hormonal/IUD/medroxyprogesterone with adjustment per FSRH), warfarin/DOACs, antiretrovirals, antiepileptics, methadone, steroids, azoles. Orange urine/tears (stains soft contact lenses).
    • Hepatotoxicity (RHZ): counsel to report jaundice, dark urine, vomiting; avoid alcohol; LFT monitoring per specialist.
    • Ethambutol: report any visual change (colour vision) immediately.
    • A chronic painless neck lump/sinus, especially with cough, weight loss, or origin from a high-incidence country, is TB until proven otherwise — do not repeatedly treat with flucloxacillin.
    • Skin lesions heal with scarring; tuberculids clear only with anti-TB treatment of the hidden focus.

    Quick self-check

    1. Q1Commonest form of cutaneous TB in many series?Reveal

      Scrofuloderma (contiguous spread from a node).

    2. Q2Diascopy sign of lupus vulgaris?Reveal

      ‘Apple-jelly’ nodules.

    3. Q3Standard TB regimen?Reveal

      2 months rifampicin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, ethambutol, then 4 months rifampicin + isoniazid.

    4. Q4Key contraception issue on rifampicin?Reveal
