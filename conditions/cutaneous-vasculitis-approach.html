    Overview

    Module 1 · Week 4palpable purpuravessel sizeANCA

    • Spot it

      Palpable purpura, haemorrhagic blisters, nodules, livedo or ulcers, especially on the lower legs.

    • Why

      Inflammation of vessel walls from immune complexes, ANCA or infection; small vessels give purpura, medium vessels give nodules, livedo racemosa and ulcers.

    • Treat

      Urine dipstick and BP today; FBC, U&E, LFT, CRP, ANCA, complement, hepatitis B/C, cryoglobulins as indicated; biopsy within 48 h; stop culprit drugs.

    • Never miss

      Renal involvement, systemic ANCA vasculitis, endocarditis and meningococcal sepsis.

    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    What it is

    Vasculitis is inflammation of blood-vessel walls causing damage (haemorrhage, ischaemia). The skin is involved in many systemic vasculitides, and can be the only organ affected (single-organ cutaneous small-vessel vasculitis, the commonest form seen in dermatology).

    Chapel Hill Consensus Conference (CHCC 2012) nomenclature:

    CategoryExamples
    Large vesselGiant-cell arteritis, Takayasu arteritis
    Medium vesselPolyarteritis nodosa, Kawasaki disease
    Small vessel — ANCA-associatedMicroscopic polyangiitis (MPA), granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA), eosinophilic GPA (EGPA)
    Small vessel — immune complexAnti-GBM disease, cryoglobulinaemic vasculitis, IgA vasculitis (HSP), hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis (anti-C1q vasculitis)
    Variable vesselBehçet's disease, Cogan syndrome
    Single-organCutaneous leukocytoclastic angiitis, cutaneous arteritis (= cutaneous PAN)
    Associated with systemic diseaseLupus, rheumatoid, sarcoid vasculitis
    Associated with a probable aetiologyHCV-associated cryoglobulinaemic vasculitis, HBV-associated PAN, drug-associated immune-complex/ANCA vasculitis, cancer-associated vasculitis

    Step 2 of 9 · 1 min

    Why it happens

    • ANCA-mediated neutrophil activation (MPO/PR3).
    • Cell-mediated/granulomatous inflammation.
    • Direct infection of the vessel wall (septic vasculitis — meningococcus, gonococcus, endocarditis).
    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    How it’s caused

    How it is caused

    • Immune-complex deposition (antigen from a drug, infection, autoantigen or tumour → complexes deposit in post-capillary venules → complement activation → neutrophil influx → leukocytoclasis (nuclear dust), fibrinoid necrosis, red-cell extravasation = purpura).
    • ANCA-mediated neutrophil activation (MPO/PR3).
    • Cell-mediated/granulomatous inflammation.
    • Direct infection of the vessel wall (septic vasculitis — meningococcus, gonococcus, endocarditis).
    • Common causes of cutaneous small-vessel vasculitis: idiopathic (~45–55%), infection (~15–20% — streptococcal URTI, hepatitis B/C, HIV, endocarditis), drugs (~10–20% — β-lactams, sulfonamides, NSAIDs, allopurinol, thiazides, propylthiouracil/hydralazine (ANCA), TNF inhibitors, levamisole-contaminated cocaine), connective tissue disease (~15–20% — SLE, RA, Sjögren's), malignancy (<5% — haematological especially).

    Clinical features

    Clinical features & sites — lesion → vessel size

    Vessel sizeSkin lesions
    Small (post-capillary venules, dermal)Palpable purpura, petechiae, urticarial plaques lasting >24 h, haemorrhagic vesicles/bullae, pustules, superficial ulcers, splinter haemorrhages
    Medium (muscular arteries at the dermal–subcutaneous junction)Subcutaneous nodules, livedo racemosa, deep punched-out ulcers, digital infarcts/gangrene, papulonecrotic lesions
    Mixed (ANCA, cryo, CTD)Any of the above

    Distribution: dependent areas — lower legs, ankles, feet, buttocks; under tight clothing; in bed-bound patients the back and buttocks. Symptoms: burning/stinging or pain more than itch. Look for systemic symptoms (fever, arthralgia, abdominal pain, haematuria, cough/haemoptysis, sinusitis, neuropathy).

    Step 4 of 9 · 1 min

    Confirm the diagnosis

    Confirm it is purpura

    • non-blanching on diascopy (press a glass slide). Is it palpable (inflammatory → vasculitis) or flat (petechiae/ecchymoses → platelet/coagulation or capillaritis)?

    Severity assessment

    • BVAS (Birmingham Vasculitis Activity Score) in specialist care; Five-Factor Score for PAN/ANCA (see Polyarteritis nodosa — systemic, HBV-associated and cutaneous).
    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    Diagnosis

    1. Confirm it is purpura: non-blanching on diascopy (press a glass slide). Is it palpable (inflammatory → vasculitis) or flat (petechiae/ecchymoses → platelet/coagulation or capillaritis)?
    2. Skin biopsy — the most useful test: H&E: punch from a fresh lesion 24–48 h old (LCV, vessel size); Direct immunofluorescence (DIF): a second biopsy from a lesion <24 h old (or perilesional), sent in Michel's medium/saline, not formalin. IgA → IgA vasculitis; IgM/C3 → cryoglobulins/immune complex; lupus band → HUVS/SLE; For suspected medium-vessel disease (nodules, livedo, ulcers), take a deep incisional or elliptical biopsy to subcutaneous fat, from a nodule or the white centre of livedo. A 4 mm punch often misses it
    3. Baseline screen for cause and organ involvement: Urinalysis (dipstick for blood and protein — the key bedside test); urine ACR/PCR; urine microscopy (red-cell casts); Blood pressure.; FBC (platelets — exclude thrombocytopenia; eosinophilia — EGPA/drug), U&E/eGFR, LFTs, CRP/ESR, clotting; Immunology: ANA, ENA, dsDNA, ANCA (MPO/PR3), C3/C4 (± C1q, anti-C1q), rheumatoid factor, cryoglobulins (sample kept at 37 °C in transport), IgA level; Infection: hepatitis B and C, HIV, ASO/anti-DNase B, throat swab, blood cultures (endocarditis), ± IGRA (before immunosuppression); Paraprotein: serum protein electrophoresis, immunofixation, serum free light chains; As indicated: CXR, ECG/echo, faecal occult blood/calprotectin, CT angiography (PAN), nerve conduction studies
    4. Severity assessment: BVAS (Birmingham Vasculitis Activity Score) in specialist care; Five-Factor Score for PAN/ANCA (see Polyarteritis nodosa — systemic, HBV-associated and cutaneous).

    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    Differentials

    • Non-vasculitic purpura: thrombocytopenia (ITP, leukaemia, drugs), coagulopathy/anticoagulants, DIC/meningococcal sepsis, senile/actinic and steroid purpura, scurvy (perifollicular), capillaritis (pigmented purpuric dermatoses), trauma/non-accidental injury in children, raised intrathoracic pressure (petechiae above the nipple line after vomiting/coughing).
    • Vasculopathies/occlusion (non-inflammatory): antiphospholipid syndrome, cholesterol emboli, calciphylaxis, livedoid vasculopathy, warfarin necrosis, cryoglobulin/cryofibrinogen occlusion, septic emboli.
    • Also: pigmented purpuric dermatoses, stasis dermatitis, erythema multiforme/urticaria (blanching).

    Treat

    Doses as in the source notes. Always confirm in the current BNF and local formulary.

    More detailfull notes · 4 min

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    • Treat the cause: stop a suspected drug (record + Yellow Card); treat infection.
    • Skin-limited, mild disease: rest, leg elevation, compression hosiery if arterial supply is adequate, emollient, potent TCS for symptoms, antihistamine; NSAIDs only if not the culprit and renal function is normal. Most self-limiting episodes resolve in 3–4 weeks.
    • Chronic/recurrent skin-limited disease (specialist): colchicine, dapsone (G6PD check; FBC/methaemoglobin monitoring), hydroxychloroquine; short prednisolone courses for necrotic/ulcerated disease; azathioprine/methotrexate/MMF for steroid-sparing.
    • Systemic vasculitis: specialist (rheumatology/renal) — glucocorticoids + cyclophosphamide/rituximab (ANCA), etc.
    • Monitoring: clinical (old lesions healing, new crops) + ESR/CRP; urinalysis and BP at each review — renal involvement can appear weeks later.

    Red flags / refer

    • Same day (999/ED): purpura in an unwell or febrile patient — especially a child — meningococcal sepsis/DIC (NICE NG240); suspected endocarditis; rapidly spreading necrosis; haemoptysis; acute kidney injury; GI bleeding/severe abdominal pain; mononeuritis multiplex.
    • Urgent (days): palpable purpura with haematuria/proteinuria, raised creatinine or hypertension (renal); systemic symptoms; positive ANCA; nodules/ulcers/livedo racemosa (medium-vessel disease); persistent >4 weeks or recurrent.
    • Routine dermatology: skin-limited, well patient, normal urine/bloods, cause unclear.

    IP scope

    Recognition, urinalysis/BP, first-line bloods, stopping culprit drugs, symptomatic care and correct-urgency referral are within IP scope. Biopsy, immunosuppression and dapsone/colchicine regimens are specialist-led (shared care at most).

    Worked cases

    Case 1

    • Lesions suggesting vasculitis: palpable purpura, petechiae, urticarial plaques lasting >24 h, haemorrhagic vesicles/bullae, pustules, necrosis/ulcers, nodules, livedo racemosa, digital infarcts, splinter haemorrhages (see Approach to cutaneous vasculitis).
    • How to diagnose: clinical suspicion → biopsy (H&E 24–48 h lesion + DIF <24 h; deep incisional for medium vessels) → screen for cause and organ involvement (urinalysis, BP, FBC, U&E, LFT, CRP/ESR, ANA/ENA/dsDNA, ANCA, C3/C4, cryoglobulins, RF, hepatitis B/C, HIV, ASO, SPEP, blood cultures, CXR) (see Approach to cutaneous vasculitis).
    • Osler/Janeway: Osler = tender immunological finger-pad nodules; Janeway = painless septic-embolic palm/sole macules. Not pathognomonic (SLE, gonococcaemia, typhoid, haemolytic anaemia, marantic endocarditis). Splinters: commonly traumatic/psoriatic; no validated threshold — new, multiple, proximal or multi-nail splinters plus fever/murmur raise concern (see Osler nodes, Janeway lesions and splinter haemorrhages).

    Case 2: 36-year-old woman

    • Diagnosis: hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis (HUVS / anti-C1q vasculitis), with lupus overlap to exclude (ANA+, photosensitivity, arthralgia, low C3/C4, renal impairment): Strictly, Schwartz criteria require ≥6 months of urticaria; she has had 3 months. Label it "hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, probable HUVS"; The creatinine of 2.3 mg/dL (~203 µmol/L) indicates renal involvement → urgent nephrology
    • Confirmatory tests: skin biopsy with DIF (immunoreactants around vessels ± lupus band); C1q level and anti-C1q antibodies; CH50; dsDNA, ENA, antiphospholipid antibodies; urinalysis, uPCR, urine microscopy ± renal biopsy; cryoglobulins; hepatitis B/C; SPEP; CXR/PFTs; ophthalmology review (see Urticarial vasculitis and hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis syndrome).

    Case 3

    • Task 1: UV vs urticaria table; investigations (see Urticarial vasculitis and hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis syndrome).
    • 3a: symmetrical non-blanching palpable purpuric papules (some confluent/haemorrhagic) on the lower legs.
    • 3b: cutaneous leukocytoclastic (small-vessel) vasculitis, drug-induced (NSAID).
    • 3c: hydrostatic pressure, venous stasis and slow flow in dependent venules → immune-complex deposition (hence rest/elevation/hosiery).
    • 3d: see Cutaneous small-vessel (leukocytoclastic) vasculitis.
    • 3e: urinalysis.
    • 3f: see Cutaneous small-vessel (leukocytoclastic) vasculitis.
    • 3g: stop the NSAID, alternative analgesia, rest/elevation/compression, potent TCS/antihistamine; dapsone/colchicine/short prednisolone if persistent or necrotic; monitor clinically + ESR/CRP + urine/BP (see Cutaneous small-vessel (leukocytoclastic) vasculitis).

    Case 4: 30-year-old woman, HBsAg+

    • Diagnosis: HBV-associated systemic polyarteritis nodosa.: ACR criteria met: weight loss >4 kg, livedo, myalgia/leg tenderness, neuropathy, diastolic BP >90, raised creatinine (2.7 mg/dL ≈ 239 µmol/L), HBsAg+ = 7 of 10; Predictive items: HBsAg+, neuropathy positive; ANCA/cryo/asthma/ENT/GN negative
    • Supporting: nodules along arteries, necrotic ulcers, anaemia, thrombocytosis, leucocytosis. Renal impairment with hypertension reflects renal-artery vasculopathy (renovascular hypertension), not GN.
    • Investigations still needed: CT/MR or catheter angiography (renal/mesenteric microaneurysms), deep skin/muscle/nerve biopsy, HBeAg/HBV DNA, LFT, nerve conduction studies, urinalysis (bland sediment expected), echo/ECG.
    • DDx: ANCA-associated vasculitis (MPA), cryoglobulinaemic vasculitis, SLE vasculitis, infective endocarditis, APS, cholesterol emboli, lymphoma, DADA2.
    • Treatment: antiviral (entecavir/tenofovir) + short-course steroids + plasma exchange (avoid prolonged immunosuppression in HBV-PAN); BP control; specialist MDT (rheumatology, hepatology, renal); contact tracing/vaccination (see Polyarteritis nodosa — systemic, HBV-associated and cutaneous).

    Case 5

    Diagnosis of PAN; cutaneous vs systemic PAN; causes of livedo and vessel involvement; treatment (see Polyarteritis nodosa — systemic, HBV-associated and cutaneous and Livedo reticularis vs livedo racemosa).

    Practice case: 7-year-old boy

    • QA = 4, Henoch–Schönlein purpura (IgA vasculitis): purpura + arthralgia + abdominal pain + haematuria/proteinuria after a URTI; normal platelets.
    • QB = 4, LCV with perivascular IgA and C3 on DIF.: The course explanation attributes "necrotising vasculitis of medium-sized arteries with negative DIF" to GPA as well as PAN. The correct match is: option 1 = PAN; option 2 (small- and medium-vessel vasculitis with necrosis and granulomas) = GPA; option 3 = giant-cell arteritis; option 5 (IgM) = cryoglobulinaemia
    • QC = 2, group A β-haemolytic streptococcus.
    • UK additions: exclude meningococcal sepsis first. The urinalysis already shows blood + protein, so check BP and the urine protein:creatinine ratio, and discuss with paediatrics (CKS). Then a structured urine/BP follow-up for ≥6 months.

    Red flags & referral

    • Same day

      Blood or protein on urine dipstick, or raised BP: same-day discussion with renal or medical team.

    • 999 / A&E

      Non-blanching rash in an unwell or feverish patient: suspected meningococcal disease, 999 (NICE NG240).

    • Urgent

      Haemoptysis, breathlessness, neuropathy or abdominal pain with purpura: systemic vasculitis, urgent referral.

    Step 8 of 9 · 1 min

    Prescriber pearls

    • "Glass test first." Non-blanching + palpable = vasculitis work-up; non-blanching + unwell = 999.

    • Always dip the urine and check BP — then repeat at follow-up.

    • Ask about every new drug in the last 1–3 weeks, including OTC ibuprofen and recreational cocaine (levamisole).
