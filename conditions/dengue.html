    Dengue: skin signs & safety

    A mosquito-borne viral fever with severe aches, retro-orbital headache and a rash that classically shows ‘islands of white in a sea of red’. Exclude malaria, avoid NSAIDs and aspirin, and watch for warning signs as the fever settles.

    Also calleddengue feverbreakbone feverislands of white in a sea of redpetechiaetourniquet testNSAIDs dengue+1

    Key facts

    • High fever, retro-orbital headache, muscle and joint pain and a flushing or ‘white islands’ rash within 2 weeks of tropical travel.
    • Dengue virus (flavivirus, 4 serotypes) via Aedes mosquitoes; capillary leak and low platelets drive severe disease, often as the fever falls.
    • Same-day assessment: malaria test, FBC (platelets, haematocrit), NS1 / PCR or serology; paracetamol and fluids only.
    • Severe dengue (plasma leakage, bleeding, organ impairment); NSAIDs or aspirin raise bleeding risk.

    Overview

    Dengue is an acute mosquito-borne viral illness and the most common travel-associated arboviral infection seen in the UK. UKHSA counted 904 confirmed/probable imported cases in England, Wales and NI in 2024 (a record) and 344 in 2025. Most were acquired in South and South-East Asia (India, Thailand, Indonesia), with large outbreaks also in Latin America and the Caribbean. Sporadic local transmission now occurs in southern Europe (France, Italy, Spain, Croatia). Most infections are mild or asymptomatic. A minority progress to severe dengue (plasma leakage, shock, haemorrhage, organ impairment), which is more likely with a second infection with a different serotype.

    WHO 2009 classification: dengue without warning signs → dengue with warning signs → severe dengue. The older term "dengue haemorrhagic fever" has been replaced.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    • Aetiology: dengue virus is a flavivirus (same family as yellow fever and Zika). It is a single-stranded RNA virus with four serotypes (DENV-1 to DENV-4).
    • Vector: Aedes aegypti (and Ae. albopictus). These are day-biting mosquitoes, most active in the early morning and late afternoon. They breed in small collections of clean standing water (containers, tyres, plant pots), so risk is high in urban areas. Ae. albopictus is found intermittently in parts of Europe, but not established in the UK, so UK onward transmission risk is negligible.
    • Incubation is 4–10 days (range about 3–14). A patient can infect feeding mosquitoes during the viraemic phase.
    • Immunity: infection gives lifelong immunity to that serotype and only brief cross-protection to the others. Antibody-dependent enhancement at a later infection with a different serotype is thought to drive more severe disease.
    • Skin mechanisms: capillary dilatation causes the early flush. An immune-mediated viral exanthem causes the morbilliform rash. Thrombocytopenia, capillary fragility and endothelial leak cause petechiae and purpura.

    Clinical features

    Illness phases:

    1. Febrile phase (days 1–3/7): abrupt high fever, severe frontal/retro-orbital headache, myalgia, arthralgia and back pain ("break-bone fever"), nausea and vomiting, and a positive tourniquet test.
    2. Critical phase (around defervescence, days 3–7): plasma leakage can develop as the fever settles. This is the danger window.
    3. Recovery phase: fluid reabsorbs, and there may be bradycardia and the convalescent rash.

    Skin manifestations. About half of patients get a rash, and it is often missed on darker skin.

    • Early (first 24–48 h): transient flushing/blanching erythema of the face, neck and chest.
    • Days 3–6: a maculopapular or morbilliform exanthem. It often starts on the dorsum of the hands and feet, spreads to the limbs and trunk, and may be itchy.
    • Convalescent rash: confluent erythema with round pale "islands of sparing", the classic "white islands in a sea of red", the characteristic sign. It is often itchy (palms/soles may be involved) and can be followed by fine desquamation.
    • Haemorrhagic signs: petechiae (especially on the lower legs, feet and at pressure sites), purpura/ecchymoses, conjunctival injection or bleeding, gum bleeding and nosebleeds. A positive tourniquet test shows capillary fragility.
    • Case 3 final question (lesions on the legs): these are petechiae/purpura. They signify thrombocytopenia and increased vascular (capillary) fragility or leakage. They can occur in uncomplicated dengue, but they are a prompt to check the platelet count and haematocrit and to look for warning signs of severe dengue.

    Warning signs (refer to hospital same day):

    • abdominal pain or tenderness
    • persistent vomiting
    • clinical fluid accumulation (effusions, ascites)
    • mucosal bleeding
    • lethargy or restlessness
    • liver enlargement over 2 cm
    • rising haematocrit with a rapidly falling platelet count

    Severe dengue:

    • shock or respiratory distress from plasma leakage
    • severe bleeding
    • severe organ impairment (AST/ALT ≥1000, altered consciousness, heart or kidney failure)

    Types to recognise

    • White islands

      Confluent erythema with sparing. Site: Legs.

    • Truncal exanthem

      Confluent morbilliform rash. Site: Trunk.

    • Darker skin

      Erythema subtle. Site: Trunk.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    • History: fever plus travel to an endemic area within the past 2 weeks (dengue's incubation fits returns within about 14 days). Always exclude malaria first if the patient travelled to a malarious area.
    • Bloods: FBC: leucopenia, thrombocytopenia, rising haematocrit; LFTs: raised transaminases; U&E, CRP (usually low or modest)
    • Specific tests (in the UK, via the local lab or the UKHSA Rare and Imported Pathogens Laboratory, RIPL): Days 1–5/7: RT-PCR (viral RNA) and/or NS1 antigen; After about day 5: dengue IgM (single positive = probable); Paired IgG rise or IgM seroconversion confirms
    • Serology cross-reacts with other flaviviruses (Zika, yellow fever/Japanese encephalitis vaccines), so give the full travel and vaccine history.
    • Notifiability: dengue virus is a laboratory-notifiable organism in England. Clinicians do not need to notify suspected cases.

    Management

    Treatment: there is no specific antiviral. Care is supportive and focuses on fluids and early detection of warning signs. Early supportive care brings case fatality in severe dengue below 1%.

    • Any fever in a returning traveller → same-day medical assessment (GP, urgent care, or A&E/HTD walk-in if unwell). This is not a pharmacy-only problem.
    • Analgesia/antipyretic: paracetamol only (standard doses; caution with liver involvement).
    • Avoid aspirin, ibuprofen and other NSAIDs (bleeding risk with thrombocytopenia), and do not sell them OTC to a febrile returnee. Avoid IM injections.
    • Hydration: oral fluids and oral rehydration salts, rest, and monitoring of urine output.
    • Outpatient care (only if senior clinician-assessed and no warning signs) includes daily review or bloods (FBC/haematocrit) through the critical phase (days 3–7, especially as the fever falls). Give written "come back immediately if…" safety-netting: abdominal pain; persistent vomiting; bleeding gums or nose; black stools; drowsiness or restlessness; cold, clammy skin; passing little urine
    • Admit with warning signs, severe dengue, pregnancy, infants or older adults, significant comorbidity (diabetes, renal/cardiac disease), or poor social support. Hospital care includes careful IV crystalloid and haematocrit-guided fluid management.
    • Itch in the convalescent rash: emollient, menthol in aqueous cream, or a non-sedating antihistamine. It settles over days.
    • Medication review: pause or discuss anticoagulants and antiplatelets with the treating team if platelets are low. Check for herbal products (e.g. papaya leaf) that patients may take.

    Treatment ladder

    1. 1

      Same-day assessment

      Any fever in a returning traveller → same-day GP / urgent care / A&E (HTD walk-in if unwell): not a pharmacy-only problem.

    2. 2

      Supportive

      Paracetamol only (standard doses; caution with liver involvement). Oral fluids / ORS, rest, monitor urine output.

    3. 3

      Prevention

      Bite avoidance by day: DEET 50% (adults, children > 2 months, pregnancy), sunscreen first then repellent; long sleeves, permethrin clothing; screens / AC; nets for daytime naps; remove standing water. Qdenga (live, 2 doses 3 months apart): only ≥ 4 years with previous dengue travelling to risk areas (Green Book 15a).

    Caution

    Prescribing limits and pitfalls

    • NSAIDs and aspirin: Avoid; don’t sell OTC to a febrile returnee. Avoid IM injections.

    Referral

    • 999 / A&E

      Warning signs: abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, mucosal bleeding, lethargy / restlessness, fluid accumulation, rising haematocrit with falling platelets.

    • Same day

      Fever after the tropics: exclude malaria the same day.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Warning signs: abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, mucosal bleeding, lethargy or restlessness, fluid accumulation, rising haematocrit with falling platelets: emergency admission.
    • Never give NSAIDs or aspirin in suspected dengue.
    • Fever after the tropics: exclude malaria the same day.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • Bite avoidance is the main prevention. Aedes bite during the day, so a malaria-style "dusk to dawn" message is not enough: Use DEET-based repellent (50% recommended by NaTHNaC for adults, children over 2 months, and pregnancy/breastfeeding). Alternatives are icaridin (picaridin), eucalyptus citriodora oil (PMD) or IR3535. Apply sunscreen first, repellent second, and reapply; Wear long sleeves and trousers, and permethrin-treated clothing; Stay in screened or air-conditioned rooms. Use nets for daytime naps (infants); Remove standing water around accommodation

    • Vaccine (UK, Green Book chapter 15a, Oct 2024): Qdenga® (TAK-003) is a live attenuated vaccine given as 2 doses 3 months apart; It is recommended in the UK only for people aged 4 years and over with a previous dengue infection (confirmed or likely) who are travelling to risk or outbreak areas, or who are exposed through work. It is not recommended for dengue-naive travellers (JCVI precaution); Consider delaying for 1 year after a lab-confirmed infection; Contraindicated in pregnancy, breastfeeding and immunosuppression, and after previous anaphylaxis to the vaccine; NICE CKS and TravelHealthPro carry the eligibility algorithm. Testing for past infection goes via UKHSA RIPL

    • A second dengue infection carries a higher risk of severe disease. Previously infected travellers deserve a careful pre-travel consultation.

    • Dengue cannot be caught person to person in the UK (no established vector), apart from rare blood/organ/vertical transmission.
