    Dermatitis herpetiformis & coeliac disease

    Module 4 · Week 6

    An intensely itchy, symmetrical eruption of tiny blisters on the elbows, knees, buttocks and scalp that is the skin form of coeliac disease. Test for coeliac disease while still eating gluten; a strict gluten-free diet is the long-term treatment.

    CoversDHcoeliac skingluten rashitchy elbows buttockstTGdapsonegluten-free dietIgA
    Think of it when

    Intense itch with grouped tiny vesicles or just excoriations on the elbows, knees, buttocks and scalp, not responding to steroids.

    What drives it

    Gluten sensitivity: IgA against epidermal transglutaminase deposits in the dermal papillae (granular IgA on DIF).

    Backbone of care

    Coeliac serology (tTG-IgA with total IgA) before any gluten-free trial; refer for biopsy with DIF; lifelong gluten-free diet; specialist dapsone for itch control.

    Never miss

    Anaemia, osteoporosis and small-bowel lymphoma risk; G6PD check and haemolysis/methaemoglobinaemia on dapsone.

    What it is

    Dermatitis herpetiformis is the skin form of coeliac disease. It is an intensely itchy, chronic, grouped papulovesicular eruption on the extensor surfaces, caused by gluten sensitivity. Almost all patients have gluten-sensitive enteropathy on biopsy, even though most have few or no gut symptoms (or symptoms labelled "IBS", as in Cases 2 and 4). It mostly affects people of Northern European descent aged 30–40 and is slightly commoner in men. It is rare in children, and in Japanese and African-heritage populations.

    2

    How it’s caused

    Genetic background is HLA-DQ2/DQ8. Gliadin is deamidated by tissue transglutaminase (TG2), which drives mucosal IgA anti-TG2 and then, by epitope spreading, IgA against epidermal transglutaminase (TG3). IgA–TG3 complexes deposit in the dermal papillae, neutrophils gather into papillary microabscesses, and a subepidermal vesicle forms. Iodide (e.g. iodinated contrast or seaweed) and NSAIDs can make it flare.

    3

    Clinical features

    • Symmetrical extensor distribution: elbows, forearms, knees, buttocks/sacrum, shoulders, scalp.
    • Intense itch or burning, often before lesions appear. Tiny vesicles are scratched off quickly, so patients usually present with excoriations, crusted erosions and post-inflammatory change only (Case 4: "excoriations without blisters"; photo C04).
    • Associated features: gut: bloating, loose stool, "IBS" (Cases 2 and 4); iron-deficiency anaemia or pallor (Case 2); aphthae, dental enamel defects, fatigue; personal or family history of autoimmunity (thyroid disease, T1DM, pernicious anaemia, vitiligo)
    • Case 2's atopic history is a red herring that steers people toward "eczema". Case 4's failure of Betnovate (TCS barely touches DH) and the normal routine bloods are also typical.
    4

    Diagnosis

    Case 2 and Case 4 Q2 answers:

    1. Coeliac serology while the patient is eating gluten (NICE NG20): IgA tissue transglutaminase (tTG-IgA) + total IgA first line. Use IgA EMA if tTG is weakly positive, and IgG-based tests (IgG tTG/DGP/EMA) if IgA-deficient. Advise eating gluten in more than one meal every day for at least 6 weeks before testing. In DH, serology is positive in ~75–90%, so a negative result doesn't exclude it.
    2. Skin biopsy for DIF from perilesional, uninvolved skin (about 1 cm from a lesion, ideally on the buttock or forearm; not lesional skin, where inflammation destroys the IgA). It shows granular IgA in the dermal papillae, which is the diagnostic gold standard. Lesional H&E (if an intact vesicle exists) shows a subepidermal blister with papillary neutrophil microabscesses.
    3. Gastroenterology: NICE NG20 says to refer anyone with DH for specialist assessment. Whether a duodenal biopsy is needed in proven DH is decided by gastroenterology and dermatology together (many UK centres skip it when skin DIF is positive).
    4. Baseline bloods: FBC, ferritin, folate, B12, calcium/vitamin D/ALP, LFT, TFT, glucose; G6PD before dapsone. Consider a DXA scan (NG20 criteria).
    5

    Differentials

    • Scabies (burrows, web spaces, genitals, contacts; Case 4 specifically had no burrows)
    • atopic or nummular eczema (Case 2's atopic history)
    • neurotic excoriations or prurigo
    • papular urticaria / insect bites
    • linear IgA disease (linear IgA on DIF, no coeliac link, often drug-induced)
    • BP (older patient, tense bullae)
    • folliculitis
    • Grover's (trunk, older men)
    • EM
    • scabies + DH both possible
    6

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    Case 4 Q3–Q5:

    • Immediate management (Q3): do not start a gluten-free diet yet; keep eating gluten until serology and biopsy are done. Refer to dermatology for biopsy with DIF, and to gastroenterology (NICE NG20). Meanwhile, symptomatic relief with emollients, a sedating antihistamine at night (limited benefit), and a potent TCS for short-term itch relief only (expect little response).
    • Best skin drug (Q4): dapsone (specialist-initiated, then shared care): Typical start is 25–50 mg daily, titrated to the lowest dose that controls itch (often 50–100 mg). Itch responds within 48–72 h, which also supports the diagnosis; Pre-checks: G6PD (risk of severe haemolysis if deficient), FBC, LFT, U&E; Monitoring: FBC weekly for 4 weeks, then every 2 weeks to 12 weeks, then 3-monthly, plus LFT and reticulocytes per the local shared-care protocol. Every patient has dose-related haemolysis and methaemoglobinaemia (blue-grey lips, breathlessness, headache). Rarer but serious: agranulocytosis (usually weeks 3–12, so counsel about sore throat, fever and mouth ulcers → urgent FBC), dapsone hypersensitivity syndrome / DRESS (weeks 2–6; HLA-B*13:01), hepatitis, and peripheral motor neuropathy; Interactions: trimethoprim raises levels, rifampicin lowers them, and other oxidant drugs add to haemolysis; Alternatives: sulfapyridine (unlicensed, import or specials) or sulfamethoxypyridazine; sulfasalazine is sometimes used. Superpotent TCS as an adjunct for lesions
    • Lifestyle (Q5): lifelong strict gluten-free diet (GFD), which is the definitive treatment. It lets dapsone be reduced or stopped, usually after 1–2 years (the skin lags behind the gut), and lowers the risk of lymphoma. Wheat, barley, rye and cross-contaminated oats are excluded; uncontaminated oats are allowed for most. Refer to a dietitian, and offer Coeliac UK membership. Gluten-free food prescribing varies across the UK (e.g. England restricted it to bread and mixes in 2018, and ICBs vary).
    • Complications to screen for or counsel about (Q6): enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma and other NHL (risk falls with strict GFD); small-bowel adenocarcinoma (rare); osteoporosis/osteopenia (calcium, vitamin D, DXA per NICE); iron, folate and B12 deficiency; autoimmune thyroid disease, T1DM, pernicious anaemia, vitiligo, alopecia areata; hyposplenism (NG20: consider pneumococcal and other vaccines); reduced fertility and adverse pregnancy outcomes if untreated; dapsone toxicity; psychosocial burden of the diet; first-degree relatives have ~10% risk (offer serology if symptomatic; NG20)
    7

    Red flags & referral

    • Do not start a gluten-free diet before serology and biopsy: it gives false-negative results.
    • On dapsone: check G6PD first; watch for haemolysis, methaemoglobinaemia (blue lips, breathlessness), agranulocytosis and DRESS.
    • Weight loss, anaemia or diarrhoea: gastroenterology (NICE NG20).
    8

    Prescriber pearls

    • "Itchy elbows and buttocks plus 'IBS' or anaemia" means DH. Test before the patient tries a gluten-free diet on their own (Case 4's dietitian referral could start this by accident; tell the dietitian).
    • Check excipients: wheat starch in tablet excipients is usually at negligible gluten levels (per BNF/Coeliac UK), but counsel patients who ask.
    • Dapsone: record the G6PD result before the first dose, give written agranulocytosis and methaemoglobinaemia warnings, and do not issue repeats without up-to-date bloods.
    • An annual coeliac review covers weight, symptoms, diet adherence, dietitian referral if needed, and bloods.
