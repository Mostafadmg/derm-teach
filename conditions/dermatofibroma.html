    Dermatofibroma

    A common firm button-like papule, usually on the legs, that dimples when pinched. Dermoscopy shows a central white patch with a fine peripheral network. Pigmented forms can mimic a mole or melanoma.

    Overview

    Module 2 · Week 1benign fibrous histiocytomadimple signpinch sign

    • Spot it

      A firm, pink-brown, button-like papule on a limb (often the lower leg of a woman) that dimples when pinched.

    • Why

      A benign reactive proliferation of dermal fibroblasts and histiocytes, sometimes after a bite or minor injury.

    • Treat

      Clinical diagnosis plus dermoscopy; reassure; excise only if symptomatic or uncertain (leaves a scar, may recur).

    • Never miss

      DFSP (growing, larger than 2–3 cm, on the trunk), melanoma (pigmented or changing), and atypical features on dermoscopy.

    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    What it is

    Dermatofibroma (DF) is a very common benign fibrohistiocytic proliferation in the dermis. It is seen mostly in adults aged 20–50 and is more common in women. It is a reactive or neoplastic nodule; which of the two is still debated.

    Pigmented dermatofibroma

    A dermatofibroma (core: the skin lumps notes) with marked overlying epidermal hyperpigmentation. It is commoner and darker in skin of colour, so it is often brought in as a "mole".

    Start: Why it happens
    2

    Step 2 of 9 · 1 min

    Why it happens

    • Often follows minor trauma such as an insect bite, folliculitis or a shaving nick, but many patients recall none.
    • Dermal fibroblasts and histiocytes proliferate and trap collagen bundles at the periphery. The overlying epidermis is induced to thicken and hyperpigment, which is why the surface is often brown.
    • Multiple (eruptive) DFs (≥15, or many appearing quickly) are associated with altered immunity: SLE, HIV, immunosuppressive drugs and pregnancy.
    • Histology: Factor XIIIa positive, CD34 negative. This is the reverse of DFSP (see Dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans), and the stain pair is a favourite exam question.
    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    How it’s caused

    • Often follows minor trauma such as an insect bite, folliculitis or a shaving nick, but many patients recall none.
    • Dermal fibroblasts and histiocytes proliferate and trap collagen bundles at the periphery. The overlying epidermis is induced to thicken and hyperpigment, which is why the surface is often brown.
    • Multiple (eruptive) DFs (≥15, or many appearing quickly) are associated with altered immunity: SLE, HIV, immunosuppressive drugs and pregnancy.
    • Histology: Factor XIIIa positive, CD34 negative. This is the reverse of DFSP (see Dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans), and the stain pair is a favourite exam question.

    Pigmented dermatofibroma

    Dermal fibrohistiocytic proliferation induces the overlying epidermis to thicken (acanthosis) and to increase basal melanin ("dirty fingers" rete ridges). Haemosiderin deposits make the aneurysmal/haemosiderotic variant blue-black.

    Next: What you’ll see
    3

    Step 3 of 9 · 2 min

    What you’ll see

    • Firm to hard papule or nodule, 3–10 mm, that feels like a "button" tethered to the skin but mobile over deeper tissue.
    • Colour varies (skin-coloured, pink, brown, or a darker rim with a paler centre), especially in darker skin (see the benign lesion notes, pigmented DF).
    • Lower legs are the classic site, then arms and trunk. Usually solitary and asymptomatic. It may itch or be tender, and it is often nicked when shaving.
    • Dimple (Fitzpatrick / pinch) sign: pinching the lesion from the sides makes it dimple inward.
    • A firm 3–10 mm papule, brown to dark brown, often darker at the rim with a paler centre. Sometimes pink-brown.
    • Limbs (arms in Case 1, legs classically). Slow growth over years. A positive dimple sign.

    Clinical features

    • Firm to hard papule or nodule, 3–10 mm, that feels like a "button" tethered to the skin but mobile over deeper tissue.
    • Colour varies (skin-coloured, pink, brown, or a darker rim with a paler centre), especially in darker skin (see the benign lesion notes, pigmented DF).
    • Lower legs are the classic site, then arms and trunk. Usually solitary and asymptomatic. It may itch or be tender, and it is often nicked when shaving.
    • Dimple (Fitzpatrick / pinch) sign: pinching the lesion from the sides makes it dimple inward.
    • Variants to know: cellular, aneurysmal/haemosiderotic (can enlarge quickly and look blue-black), atypical ("pseudosarcomatous"), and deep or subcutaneous. Cellular, aneurysmal and atypical variants recur more often after excision, and rare cases metastasise. This is the course Case 1 answer.

    Pigmented dermatofibroma

    • A firm 3–10 mm papule, brown to dark brown, often darker at the rim with a paler centre. Sometimes pink-brown.
    • Limbs (arms in Case 1, legs classically). Slow growth over years. A positive dimple sign.
    Next: Confirm the diagnosis
    4

    Step 4 of 9 · 1 min

    Confirm the diagnosis

    Key points

    • Clinical: typical site, firmness, and the dimple sign.
    • Dermoscopy: a delicate peripheral pigment network with a central white scar-like patch. The commonest pattern, but not universal.
    • Biopsy is needed only for atypical lesions: >1 cm, rapid growth, ulceration, bleeding, recurrence after removal, unusual site (face, scalp, acral), or doubt between DF and a melanocytic lesion.
    • Pinch test. Dermoscopy: delicate peripheral pigment network + central white scar-like area. Some show a bland structureless or homogeneous pattern, and those are less reassuring, so refer if unsure.

    Pigmented dermatofibroma

    Melanocytic naevus; melanoma (EFG, change, irregular dermoscopy); pigmented BCC (pearly, ovoid nests, arborising vessels); SK (stuck-on, milia-like cysts); post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation over a prurigo nodule; blue naevus.

    More detailfull notes · 3 min

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    • Typical DF: reassure and give the BAD/DermNet leaflet. No biopsy or removal is needed.- Removal for cosmetic reasons only is generally not funded on the NHS.* NHS England's *Evidence-Based Interventions programme and local "procedures of limited clinical value" policies restrict benign lesion removal to set criteria: diagnostic doubt, recurrent bleeding/infection, functional impairment or significant pain. Excision leaves a scar often larger and more visible than the DF, and incomplete removal (DF extends deeper than it looks) leads to recurrence.
    • Cryotherapy or shave can flatten a DF but often leaves a white mark and recurrence. These are specialist or GP-surgeon decisions, not IP.
    • Refer (routine dermatology) if diagnosis is uncertain, a variant is suspected, or the lesion is >1 cm and growing.
    • Refer urgently (two-week-wait / suspected cancer pathway) if it may be melanoma: NG12 weighted 7-point checklist ≥3, or features of nodular melanoma. Refer urgently (sarcoma or dermatology as per local pathway) if DFSP is suspected (see Dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans).

    Pigmented dermatofibroma

    • Classic DF features plus the dimple sign: reassure; leaflet.
    • Any doubt about a melanocytic lesion in darker skin: refer (routine dermatology/teledermatology with dermoscopic). If the 7-point score is ≥3 (e.g. change in size + irregular colour), use the suspected cancer pathway.
    • Removal on cosmetic grounds is not routinely NHS-funded (the skin lumps notes see Pigmented dermatofibroma).

    Worked cases

    Case 1: 42-year-old woman, firm dome-shaped skin-coloured papule on the leg for 8 months

    • Diagnosis: dermatofibroma.
    • Bedside test: dimple (pinch/Fitzpatrick) sign. Dermoscopy: peripheral network with a central white patch.
    • Subtype with recurrence/metastatic potential: cellular (also aneurysmal and atypical) fibrous histiocytoma.
    • Management (UK teaching answer): reassure; no biopsy or removal for a typical lesion. Removal only for symptoms or diagnostic doubt, within local funding criteria. Refer if atypical (>1 cm, growing, ulcerated, recurrent) or if the 7-point checklist is ≥3. The course "excise with a 3–5 mm margin" is a reasonable specialist approach if excision is indicated, not a routine plan (see Where course teaching differs from UK guidance).

    Case 2: 5-year-old boy, congenital hairless scalp patch, now thicker and yellow-orange

    • Diagnosis: sebaceous naevus of Jadassohn (pubertal/hormonal phase approaching; HRAS/KRAS mosaicism).
    • Counsel: risk of secondary neoplasms in adult life, mostly benign (trichoblastoma, syringocystadenoma papilliferum); BCC rare (<1%). Watch for any new lump, ulcer or bleeding.
    • Management: refer routinely to paediatric dermatology. Observation or elective complete excision (narrow margin, timing shared with the family; earlier for the face). Biopsy any nodule. Look for syndromic features (Schimmelpenning) if the lesion is extensive.
    • Differential course teaching raised: linear morphoea en coup de sabre (acquired, indurated, progressive, which needs urgent treatment).

    Case 3: 38-year-old man with a forehead nodule, punctum and odorous contents; 56-year-old woman with multiple painful lipomas

    • Diagnosis 1: epidermoid (infundibular) cyst. Syndrome association: Gardner syndrome (FAP, APC): multiple early-onset cysts, osteomas, dental anomalies, desmoids, and colorectal polyposis.
    • Management: reassure. Treat inflammation without routine antibiotics. Elective complete excision of the cyst wall once quiet if criteria are met. Image midline dermoids first.
    • Diagnosis 2: Dercum's disease (adiposis dolorosa): painful lipomas, obesity, female, depression/fatigue. Symptomatic, multidisciplinary care; screen mood. Use NG12 sarcoma red flags for any enlarging lump.

    Red flags & referral

    • Routine

      Growing beyond 2–3 cm, irregular, nodular or on the trunk: think DFSP and refer.

    • Urgent

      Changing pigmented lesion with atypical dermoscopy: refer on a suspected cancer pathway (NICE NG12).

    Next: Prescriber pearls
    8

    Step 8 of 9 · 1 min

    Prescriber pearls

    • Teach the patient the pinch test and explain that it is "a little knot of scar-like tissue". Shaving over it causes bleeding; a shaving guard helps.

    • Many eruptive DFs in someone on immunosuppressants (e.g. methotrexate, biologics) or with possible SLE/HIV: check the drug list and consider whether investigations are warranted (GP/specialist).

    • A painful DF that the patient wants removed is a worked case in the skin surgery notes surgery case. See the skin surgery notes for the anticoagulant, latex and local anaesthetic issues.

    • Five years of slow growth fits DF, but "growth" is still a 7-point major feature (2 points). Document the dimple sign and size, and photograph with a ruler for comparison.

    • In skin of colour, do not dismiss acral or nail pigmentation as "ethnic". See the melanoma notes (acral melanoma) and Hutchinson's sign.
