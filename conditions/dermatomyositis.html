    Dermatomyositis

    An autoimmune disease of skin and muscle: violet eyelids, Gottron papules over the knuckles, a photo-distributed rash and proximal weakness. In adults it can herald a cancer, so every new case needs a cancer screen.

    Step 1 of 9 · 1 min

    Overview

    Module 1 · Week 5heliotrope rashGottron papulesshawl sign

    • Spot it

      Violaceous eyelids, scaly papules over the knuckles, a red V or shawl on the chest and back, ragged cuticles, with or without proximal muscle weakness.

    • Why

      Autoimmune damage to small vessels of skin and muscle; myositis-specific antibodies predict cancer (TIF1-γ, NXP-2) or lung disease (MDA5).

    • Treat

      CK, ANA and myositis antibodies, urgent rheumatology/dermatology referral; age-appropriate cancer screening; strict photoprotection.

    • Never miss

      Underlying malignancy (ovary, lung, gut, breast); interstitial lung disease; swallowing and respiratory muscle weakness.

    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    What it is

    An idiopathic inflammatory myopathy with characteristic skin signs. Incidence ~1 per 100,000. Peaks in childhood (juvenile DM, 5–14 years) and at 40–60 years (F>M). Around 20% are clinically amyopathic (skin only — CADM). In adults it is a paraneoplastic marker in a significant minority.

    How it’s caused

    How it is caused

    • Complement-mediated microangiopathy (MAC deposition in capillaries → perifascicular atrophy), with type I interferon signatures, in genetically predisposed people.
    • Triggers: malignancy, UV, drugs (hydroxyurea, statins — more often necrotising myopathy; TNF-i; checkpoint inhibitors).

    Next: What you’ll see
    3

    Step 3 of 9 · 1 min

    What you’ll see

    • Heliotrope rash: violaceous upper-eyelid erythema ± periorbital oedema.
    • Gottron papules: violaceous flat-topped papules over the MCP/PIP/DIP joints (pathognomonic, A02). Gottron sign: macular erythema over the knuckles, elbows, knees.
    • Nailfold: dilated capillary loops, ragged cuticles (Samitz sign), periungual erythema.
    • Others: itchy, scaly scalp (psoriasiform/seb-derm-like), mechanic's hands (antisynthetase), calcinosis (juvenile), flagellate erythema, panniculitis, ulcers (MDA5), oedema.
    • Itch is prominent — a useful contrast with lupus.
    • Muscle: symmetrical proximal weakness (rising from a chair, climbing stairs, combing hair), myalgia, dysphagia (pharyngeal), respiratory muscle weakness.

    • Heliotrope rash: violaceous upper-eyelid erythema ± periorbital oedema.

    Step 4 of 9 · 1 min

    Confirm the diagnosis

    Bloods

    • CK (may be normal in CADM), aldolase, LDH, ALT/AST (raised from muscle — don't misread as liver disease), ESR/CRP, ANA and myositis antibody panel (MSA/MAA), TFT, FBC, U&E.

    Specialist tests

    • EMG, MRI of thighs (oedema → guides biopsy), muscle ± skin biopsy (interface dermatitis with mucin, similar to lupus).

    ILD screen

    • HRCT, PFTs incl. TLCO.

    Heart

    • ECG ± echo/troponin.
    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    Diagnosis

    • Bloods: CK (may be normal in CADM), aldolase, LDH, ALT/AST (raised from muscle — don't misread as liver disease), ESR/CRP, ANA and myositis antibody panel (MSA/MAA), TFT, FBC, U&E.
    • Specialist tests: EMG, MRI of thighs (oedema → guides biopsy), muscle ± skin biopsy (interface dermatitis with mucin, similar to lupus).
    • ILD screen: HRCT, PFTs incl. TLCO.
    • Heart: ECG ± echo/troponin.
    • Swallow assessment.
    • EULAR/ACR 2017 classification criteria.
    • Cancer screening: Adult DM carries a ~3–7× increased cancer risk (~15–25% of adults with DM). Highest in the 3 years around diagnosis (especially the first year), in those >40, with anti-TIF1-γ/NXP2, rapid onset, dysphagia, cutaneous necrosis, or a poor treatment response; Typical cancers: ovarian, lung, pancreatic, stomach, colorectal, breast, non-Hodgkin lymphoma; nasopharyngeal in South-East Asian patients; IMACS 2023 international guideline stratifies risk:; Basic screen for all: history/exam (incl. breast, pelvic, rectal/prostate, lymph nodes), FBC, LFT, ESR/CRP, SPEP/free light chains, urinalysis, CXR, and age-appropriate national screening (cervical, breast, bowel); Enhanced screen for moderate/high risk: CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, ± mammography, pelvic/transvaginal USS + CA-125, PSA, upper/lower endoscopy, FDG-PET/CT; Repeat annually for 3 years in high-risk patients; Juvenile DM is not cancer-associated

    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    Differentials

    SLE/SCLE (spares knuckles — lupus favours skin between the joints; nasolabial folds spared; less itch), psoriasis (scalp/elbows), seborrhoeic dermatitis, contact/photo-dermatitis (eyelids), rosacea, drug-induced DM-like eruptions (hydroxyurea), lichen planus, polymorphic light eruption. For weakness: statin myopathy/immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (anti-HMGCR), hypothyroid myopathy, inclusion-body myositis, steroid myopathy, polymyalgia rheumatica (pain, stiffness, not weakness; normal CK).

    Treat

    Doses as in the source notes. Always confirm in the current BNF and local formulary.

    More detailfull notes · 11 min

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    • Refer to dermatology + rheumatology (urgently if weakness, dysphagia, breathlessness or a high CK). Same-day if there is respiratory compromise or difficulty swallowing.
    • Skin: strict photoprotection (broad-spectrum high-UVA sunscreen); potent TCS or topical tacrolimus (face); antihistamines for itch; hydroxychloroquine — note that drug eruptions to HCQ are more common in DM than in lupus; RCOphth retinal screening applies
    • Systemic (specialist): high-dose oral prednisolone (~1 mg/kg) ± IV methylprednisolone; steroid-sparing methotrexate, azathioprine or mycophenolate; IVIg (licensed in the UK for DM since 2021 — Octagam 10%); rituximab; JAK inhibitors (emerging, off-label); Anti-MDA5 ILD → combination immunosuppression urgently
    • Pharmacist checklist: Steroid Emergency Card, bone protection (calcium/vitamin D ± bisphosphonate — NOGG), gastroprotection as needed; PJP prophylaxis on combination therapy (specialist); inactivated vaccines (flu, COVID-19, pneumococcal; Shingrix); no live vaccines; methotrexate safety (the therapeutics notes see Dermatitis herpetiformis); review statins if weakness develops (statin-associated IMNM); support cancer screening appointments

    Red flags / refer

    Proximal weakness, dysphagia/aspiration, breathlessness/cough (ILD), very high CK, chest pain/palpitations (myocarditis), skin ulceration (MDA5), constitutional symptoms or other cancer red flags (NG12 — urgent cancer pathway).

    IP scope

    Recognition, CK/bloods, photoprotection and topical care, safe-prescribing support (steroids, MTX, HCQ shared care), and urgent referral. Diagnosis and immunosuppression are specialist.

    Worked cases

    Case 1: dermatomyositis, lung cancer and the striae case

    • Skin signs of dermatomyositis: heliotrope rash (± periorbital oedema); Gottron papules (pathognomonic) and Gottron sign over knuckles, elbows and knees; photodistributed poikiloderma — shawl, V and holster signs; facial erythema that involves the nasolabial folds; ragged cuticles and dilated nailfold capillaries; itchy scaly scalp; mechanic's hands (antisynthetase); calcinosis (juvenile); ulcers (anti-MDA5). Itch is prominent (see Dermatomyositis).
    • Association with underlying disease: Malignancy in adults — 3–7× risk (ovary, lung, pancreas, stomach, colorectal, breast, NHL; nasopharynx in South-East Asian patients), highest within 3 years of diagnosis, with anti-TIF1-γ or anti-NXP2; screen using the IMACS 2023 risk-stratified approach (see Dermatomyositis); Interstitial lung disease (anti-MDA5, antisynthetase antibodies), dysphagia, myocarditis; overlap with other connective tissue diseases; Juvenile DM is not cancer-associated
    • Skin signs of lung cancer: clubbing ± HPOA; cutaneous metastases (chest wall, scalp, umbilicus; carcinoma erysipeloides); SVC obstruction; Horner's syndrome (Pancoast); dermatomyositis; tripe palms and malignant AN; ectopic ACTH pigmentation; erythema gyratum repens, acquired hypertrichosis lanuginosa, Leser–Trélat, Trousseau's sign, Bazex syndrome; EGFR-inhibitor and checkpoint-inhibitor rashes. Know the NICE NG12 CXR triggers (see Skin signs of lung cancer).
    • 40-year-old woman with abdominal striae, amenorrhoea, weight gain, BP 142/92: Most probable diagnosis: Cushing's syndrome — the striae are wide, purple ("livid"), with menstrual disturbance, central weight gain and hypertension. Exclude exogenous glucocorticoids first (all routes, skin-lighteners, ritonavir/cobicistat + inhaled or nasal steroid); Bedside/clinical tests: BP; proximal myopathy (rise from a chair or squat without the arms); thin skin and bruising; width and colour of striae; facial plethora, fat pads; capillary glucose/urine dipstick; weight and waist; visual fields; Single primary-care investigation: the course teaches 24-h urinary free cortisol (or late-night salivary cortisol where available). UK practice most often uses the 1 mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test (cortisol < 50 nmol/L = normal suppression), and many pathways send suspected Cushing's straight to endocrinology. Any of the three is an acceptable first test; avoid the DST in women on oestrogen-containing contraception (see Cushing's syndrome — the striae case; see Where course teaching differs from UK guidance)

    Case 2: 41-year-old man with hyperhidrosis

    • Further history: onset, pattern (focal vs generalised, day vs night, symmetry), triggers, family history; drugs (SSRIs/SNRIs, TCAs, opioids, insulin/sulfonylureas, anabolic steroids or testosterone withdrawal, PPIs); alcohol and recreational drugs — specifically ask about cocaine (common and often not asked); hypogonadism (erectile dysfunction, low libido, reduced shaving, fatigue, gynaecomastia); acromegaly (ring or shoe size change, coarse features, headache); OSA (snoring, apnoeas, sleepiness); phaeochromocytoma (paroxysms of headache, palpitations, pallor), carcinoid (flushing, diarrhoea), infection or lymphoma (fever, night sweats, weight loss, lymph nodes, TB/HIV risk), hypoglycaemia, anxiety, GORD; impact (HDSS, DLQI)
    • Is primary hyperhidrosis likely? No. Primary focal hyperhidrosis needs ≥ 6 months of focal sweating plus ≥ 2 of: bilateral and symmetrical; impairs daily activity; ≥ 1 episode a week; onset before 25; positive family history; no sweating during sleep. New sweating at 41, at most times of day, with obesity, points to a secondary cause (see Hyperhidrosis — the 41-year-old man).

    • Further investigations: 9 am fasting total testosterone (repeat if low) with SHBG, LH/FSH and prolactin; fasting glucose and lipids; urine drug screen with consent; metanephrines if paroxysmal or hypertensive; IGF-1 if acromegalic; ESR/CRP, HIV test and CXR if systemic features; 24-h urinary 5-HIAA if flushing; sleep study if OSA likely (see Hyperhidrosis — the 41-year-old man).

    Case 3: Addison's disease and the 40-year-old man with new diabetes

    • Addison's — skin features: generalised bronze hyperpigmentation, most marked in palmar creases, knuckles, elbows, knees, scars, nipples, gums and buccal mucosa; darkening of naevi; vitiligo (autoimmune overlap); loss of axillary/pubic hair in women. On black or brown skin the change may be subtle — ask and look inside the mouth (NICE NG243).
    • Screening test in primary care: an 8–9 am serum cortisol with U&E (low sodium, high potassium), glucose and FBC (NICE NG243): < 150 nmol/L refer to endocrinology (and treat as crisis if unwell); 150–300 nmol/L uncertain, repeat or seek advice; > 300 nmol/L AI very unlikely. Never delay hydrocortisone 100 mg IM/IV in suspected adrenal crisis (see Addison's disease).
    • Why does pigmentation occur? Low cortisol removes negative feedback → more POMC → cleaved into ACTH and α-MSH, which stimulate MC1R on melanocytes → more melanin. Secondary (pituitary) adrenal insufficiency therefore causes pallor, not pigmentation.
    • One investigation: a fasting lipid profile — expect a turbid/milky (lipaemic) sample with very high triglycerides. The diagnosis is eruptive xanthomas from hypertriglyceridaemia driven by uncontrolled type 2 diabetes (± obesity, alcohol, drugs, familial). It matters because of the high risk of acute pancreatitis: TG > 20 mmol/L → urgent specialist referral (NICE NG238).
    • Differentials: molluscum contagiosum, dermatitis herpetiformis, folliculitis, acne/keloidal papules, papular granuloma annulare, xanthoma disseminatum/histiocytoses, papular sarcoidosis, lichen planus, insect bites. A shave biopsy (foamy macrophages ± extracellular lipid) is needed only if uncertain. "Nappy rash with cleansers and emollients" is not a diagnosis for persistent papules in an adult — re-examine and test.

    Case 4: acanthosis nigricans with PCOS, and dermatitis herpetiformis

    • Describe the rash: ill-defined, symmetrical, velvety brown-grey thickening with accentuated skin markings on the back of the neck, ± skin tags.
    • Likely diagnosis: acanthosis nigricans with polycystic ovary syndrome and insulin resistance (HAIR-AN phenotype). Rotterdam criteria: oligo-ovulation + clinical and biochemical hyperandrogenism (jawline hirsutism; testosterone 2.3 nmol/L, mildly raised). The COC had masked the symptoms. Normal TFT and prolactin help exclude other causes; check SHBG/free androgen index, 17-OH-progesterone (NC-CAH) and a 75 g OGTT or HbA1c (see Acanthosis nigricans and PCOS).
    • Associations of AN: obesity and insulin resistance (pre-diabetes, type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome), PCOS, other endocrinopathies (Cushing's, acromegaly, hypothyroidism), familial/syndromic forms, drugs (systemic glucocorticoids, nicotinic acid, insulin injection sites, oral contraceptives, protease inhibitors), and malignancy (see Acanthosis nigricans and PCOS).
    • Histology and malignancy link: papillomatosis and hyperkeratosis with only mild acanthosis, little or no extra melanin — the brown colour comes from the thickened keratin. Why some people get it: hyperinsulinaemia activates IGF-1 receptors on keratinocytes and fibroblasts (endocrine AN, mild, gradual); in malignant AN tumour-derived growth factors (TGF-α acting on EGFR) cause sudden, extensive, often mucosal or palmar ("tripe palms") disease in older, non-obese people — most often gastric adenocarcinoma; endocrine AN is mild, malignant AN is severe and rapid. Advise about diabetes risk and weight (see Acanthosis nigricans and PCOS).
    • Visible abnormalities: grouped small papules and vesicles on the extensor elbows, mostly excoriated, eroded and crusted (intact blisters are rare because of the intense itch); symmetrical, also knees and sacrum (see Dermatitis herpetiformis).
    • Clinical diagnosis: dermatitis herpetiformis, the skin form of coeliac disease (bloating, malaise and weight loss support it).
    • Confirm and further tests: Skin biopsy — perilesional skin for DIF (granular IgA in the dermal papillae), sent in Michel's medium or saline, not formalin, ± lesional biopsy in formalin for H&E; usually by dermatology; tTG-IgA with total IgA while the patient is eating gluten (more than one meal a day for ≥ 6 weeks; NICE NG20); IgG-based tests if IgA-deficient; EMA if equivocal; FBC, ferritin, folate, B12, LFT, calcium, vitamin D, TFT, HbA1c; G6PD before dapsone; bone density if indicated; Duodenal biopsy is usually not needed when skin DIF confirms DH (BSG), but refer to gastroenterology for significant GI symptoms or red flags (see Dermatitis herpetiformis)

    Case 5: 40-year-old man with breathlessness, weight loss, night sweats and painful shins

    • Describe the rash: bilateral, tender, warm, ill-defined red-violaceous nodules and plaques on the shins = erythema nodosum (see Sarcoidosis — Löfgren syndrome).
    • CXR: bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy (Scadding stage I) ± right paratracheal nodes.
    • Likely diagnosis and why: acute sarcoidosis, Löfgren-type — EN + BHL, with hypercalcaemia (2.7 mmol/L) from granuloma 1-α-hydroxylase activity and normocytic anaemia. But weight loss, night sweats and anaemia mean TB and lymphoma must be excluded: IGRA, sputum AFB/culture, HIV, LDH, film, HRCT and tissue (EBUS-TBNA) — classic Löfgren without B symptoms can skip tissue confirmation, this case cannot (see Sarcoidosis — Löfgren syndrome).
    • Related question — how an EN nodule evolves: bright red, raised, tender nodule (1–5 cm) → flattens over 1–2 weeks and turns dusky violaceous, then yellow-green like a bruise (erythema contusiformis) → fades without ulceration or scarring over 3–6 weeks (occasionally up to 8); crops recur; post-inflammatory pigmentation may linger in darker skin.
    • Related question — causes of EN: idiopathic (~30–50%); infection (streptococcal pharyngitis — the commonest identifiable cause; TB; Yersinia, Salmonella, Campylobacter; Chlamydia, Mycoplasma; EBV; hepatitis B; fungal infections such as coccidioidomycosis); sarcoidosis; IBD; pregnancy and oestrogen-containing contraception; drugs (sulfonamides, penicillins, oral contraceptives, bromides, vemurafenib/BRAF inhibitors); Behçet's; lymphoma and leukaemia (rare). First tests: throat swab/ASO titre, FBC, CRP/ESR, CXR, IGRA, pregnancy test, stool culture if diarrhoea, faecal calprotectin.
    • Related question — differentials of EN: cellulitis (unilateral, spreading, fever); superficial thrombophlebitis and DVT; erythema induratum (nodular vasculitis) — calves, ulcerates, linked to TB; other panniculitides (lupus profundus, pancreatic panniculitis, α1-antitrypsin deficiency, cold panniculitis); cutaneous polyarteritis nodosa (painful nodules with livedo); Sweet syndrome; insect bites; bruises/trauma; subcutaneous sarcoid; lymphoma. See the drug eruption notes for the full table.

    Practice case: 38-year-old woman with Crohn's on azathioprine

    • QA = 4, pyoderma gangrenosum. A minor graze (pathergy) → pustule within days → rapidly enlarging, very painful ulcer in a woman with Crohn's disease, low-grade fever and raised CRP/ESR (see Pyoderma gangrenosum): 1. Atypical mycobacterial infection: evolves over weeks to months; it should still be excluded (tissue culture) because she is on azathioprine; 2. S. aureus infection: would not usually produce a violaceous undermined ulcer this painful and fast, and would respond to antibiotics; swab anyway; 3. Martorell ulcer: an ischaemic ulcer of long-standing hypertension ± diabetes, usually older patients, lateral/posterior leg; her BP is 125/80; 5. SCC: would not reach this size in 4 weeks, is rarely this painful and lacks an inflammatory violaceous border (see Pyoderma gangrenosum)
    • QB = 2, diagnosis based on clinical and laboratory criteria. There is no confirmatory test; PG is a diagnosis of exclusion using the Su criteria (the course answer); current UK specialist practice uses the Delphi consensus criteria (see Pyoderma gangrenosum): 4/5. Skin biopsy for histology (and tissue culture): an essential part of the work-up — mainly to exclude infection, vasculitis and malignancy — but histology is non-specific, so biopsy alone does not confirm PG (and a biopsy of the edge is the major Delphi criterion; see Where course teaching differs from UK guidance); 1. ABPI: assesses arterial disease and safety of compression; no diagnostic role in PG; 3. Nerve conduction studies/EMG: for neuropathic ulcers
    • QC = 1, myeloid blood dyscrasias (AML, myelodysplasia, myeloproliferative disorders; also IgA monoclonal gammopathy/myeloma). Other well-described associations are rheumatoid and other inflammatory arthritis and IBD. Eosinophilic asthma, penicillin antibiotics, pregnancy and ischaemic heart disease are not recognised associations (see Pyoderma gangrenosum).
    • UK additions for this patient: exclude infection first (swab, FBC — azathioprine can cause neutropenia — CRP, tissue culture via biopsy); urgent dermatology referral and tell her IBD team; no debridement; analgesia (avoid NSAIDs in Crohn's); non-adherent dressings; check her Crohn's activity. Expect prednisolone or ciclosporin (STOP GAP), given active IBD, an anti-TNF (infliximab/adalimumab); steroid safety, interaction and vaccination checks.

    The course summary lists the week's themes: Cushing's, dermatomyositis, sarcoid and dermatitis herpetiformis (diagnosis, differentials, investigations and associations); Addison's features and screening; eruptive xanthomas and their pancreatitis risk; and pyoderma gangrenosum, a painful ulcerating disease whose commonest association is IBD. The common thread: name the skin sign, think of the internal disease, do the first test, and refer at the right speed.

    Step 9 of 9 · 1 min

    Quick self-check

    1. Q1When should you think of Dermatomyositis?Reveal

      Violaceous eyelids, scaly papules over the knuckles, a red V or shawl on the chest and back, ragged cuticles, with or without proximal muscle weakness.

    2. Q2What drives it?Reveal

      Autoimmune damage to small vessels of skin and muscle; myositis-specific antibodies predict cancer (TIF1-γ, NXP-2) or lung disease (MDA5).

    3. Q3What is the backbone of care?Reveal

      CK, ANA and myositis antibodies, urgent rheumatology/dermatology referral; age-appropriate cancer screening; strict photoprotection.

    4. Q4What must you never miss?Reveal

      Underlying malignancy (ovary, lung, gut, breast); interstitial lung disease; swallowing and respiratory muscle weakness.

    5. Q5Name a red flag that needs escalation.Reveal

      Difficulty swallowing, breathlessness or rapidly worsening weakness: same-day assessment.
