    Drug eruptionsModule 1 · Week 3

    Approach to a suspected drug eruption

    How to work out whether a rash is drug-induced: the drug timeline, morphology, severity markers (SCARs), which drug to stop, documenting allergy properly and reporting on the Yellow Card scheme.

    Also calleddrug rashadverse drug reactiondrug allergyYellow CardNaranjoSCAR+1
    Related pagesMorbilliform drug eruption & drug urticariaSJS/TEN and drug-induced blisteringDRESS (drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms)AGEP (acute generalised exanthematous pustulosis)Fixed drug eruption

    Key facts

    • Drug reactions are common: first classify the morphology (maculopapular, urticarial, blistering, pustular, fixed, photosensitive, lichenoid).
    • Build a drug timeline: everything started in the last 8 weeks, including OTC and herbal products.
    • Latency clues: urticaria/anaphylaxis minutes–hours; AGEP hours–2 days; MDE 4–14 days; SJS/TEN 4–28 days; DRESS 2–8 weeks.
    • Red flags: mucosal involvement, skin pain, blisters, facial oedema, fever, organ dysfunction: same-day referral.
    • Stop the likely culprit, document the reaction precisely, Yellow Card serious reactions, and refer for allergy testing when it matters.

    Overview

    Cutaneous ADRs affect ~2–3% of hospital inpatients. Most (~90%) are benign exanthems, urticaria or FDE. About 2% are SCARs (SJS/TEN, DRESS, AGEP), which are potentially fatal. Antibiotics (aminopenicillins, sulfonamides), anticonvulsants (carbamazepine, phenytoin, lamotrigine), allopurinol, NSAIDs (oxicams) and contrast media dominate the culprit lists.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    Myths to address

    • "Any rash on amoxicillin = penicillin allergy": many are viral or non-allergic; mislabelled allergy harms future care.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    Drug reactions follow different immune pathways, which explain their timing and appearance.

    1. 1

      Type I (IgE)

      Mast cell degranulation within minutes: urticaria, angioedema, anaphylaxis.

    2. 2

      Type IV T-cell reactions

      Delayed days to weeks: maculopapular (IVb/c), SJS/TEN (IVc), AGEP (IVd), DRESS (IVb).

    3. 3

      p-i concept

      Some drugs bind HLA or T-cell receptors directly; HLA alleles predict risk (HLA-B*58:01, B*15:02, B*57:01).

    4. 4

      Non-immune

      Phototoxicity, pigmentation and pharmacological effects (NSAID-exacerbated urticaria).

    How it is caused

    • Immune-mediated (Gell & Coombs, revised): Type I (IgE): urticaria, angioedema, anaphylaxis; minutes to hours; Type II: drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia/pemphigus; Type III (immune complex): serum-sickness-like reaction, vasculitis (the vasculitis notes); Type IV (T-cell, delayed), subdivided:; IVa (Th1) — eczematous; IVb (Th2/eosinophils) — DRESS, maculopapular; IVc (cytotoxic CD8 T cells, granulysin) — SJS/TEN, FDE; IVd (neutrophils, IL-8) — AGEP
    • Non-immune: pharmacological (bradykinin — ACE-i angioedema; COX-1 inhibition — NSAID urticaria/angioedema); phototoxicity; direct mast-cell activation (opioids, vancomycin "red man" — MRGPRX2); deposition (amiodarone, minocycline pigmentation); overdose/cumulative toxicity
    • Host factors: HLA alleles (see Stevens–Johnson syndrome / toxic epidermal necrolysis), viral infection (EBV + amoxicillin; HIV ↑ sulfonamide reactions; HHV-6 reactivation in DRESS), older age, renal impairment, autoimmune disease.

    Types to recognise

    • Urticarial / angioedema

      Minutes–hours (IgE); days (serum-sickness-like) Site: Penicillins/cephalosporins, NSAIDs/aspirin, opioids, contrast; ACE-i (angioedema, weeks–years).

    • Morbilliform (exanthem)

      4–14 days (sooner on re-exposure) Site: Aminopenicillins, cephalosporins, sulfonamides (co-trimoxazole), anticonvulsants, allopurinol.

    • Photosensitive (face, V of neck, backs of hands, sparing behind ears/under chin)

      Days–weeks of sun exposure Site: Thiazides, tetracyclines (doxycycline), amiodarone, quinolones, NSAIDs (piroxicam), voriconazole, phenothiazines, PPIs/terbinafine (SCLE).

    • Blistering

      Hours–days (FDE), 4–28 days (SJS/TEN), weeks–months (pseudoporphyria, drug-induced BP) Site: SJS/TEN: allopurinol, carbamazepine, lamotrigine, phenytoin, sulfonamides, oxicam NSAIDs, nevirapine. FDE: NSAIDs, co-trimoxazole, tetracyclines, paracetamol. DPP-4 inhibitors (gliptins) → bullous pemphigoid. Naproxen → pseudoporphyria. Furosemide/ACE-i/PD-1 inhibitors → BP.

    • Pustular (AGEP)

      <48 h (often hours) Site: Aminopenicillins, macrolides, pristinamycin, hydroxychloroquine, diltiazem, terbinafine.

    • Fever + rash + organ involvement (DRESS)

      2–8 weeks Site: Allopurinol, carbamazepine, phenytoin, lamotrigine, sulfonamides, dapsone, minocycline, vancomycin.

    • Lichenoid

      Months Site: Thiazides, β-blockers, ACE-i, antimalarials, gold, PD-1 inhibitors.

    • Vasculitic purpura

      1–3 weeks Site: Antibiotics, NSAIDs, allopurinol (see the vasculitis notes).

    Diagnosis and investigations

    1. Drug chart/timeline. List every drug, with start/stop/dose-change dates, for the previous 8 weeks (up to 3 months for DRESS). Include OTC, herbal, supplements, recreational drugs, contrast, vaccines and depot/long-acting drugs. Plot the rash onset against each one. A drug started >8 weeks before the rash (and taken without problem) is less likely; one started after onset can be excluded.
    2. Previous exposure: a previous reaction to the same or a cross-reacting drug; re-exposure shortens the latency.
    3. Morphology and extent: pattern (above), mucosal involvement, skin pain, Nikolsky sign, blisters/detachment (% BSA), pustules, facial oedema, lymphadenopathy, purpura.
    4. Systemic features: fever, lymphadenopathy, organ dysfunction.
    5. Investigations: FBC with differential (eosinophilia → DRESS; lymphopenia → SJS/TEN; atypical lymphocytes; neutrophilia → AGEP); U&E and LFTs (DRESS hepatitis/nephritis); CRP, glucose and bicarbonate (SCORTEN); Urinalysis (vasculitis); CK; TFTs (DRESS late effect); Viral serology/PCR (EBV, HHV-6, Mycoplasma in EM/SJS-like mucositis); Skin biopsy (H&E ± direct immunofluorescence — confirms SJS/TEN, AGEP, vasculitis; excludes AIBD/SSSS); Mast-cell tryptase within 1–4 h of suspected anaphylaxis; Later, by the allergy service: skin prick/intradermal tests, patch tests (FDE lesional patch, DRESS/AGEP delayed patch), lymphocyte transformation tests, and oral drug provocation for low-risk penicillin allergy. Never rechallenge after SJS/TEN or DRESS.
    6. Causality tools: Naranjo — a general ADR probability scale (10 questions; ≥9 definite, 5–8 probable, 1–4 possible, ≤0 doubtful); ALDEN — specific to SJS/TEN (see Stevens–Johnson syndrome / toxic epidermal necrolysis); RegiSCAR — DRESS scoring

    Differential diagnoses

    Viral exanthem (EBV, parvovirus, measles, COVID-19), Mycoplasma-associated mucositis (RIME), graft-versus-host disease, staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome (SSSS — superficial split, children/renal failure), autoimmune bullous disease, Kawasaki disease, scarlet fever, toxic shock syndrome, acute cutaneous lupus, pustular psoriasis (vs AGEP), cellulitis (vs FDE/EN).

    Management

    • Stop the probable culprit (and any non-essential drugs). Choose a structurally unrelated alternative (e.g. levetiracetam rather than another aromatic anticonvulsant).
    • Benign exanthem: emollient, moderate–potent topical steroid, sedating or non-sedating antihistamine for itch. The rash may worsen for 1–3 days after stopping, then fades with desquamation over 1–2 weeks.
    • Record the reaction (NICE CG183 Drug allergy): drug name, reaction description, date, severity, and whether it is an allergy or an intolerance. Tell the patient in writing, and make sure the record is shared (GP record, hospital discharge letter, community pharmacy PMR).
    • Report via the MHRA Yellow Card scheme (https://yellowcard.mhra.gov.uk): all suspected serious reactions; any reaction to ▼ black triangle drugs; and any reaction in children. SJS/TEN, DRESS and AGEP should always be reported.
    • Penicillin allergy de-labelling: ~90% of labels are inaccurate. Refer suitable patients to allergy or local de-labelling pathways (BSACI 2022). A "rash on amoxicillin during glandular fever" is not true allergy.

    IP scope

    IPs can stop and substitute drugs, treat benign exanthems/urticaria symptomatically, document allergies and submit Yellow Cards. SCARs need emergency admission (dermatology/burns/ITU). Allergy testing and drug provocation are specialist.

    Worked cases

    Case 1: memorable drug reaction

    There is no single answer. Strong answers covered: timing relative to a new drug, morphology (pattern recognition, see Approach to a suspected drug eruption), resolution on withdrawal, recurrence on re-exposure, skin of colour (erythema looks violaceous/grey, and PIH dominates), and patient education (name the drug, avoid it, carry an alert, read OTC labels).

    Case 2: 76-year-old woman on a medical ward, multiple drugs

    • History: a dated drug chart for 8 weeks: new drugs, dose changes, OTC/herbal, contrast, IV antibiotics, and drugs given in other settings (ED, theatre); previous drug reactions and tolerance; onset and evolution of the rash; itch vs pain, fever, mucosal/eye symptoms; viral illness; renal/hepatic function (drug accumulation)
    • Examination: morphology and distribution (urticarial / morbilliform / photo-distributed / blistering / pustular / purpuric), mucosae, % BSA, Nikolsky sign, facial oedema, lymph nodes, temperature, organ signs.
    • Investigations: FBC + differential (eosinophils, lymphocytes, neutrophils), U&E, LFT, CRP, urinalysis, ± tryptase, viral PCR, skin biopsy (H&E + DIF). Score causality with Naranjo (general) or ALDEN (SJS/TEN). Later testing by the allergy service (patch tests, specific IgE, provocation).
    • Likely candidates (typical inpatient drugs): Urticarial: β-lactam antibiotics, NSAIDs/aspirin, opioids (morphine, codeine — pseudo-allergic), contrast media, vancomycin, ACE-i (angioedema); Photosensitive: thiazides/loop diuretics (pseudoporphyria — furosemide), amiodarone, doxycycline, quinolones (ciprofloxacin), NSAIDs, phenothiazines, PPIs (SCLE); Blistering: in an older patient think drug-induced bullous pemphigoid (gliptins, loop diuretics, spironolactone, PD-1 inhibitors), SJS/TEN (allopurinol, anticonvulsants, co-trimoxazole, oxicams), FDE/GBFDE, pseudoporphyria (furosemide, naproxen) and linear IgA disease (vancomycin)

    Case 2: EM vs SJS vs TEN

    See the table in and the classification in Stevens–Johnson syndrome / toxic epidermal necrolysis:

    • EM: HSV-triggered, typical targets, acral, mild mucosa, good prognosis; treat HSV (aciclovir prophylaxis if recurrent).
    • SJS (<10%) / overlap / TEN (>30%): drug-induced CD8/granulysin necrosis, atypical targets, severe mucositis. Stop the drug, admit, supportive care, ± ciclosporin/IVIg.

    Case 3

    • Case 1: ACE-inhibitor (ramipril)-induced, bradykinin-mediated angioedema, possibly triggered by minor surgery. No useful response to antihistamine or steroid. Investigations: clinical; C4/C1-INH if atypical, tryptase if anaphylaxis is possible. Management: airway assessment, stop ramipril permanently (class allergy), consider an ARB with caution or a CCB, avoid gliptins/sacubitril, Yellow Card, explain that recurrences can happen for weeks (see ACE-inhibitor (bradykinin-mediated) angioedema).
    • Case 2: erythema nodosum, most likely COC-induced. Exclude infection (strep), sarcoid/TB (CXR), IBD and pregnancy, and exclude DVT/thrombophlebitis given the unilateral leg + COC + tranexamic acid; cellulitis is a valid differential. Treat: stop the COC (alternative contraception/cycle control), rest, elevation, NSAID analgesia; review; biopsy if atypical (see Drug-induced erythema nodosum).
    • Case 3: Question 1 — main causes of photosensitivity (see Drug-induced pigmentation — amiodarone); Question 2 — the visible abnormality is blue-grey (slate) pigmentation of sun-exposed facial skin ± phototoxic erythema; Question 3 — amiodarone-induced phototoxicity and pigmentation (UVA-absorbing drug → ROS; lipofuscin-like drug–lipid deposits in dermal macrophages; long half-life); Question 4 — cardiology liaison (dose reduction/alternative), strict UVA photoprotection, monitoring (TFT/LFT/eyes/lung), counselling that the pigment fades slowly, Yellow Card (see Drug-induced pigmentation — amiodarone)

    Case 4

    • Case 1: drug-induced photosensitivity (phototoxic) from bendroflumethiazide. Investigate clinically ± ANA/Ro (SCLE — omeprazole), porphyrins if blistering; phototesting/photopatch rarely. Treat: stop the thiazide → amlodipine; topical steroid, emollient, antihistamine; strict photoprotection and education; review diabetes (HbA1c 78) and the PPI indication (see Drug-induced photosensitivity — thiazide case).
    • Case 2: fixed drug eruption from diclofenac (taken during two consecutive periods), misdiagnosed as a cold sore. Stop/avoid diclofenac (± related NSAIDs), alternative dysmenorrhoea treatment, topical steroid, document and report (see Fixed drug eruption).

    Practice case: 56-year-old man, 60% detachment

    • QA = TEN (>30% detachment + severe mucositis + systemic illness). course follow-up "does TEN involve >10%?" — >10% is the SCORTEN item and the lower bound for overlap. TEN is >30%; SJS <10%; overlap 10–30%.
    • QB = 5–28 days (typical latency from starting the culprit; the ALDEN "suggestive" window). BAD quotes 4–28 days.
    • QC = carbamazepine. Worked ALDEN, with index day = fever onset 5 days ago:

    Referral

    • Same day

      Skin pain, blisters, skin detachment or mucosal erosions: possible SJS/TEN, stop the drug and admit the same day.

    • Routine

      Fever, facial swelling, lymph nodes and abnormal bloods 2–8 weeks after a new drug: possible DRESS.

    • 999 / A&E

      Swelling of lips, tongue or throat, or breathing difficulty: 999.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Skin pain, blisters, skin detachment or mucosal erosions: possible SJS/TEN, stop the drug and admit the same day.
    • Fever, facial swelling, lymph nodes and abnormal bloods 2–8 weeks after a new drug: possible DRESS.
    • Swelling of lips, tongue or throat, or breathing difficulty: 999.

    Red flags / refer

    Mucosal involvement (≥2 sites); skin pain or burning; dusky/targetoid lesions or blisters; Nikolsky sign; fever; facial oedema; lymphadenopathy; abnormal LFTs/renal function; eosinophilia; widespread pustules; signs of anaphylaxis (call 999, IM adrenaline 500 micrograms in adults).

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • SJS/TEN, DRESS, anaphylaxis; mislabelled allergies.

    Prognosis

    • Most simple drug rashes resolve in 1–2 weeks after stopping the drug.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • "Any rash on any drug — take a dated drug history, then think SCAR red flags."

    • Give the patient the drug name, the reaction and the date in writing; advise wearing a medical alert if severe; check OTC products (ibuprofen) and combination products contain none of the culprit.

    • Write the reaction, not just "allergy", in the record (e.g. "amoxicillin — maculopapular rash day 7, 2026, no mucosal involvement").

    Self-check quiz

    5 questions · optional
    1. Q1When should you think of Approach to a suspected drug eruption?Reveal

      Any new rash in someone who started, changed or restarted a medicine in the last 8 weeks.

    2. Q2What drives it?Reveal

      Immune reactions (type I to IV) or non-immune effects (phototoxicity, deposition, pharmacology) of a drug or its metabolites.

    3. Q3What is the backbone of care?Reveal

      Build a drug timeline, name the morphology, check for SCAR red flags, stop the most likely culprit, record the reaction precisely (NICE CG183) and report.

    4. Q4What must you never miss?Reveal

      SJS/TEN, DRESS, AGEP, anaphylaxis and angioedema; vasculitis with organ involvement.

    5. Q5Name a red flag that needs escalation.Reveal

      Skin pain, blisters, skin detachment or mucosal erosions: possible SJS/TEN, stop the drug and admit the same day.
