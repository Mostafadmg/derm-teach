    Blistering diseasesModule 4 · Week 6

    Drug-induced blistering (SJS/TEN, FDE, AGEP)

    Drugs cause a range of blistering reactions, from a single recurring fixed drug eruption to SJS/TEN, which is a medical emergency. Stop the culprit early, record the allergy and report on a Yellow Card.

    Also calledSJSTENStevens-Johnsontoxic epidermal necrolysisfixed drug eruptionAGEP+6

    Key facts

    • Blistering, dusky or pustular rash 4–28 days after starting a drug (days for AGEP; hours for repeat fixed drug eruption).
    • T-cell-mediated keratinocyte death (SJS/TEN, FDE), neutrophilic reactions (AGEP) or drug-triggered autoantibodies (linear IgA, pemphigoid, pemphigus).
    • Identify and stop the culprit, admit SJS/TEN, document the allergy clearly, Yellow Card, and screen high-risk drugs (HLA-B*1502 before carbamazepine in at-risk groups).
    • SJS/TEN: skin pain, fever, mucosal erosions and Nikolsky-positive skin detachment.

    Overview

    This is a group of reactions in which a drug causes vesicles, bullae, pustules or epidermal necrosis. They range from trivial (localised FDE) to life-threatening (TEN). For the IP, drug causation is the main reason to know them.

    SJS/TEN in depth

    SJS and TEN are one disease spectrum. They are rare, life-threatening mucocutaneous reactions, almost always drug-induced, with widespread keratinocyte death and epidermal detachment. They are classified by the maximum % BSA detached (Bastuji-Garin):

    CategoryDetached / detachable BSA
    SJS<10%
    SJS/TEN overlap10–30%
    TEN>30%

    Incidence is ~1–6 per million per year (higher in HIV). Mortality is ~5–10% for SJS and ~30% (25–50%) for TEN; it is higher in older people and those with comorbidity.

    Erythema multiforme (EM) is a different disease. It is usually triggered by HSV (or Mycoplasma), and drugs cause it only rarely. It gives typical raised 3-zone target lesions on the acral skin, with or without mild mucosal involvement ("EM major"), and has a good prognosis (see the inflammatory rash pages notes).

    SJS/TEN in depth

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    How it is caused

    • A type IVc reaction: drug-specific cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and NK cells release granulysin (the key mediator), perforin/granzyme B and soluble Fas ligand → widespread keratinocyte apoptosis/necrosis → full-thickness epidermal necrosis with subepidermal separation.
    • HLA associations (pharmacogenomics), with UK screening implications:
    DrugAllelePopulationUK practice
    Carbamazepine (also oxcarbazepine, phenytoin, fosphenytoin)HLA-B*15:02Han Chinese, Thai, Malaysian, other SE AsianMHRA/BNF/SmPC: test before starting carbamazepine in people of these ancestries; do not start if positive unless no alternative
    CarbamazepineHLA-A*31:01Europeans, JapaneseAssociated with DRESS, MPE and SJS/TEN; testing considered (SmPC) — not routine in the UK
    AllopurinolHLA-B*58:01Han Chinese, Korean, Thai; also African ancestryStart low (100 mg or less in CKD) and titrate; testing where available in high-risk groups (BSR gout guidance)
    AbacavirHLA-B*57:01AllMandatory pre-treatment test (hypersensitivity syndrome)
    • High-risk ("notorious") drugs (EuroSCAR/RegiSCAR, BAD 2016): allopurinol (the commonest in Europe), carbamazepine, lamotrigine (especially fast titration or co-prescribed with valproate), phenytoin, phenobarbital, sulfonamides (co-trimoxazole, sulfasalazine), oxicam NSAIDs (meloxicam, piroxicam), nevirapine. Lower risk: other antibiotics (aminopenicillins, cephalosporins, quinolones), paracetamol (controversial), PPIs. ~5–15% of cases are idiopathic or Mycoplasma-related (especially in children).
    • Latency: typically 4–28 days after the first dose (BAD 2016; ALDEN "suggestive" window 5–28 days). It is shorter on re-exposure (hours to 2 days). Drugs used for many months are rarely culprits, and anticonvulsants mostly cause it within the first 8 weeks.

    Clinical features

    SJS/TEN classification by detached BSA: SJS <10%, SJS/TEN overlap 10–30%, TEN >30% (Module 4 exam Q27, 40% = TEN).

    • Prodrome: fever, sore throat and eye symptoms 1–3 days before the skin.
    • Then painful dusky macules or atypical flat targets, which form bullae and sheet-like detachment with a positive Nikolsky sign.
    • ≥2 mucosal sites are involved (oral haemorrhagic crusting of the lips, conjunctivitis, genital; photo E02).

    SSSS is the toxin-mediated mimic in young children: superficial peeling, mucosa spared, and the child is irritable and tender.

    SJS/TEN in depth

    • Prodrome (1–3 days): fever, malaise, sore throat, conjunctivitis/photophobia, painful swallowing — often treated as an infection, which leads to new drugs (NSAIDs, antibiotics) that confuse the timeline.
    • Skin: painful dusky-red macules and atypical flat targets (two zones, purpuric centres) on the trunk and face, spreading and coalescing. Flaccid blisters and sheet-like detachment follow ("wet cigarette paper"); Nikolsky positive.
    • Mucosae (≥2 sites in >90%): haemorrhagic crusted lips, oral erosions, conjunctivitis/pseudomembranes, genital and urethral erosions; also respiratory/GI epithelium.
    • Systemic: fever, tachycardia, fluid and protein loss, hypothermia, sepsis (skin barrier lost), acute kidney injury, hepatitis (raised ALT), anaemia, lymphopenia (common), neutropenia (poor prognosis), and respiratory involvement (bronchial sloughing).
    • Sequelae (in up to 50–90% of survivors): ocular dry eye, trichiasis, symblepharon and corneal scarring/blindness; pigmentary change; nail loss/dystrophy; scarring; genital adhesions; oesophageal/urethral strictures; psychological trauma.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    Diagnosis is mostly clinical, with urgent specialist confirmation and frozen-section or standard biopsy. SCORTEN (calculated in the first 24 h) predicts mortality. Its 7 points are:

    1. age ≥40
    2. malignancy
    3. HR ≥120
    4. BSA ≥10%
    5. urea >10 mmol/L
    6. bicarbonate <20
    7. glucose >14

    For drug causation use ALDEN (for SJS/TEN). Patch testing is useful later for FDE and AGEP. For DRESS (the systemic DDx), RegiSCAR.

    SJS/TEN in depth

    • Clinical diagnosis + urgent skin biopsy: H&E shows full-thickness epidermal necrosis with sparse dermal infiltrate. Take a second sample for direct immunofluorescence (negative) to exclude autoimmune bullous disease.
    • Severity at admission — SCORTEN (calculate within 24 h, repeat on day 3), 1 point each:
    SCORTEN itemPoint if
    Age≥40 years
    MalignancyPresent (active)
    Heart rate≥120/min
    Epidermal detachment at day 1>10% BSA

    Serum glucose>14 mmol/L
    Serum bicarbonate<20 mmol/L

    (Bastuji-Garin et al., 2000. It is endorsed by BAD 2016 and tends to over-predict mortality in modern care; ABCD-10 is an alternative.)

    • Drug causality — ALDEN (Algorithm of Drug causality for Epidermal Necrolysis; Sassolas et al., 2010). Score every drug taken in the 8 weeks before the index day (the first day of symptoms, including the prodrome):
    CriterionScoring (summary)
    1. Delay from first intake to index day5–28 days +3; 29–56 days +2; 1–4 days +1; >56 days −1; started on or after the index day −3 (previous reaction to the same drug: 1–4 days +3, 5–56 days +1)
    2. Drug present in the body on the index dayDefinite 0; doubtful −1; excluded −3 (stopped >5 half-lives before the index day, with normal clearance)
    3. Prechallenge/rechallengeSJS/TEN with same drug +4; similar drug or other reaction with the same drug +2; other reaction with a similar drug +1; no known exposure 0; previous exposure without reaction −2
    4. DechallengeStopped (or unknown) 0; continued without harm −2
    5. Type of drug (notoriety)High-risk ("strongly associated") +3; associated +2; suspected +1; unknown 0; not suspected −1
    6. Other causeIf any drug has an intermediate score >3, subtract 1 from each of the other drugs
    Interpretation<0 very unlikely; 0–1 unlikely; 2–3 possible; 4–5 probable; ≥6 very probable
    • Baseline tests: FBC, U&E, LFT, glucose, bicarbonate/venous gas, CRP, coagulation, Mg/phosphate, albumin; skin, mucosal and blood cultures; Mycoplasma serology/PCR (in children/young adults); CXR (respiratory); HIV test.

    Differential diagnoses

    • SSSS (child, superficial, no mucosa)
    • EM major (HSV, acral targets, <10%)
    • generalised bullous FDE
    • AGEP (pustules, not detachment)
    • DRESS (facial oedema, eosinophilia, organ involvement, latency 2–8 weeks)
    • GVHD
    • paraneoplastic pemphigus
    • linear IgA (TEN-like)
    • phototoxic reactions
    • burns

    Management

    • Suspected SJS/TEN or extensive or mucosal blistering: STOP all non-essential drugs immediately, especially the likely culprits started 4–28 days ago; 999 / same-day ED for transfer to a burns or dermatology intensive unit (UK SJS/TEN guideline, Creamer 2016). Don't wait for a GP appointment; Ophthalmology within 24 h, and urology/gynaecology/oral medicine input; Specialist adjuncts: ciclosporin, IVIG (the evidence for blocking Fas-FasL is mixed and UK guidance does not routinely recommend it), steroids, and anti-TNF (etanercept, being studied)
    • AGEP: stop the drug, give supportive care and a potent TCS. Refer the same day if febrile or extensive.
    • FDE: stop and avoid the culprit; a potent TCS for 1–2 weeks; document the specific drug.
    • Drug-induced BP or pemphigus: stop the culprit and follow the specialist pathway. Recovery may take months.
    • Allergy documentation: Record the exact drug and reaction in the GP record (SNOMED) and on the patient's allergy list; Report to MHRA Yellow Card (all serious reactions; anything involving a black-triangle drug); For SJS/TEN: lifelong avoidance of the culprit and structurally related drugs, and advice for first-degree relatives about HLA risk. A MedicAlert bracelet is reasonable
    • Prevention (pharmacist role): HLA-B*15:02 screening before carbamazepine in people of Han Chinese or Thai ancestry (BNF/MHRA); Start allopurinol low (100 mg) and titrate in CKD; Follow the lamotrigine titration schedule (halve doses with valproate), and counsel all new starters to stop and seek help with any rash, fever or mouth ulcers in the first 8 weeks

    SJS/TEN in depth

    Primary-care/community actions (you will see the prodrome or early rash):

    1. Suspect it: a new drug in the past 8 weeks + skin pain + dusky/target lesions or blisters + mucositis + fever.
    2. Stop all suspect drugs immediately. Delay in withdrawal raises mortality; early withdrawal of drugs with a short half-life improves survival (Garcia-Doval 2000).
    3. Same-day emergency admission (999/ED with a call to on-call dermatology). The BAD advises transfer to a specialist centre (burns unit/ITU or dermatology with specialist MDT) when BSA >10% or the patient is unwell. Do not start oral steroids or antibiotics in primary care "to cover".
    4. Document the culprit(s) prominently, report via Yellow Card, and tell the patient and family that the drug and structurally related drugs must be avoided for life (aromatic anticonvulsants carbamazepine/phenytoin/phenobarbital/oxcarbazepine/lamotrigine cross-react; avoid all sulfonamide antibiotics after co-trimoxazole SJS). Advise a medical alert bracelet/card.
    5. First-degree relatives may share the HLA risk — mention this for carbamazepine/allopurinol.

    Hospital care (BAD 2016 UK guideline — for teaching):

    • Supportive care is the cornerstone: warmed environment (25–28 °C); pressure-relieving/air-fluidised mattress; barrier nursing; fluids guided by urine output (avoid over-resuscitation); nutrition (NG feed); analgesia (incl. for dressing changes); VTE prophylaxis; stress-ulcer prophylaxis; repeated swabs, with no prophylactic antibiotics
    • Skin: leave detached epidermis in place as a biological dressing; decompress blisters; whole-body greasy emollient (e.g. 50% white soft paraffin/50% liquid paraffin); non-adherent silicone dressings.
    • Eyes: ophthalmology review within 24 h of admission, then daily. Lubricants, topical steroid/antibiotic drops, lysis of adhesions (amniotic membrane in severe disease).
    • Mouth: white soft paraffin to the lips, benzydamine/mucoprotectant mouthwashes, antiseptic chlorhexidine mouthwash, and a corticosteroid mouthwash (e.g. betamethasone soluble tablet in water) — doses per BAD/BNF.
    • Urogenital: review; catheter; potent topical steroid to non-eroded mucosa; dressings to prevent adhesions.
    • Immunomodulation: no treatment is proven (BAD 2016, Cochrane 2022). Options used in UK centres, decided by the specialist team: ciclosporin (~3 mg/kg/day for 7–10 days), which has the most observational support; IVIg; short pulsed corticosteroids early; etanercept (emerging RCT data)

    IP scope

    Recognition, stopping drugs, emergency referral, documentation and Yellow Card are core IP responsibilities. All treatment is in secondary care. After discharge: lifelong avoidance list, eye follow-up, psychological support, and checks on all new prescriptions and OTC sales (e.g. no ibuprofen if an NSAID was implicated).

    Referral

    • 999 / A&E

      Skin pain, fever, dusky targets, mucosal erosions or skin detachment: SJS/TEN, stop all non-essential drugs and call 999 / admit (burns unit or ICU).

    • Same day

      Widespread sterile pustules with fever after a new drug (AGEP): same-day assessment.

    • Routine

      Rash with fever, facial swelling, lymph nodes and abnormal liver tests: DRESS.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Skin pain, fever, dusky targets, mucosal erosions or skin detachment: SJS/TEN, stop all non-essential drugs and call 999 / admit (burns unit or ICU).
    • Widespread sterile pustules with fever after a new drug (AGEP): same-day assessment.
    • Rash with fever, facial swelling, lymph nodes and abnormal liver tests: DRESS.

    Red flags / refer

    Any suspected SJS/TEN → same-day admission. Red flags: skin pain, ≥2 mucosal sites, dusky/atypical targets, Nikolsky sign, fever, conjunctivitis with rash, BSA detachment, dehydration, inability to eat/drink.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • Ask "What new medicine did you start in the last 8 weeks?" at every acute rash with fever, skin pain or mucosal involvement.

    • Skin pain out of proportion + mucosal erosions + dusky skin = SJS/TEN until proven otherwise.

    • Paracetamol can cause FDE, so don't assume over-the-counter means innocent. Always ask about OTC and herbal products.

    • "Pain out of proportion + mouth/eye involvement + a new drug = SJS until proven otherwise."

    • The prodrome is treated as an infection, so the drugs given for it (NSAIDs, antibiotics) appear on the chart just before the rash — ALDEN's "started after index day −3" stops you blaming them.

    • Prevention at prescribing: test for HLA-B*15:02 before carbamazepine in people of SE Asian ancestry. Start allopurinol low (≤100 mg, lower in CKD) and titrate. Titrate lamotrigine exactly as in the SmPC (halve the dose with valproate) and warn about rash in the first 8 weeks. Avoid co-trimoxazole where alternatives exist.

    • Patient leaflet: "If you develop a rash with blisters, sore mouth or sore eyes, stop the medicine and seek urgent help."
