    Drug-induced photosensitivity

    Many common drugs make the skin burn or react in sunlight. Phototoxic reactions look like exaggerated sunburn; photoallergic ones look like eczema. A medication review is the key intervention.

    Also calledphototoxicphotoallergicdoxycycline sunburnthiazide photosensitivityamiodaroneNSAID photosensitivity+2
    Related pagesPolymorphic light eruptionPhytophotodermatitisChronic actinic dermatitisDrug-induced pigmentation (amiodarone, minocycline)Approach to a suspected drug eruption

    Key facts

    • Drug photosensitivity is a rash on sun-exposed skin caused by a drug plus UV (mostly UVA).
    • Phototoxic (commonest, dose-related, 'exaggerated sunburn') versus photoallergic (eczematous, immune).
    • Common culprits: tetracyclines (doxycycline), thiazides, amiodarone, voriconazole, NSAIDs, quinolones, phenothiazines.
    • Sharp cut-off at clothing lines; spares under the chin, behind the ears and skin folds.
    • Stop or switch the culprit with the prescriber (never stop amiodarone, voriconazole or antiretrovirals without specialist input); strict UVA protection.

    Overview

    An abnormal skin reaction to UV (mostly UVA, 320–400 nm) or visible light caused by a systemic or topical drug (or chemical). Two mechanisms: phototoxic (common; non-immune) and photoallergic (uncommon; immune). Some drugs do both. A core pharmacist topic because most cases follow a medicine started in the previous days–months, and many are preventable with counselling at the point of supply.

    An abnormal skin reaction to light (usually UVA, 320–400 nm) caused by a systemic or topical drug.

    Epidemiology

    • Who
      • Common in older people on multiple drugs; holiday or summer onset.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    • Dose- and light-dose-dependent; can happen to anyone, on first exposure, no sensitisation needed.
    • Requires prior sensitisation, only affects a minority, not strictly dose-related; may cross-react with chemically related drugs and may persist after drug withdrawal (persistent light reaction → CAD). Topical agents (ketoprofen gel, sunscreen filters, fragrances, phenothiazine creams) are commoner causes than oral drugs.

    Risk factors and associations

    • Photosensitising drug in the skin plus UVA (passes through window glass).

    Myths to address

    • "Sunscreen fixes it": many sunscreens protect poorly against UVA; clothing and avoiding sun matter.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    A drug in the skin absorbs UV energy and damages cells directly or creates an allergen.

    1. 1

      Drug in skin

      The drug or its metabolite reaches the dermis and epidermis.

    2. 2

      Phototoxic

      Absorbed UVA excites the drug, releasing free radicals that damage cells: sunburn-like reaction within hours.

    3. 3

      Photoallergic

      UV converts the drug into a hapten; T cells become sensitised: eczema days later, may spread beyond exposed sites.

    4. 4

      Pigmentation

      Some drugs (amiodarone, minocycline) leave slate-grey or blue pigment in exposed skin.

    How it is caused

    • Some drugs do both (thiazides, quinine, NSAIDs, phenothiazines).
    • Thiazides/sulfonamides can also cause photo-distributed lichenoid eruptions, SCLE and pseudoporphyria.
    • Hydrochlorothiazide carries a dose-dependent increased risk of non-melanoma skin cancer (MHRA DSU Nov 2018). The evidence applies to HCTZ — bendroflumethiazide data are limited — but photoprotection advice applies to all thiazides.
    • Common UK photosensitisers: thiazides (bendroflumethiazide, HCTZ, indapamide), doxycycline/tetracyclines, quinolones, sulfonamides (co-trimoxazole), amiodarone, NSAIDs (piroxicam, naproxen, ketoprofen gel — photoallergic), phenothiazines (chlorpromazine), voriconazole (SCC risk), St John's wort, retinoids, methotrexate (UV recall), quinine, hydroxychloroquine, furosemide (pseudoporphyria), vemurafenib.

    Histopathology

    • Phototoxic: necrotic keratinocytes like sunburn. Photoallergic: spongiotic dermatitis. Phototesting confirms in specialist units.

    (Three discriminators: sensitisation, distribution, burning vs itching.)

    Other drug-related photo-patterns: drug-induced subacute cutaneous lupus (thiazides, terbinafine, PPIs, calcium-channel blockers, checkpoint inhibitors), drug-induced pellagra (isoniazid), lichenoid photo-eruption (thiazides, quinine), methotrexate UV-recall of a recent sunburn.

    • Itchy or burning erythema on the face, V of the neck, nape, backs of hands and forearms.
    • There is a sharp cut-off at clothing, with sparing of the upper eyelids, under the chin (submental), behind the ears, nasolabial folds and finger webs.

    Special groups

    • Photo-onycholysis
      • Tetracyclines and psoralens can lift the nails from the bed after sun exposure.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    Clinical + temporal drug history (including OTC, topical, herbal — St John's wort — and supplements, and recent dose increases). Specialist: phototesting on and off the drug, photopatch testing for suspected photoallergens, biopsy if lupus/lichenoid suspected; ANA/Ro if lupus-like.

    • Clinical: distribution + drug history + sun exposure.
    • Investigations mainly exclude differentials: ANA, anti-Ro/La (SCLE — note her omeprazole, the commonest drug cause of SCLE, and thiazides also cause SCLE); porphyrin screen if blistering or fragility (PCT, pseudoporphyria); biopsy if uncertain

    Management

    • Identify and stop/switch the culprit where possible, in discussion with the original prescriber (e.g. doxycycline → lymecycline is also a tetracycline, so consider a non-tetracycline per indication; thiazide → alternative antihypertensive). Never stop amiodarone, voriconazole or antiretroviral/oncology drugs without specialist input — manage with photoprotection instead; amiodarone photosensitivity can persist for months after stopping.
    • Acute phototoxic reaction: cool compresses, emollients, analgesia (paracetamol; avoid if NSAID is the culprit), potent topical corticosteroid short course for inflammation on body; burn-type dressings for blisters; oral antihistamine for itch.
    • Prevention: counsel at supply, especially before holidays: broad-spectrum high-UVA sunscreen, cover up, avoid sunbeds; consider evening dosing only where evidence supports it (e.g. some advise doxycycline with evening meal — mixed evidence; protection remains essential).
    • Report suspected ADRs via MHRA Yellow Card (serious, novel, or black-triangle drugs).

    IP scope

    Stopping/switching antihypertensives within competence, topical steroids, photoprotection advice, Yellow Card.

    Referral

    • Same day

      Blistering, extensive or systemic reaction: same-day assessment.

    • Routine

      Long-term voriconazole with photodamage: monitor for skin cancer.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Blistering, extensive or systemic reaction: same-day assessment.
    • Long-term voriconazole with photodamage: monitor for skin cancer.

    Red flags / refer

    Blistering over large area, mucosal involvement or systemic illness (consider SJS/TEN, not photosensitivity) → same-day; persistent eruption after stopping drug (persistent light reaction/CAD) → dermatology; lupus-like eruption → GP bloods/dermatology; voriconazole long-term + photodamage → dermatology for skin cancer surveillance.

    Red flags / refer

    Blistering/erosions, widespread or persistent eruption after stopping the drug (persistent light reaction/chronic actinic dermatitis), suspected SCLE/lupus, diagnostic doubt → dermatology (photobiology).

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Blistering, pigmentation, persistent light reaction (chronic actinic dermatitis).

    Prognosis

    • Phototoxic reactions clear when the drug stops (amiodarone may take months); photoallergy can persist.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • Always ask "any new tablets, creams or herbal remedies in the last 3 months?" for any photo-distributed rash.

    • Doxycycline (acne, rosacea, malaria prophylaxis, Lyme, chest infections) — the commonest UK primary-care culprit; give the sun advice with every supply, especially malaria prophylaxis for sunny destinations.

    • Topical ketoprofen gel — BNF/MHRA advice: avoid exposure of treated area to sunlight/UV (incl. sunbeds) during treatment and for 2 weeks after, wash hands, avoid octocrylene-containing sunscreens/fragrance co-sensitisers.

    • Hydrochlorothiazide — MHRA (2018) Drug Safety Update: cumulative-dose-related increased risk of non-melanoma skin cancer; advise sun protection, skin checks, and review in those with prior skin cancer. (Bendroflumethiazide is the usual UK thiazide; photosensitivity also listed.)

    • Voriconazole — phototoxicity and squamous cell carcinoma/melanoma risk with long-term use (MHRA warning): strict photoprotection; dermatology review.

    • Fluoroquinolones — photosensitivity listed; MHRA restrictions on use for other safety reasons; avoid strong sun/sunbeds during and for a few days after.

    • Methotrexate — sunburn recall; advise sun protection.

    • Isotretinoin/acitretin — increased sunburn/skin fragility (retinoid effect, not classic phototoxicity).

    • Check the BNF monograph side-effects / "Patient and carer advice" for photosensitivity statements — the definitive UK source for counselling labels.

    • BNF cautionary label 11 applies to doxycycline, demeclocycline, chlorpromazine and others. Use the same wording verbally for thiazides and amiodarone.

    • Doxycycline photosensitivity is dose-related — useful when choosing lymecycline vs doxycycline for summer acne courses.

    • Thiazide users with lots of sun exposure or previous skin cancer: discuss NMSC risk (MHRA 2018) and skin checks.

    Self-check quiz

    5 questions · optional
    1. Q1When should you think of Drug-induced photosensitivity?Reveal

      Exaggerated sunburn, blistering, eczema or pigment change on sun-exposed skin after starting a drug (doxycycline, thiazides, amiodarone, NSAIDs, quinine, voriconazole).

    2. Q2What drives it?Reveal

      Phototoxic: the drug absorbs UVA and directly damages skin (dose-dependent). Photoallergic: UV turns the drug into an allergen (type IV).

    3. Q3What is the backbone of care?Reveal

      Review and stop or switch the culprit where possible, counsel UVA protection, treat with cool compresses and potent topical steroid; Yellow Card.

    4. Q4What must you never miss?Reveal

      Voriconazole-related SCC risk, and lupus or pellagra as differentials.

    5. Q5Name a red flag that needs escalation.Reveal

      Blistering, extensive or systemic reaction: same-day assessment.
