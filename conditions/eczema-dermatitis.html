    Inflammatory skin

    Eczema / dermatitis

    Itchy, barrier-driven inflammation. Treat with generous emollients and a topical steroid matched to site and severity, and never miss eczema herpeticum.

    Also calledatopic eczemaatopic dermatitiscontact dermatitisirritant dermatitisallergic contact dermatitishand eczema+2
    Related pagesEmollients & soap substitutesTopical corticosteroids: potency, quantities & safetyHand eczema (chronic, occupational & pompholyx)Contact dermatitisItch & xerosis in older adults

    Key facts

    • Atopic eczema is the commonest form: a chronic, itchy, relapsing inflammatory skin disease with a barrier defect and type 2 (Th2) inflammation.
    • Affects up to 20% of children and 2–10% of adults; about 80% start before age 6.
    • Infants: cheeks and extensor surfaces; children and adults: flexures; chronic disease shows lichenification.
    • UK care follows NICE CG57 / CKS: generous emollients, topical steroids matched to severity and site, and treat flares early.
    • Never miss eczema herpeticum (punched-out erosions, unwell): same-day assessment.

    Overview

    Atopic eczema (atopic dermatitis) is a chronic, relapsing, itchy inflammatory skin disease built on a faulty skin barrier. It is the commonest inflammatory skin disease. Most cases start in early childhood, usually before age 5, and a sizeable minority carry on into adult life or come back later. It clusters with asthma, hay fever and food allergy.

    Contact dermatitis is eczema caused by something touching the skin. Irritant contact dermatitis is the more common type. Allergic contact dermatitis is a delayed (type IV) hypersensitivity reaction. Contact dermatitis is a major cause of occupational skin disease in cleaners, healthcare staff, caterers and hairdressers.

    Note

    Other eczema patterns you will meet

    Discoid (nummular), asteatotic (eczema craquelé), pompholyx (dyshidrotic), seborrhoeic dermatitis and venous (gravitational) eczema.

    Epidemiology

    • Prevalence
      • Lifetime prevalence over 15% in high-income countries; about 230 million people worldwide.
      • Affects up to 20% of children in the UK; 5–15% of young adults still have it.
    • Age
      • Usually starts in infancy: about 80% before age 6.
      • Typically worst at 2–4 years; may recur in adult life with irritant or occupational exposure.
    • Atopy
      • Clusters with asthma, hay fever and food allergy (the atopic march).
    • Ethnicity
      • All ethnic groups; prevalence and pattern vary between populations (e.g. more extensor and follicular eczema in skin of colour).

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    Atopic eczema: a gene–environment problem

    • Barrier defect. Filaggrin-related dryness and a disordered lipid layer increase water loss and let irritants and allergens in.
    • Immune dysregulation. Th2-skewed inflammation drives the itch.
    • Triggers: soaps and detergents, S. aureus, allergens, heat, wool, climate and stress.
    • Scratching perpetuates the cycle, causing lichenification and secondary infection.

    Contact dermatitis

    Irritant: direct chemical, toxic or frictional damage from wet work, soaps and solvents. It depends on dose and exposure, so anyone can get it, but atopic skin gets it more easily.

    Allergic: needs prior sensitisation. On re-exposure a delayed eczematous reaction appears, often a day or more later, and it can spread beyond the contact site. Common culprits are nickel, fragrance, rubber accelerants, preservatives and topical medicines themselves.

    Risk factors and associations

    • Genetic: loss-of-function filaggrin (FLG) mutations weaken the barrier; strong family history of atopy.
    • Irritants: soaps, detergents, bubble bath, wool, heat, sweating, hard water.
    • Allergens: house dust mite, pets, pollen; food allergy is a trigger in a minority of infants with moderate to severe eczema.
    • Infection: Staphylococcus aureus colonisation and flares; herpes simplex.
    • Stress, hormonal changes, dry winter weather.

    Myths to address

    • "It is caused by food allergy": most eczema is not food-driven; avoid restrictive diets without specialist advice.
    • "Steroid creams thin the skin, so avoid them": used correctly (right potency, site and duration) they are safe; steroid phobia undertreats flares.
    • "Eczema is contagious": it is not, although infected eczema can spread bacteria or herpes.
    • "Emollients are optional once the skin looks better": they are the foundation of treatment and should continue.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    Atopic eczema comes from a leaky skin barrier and an overactive type 2 immune response that feed each other through the itch–scratch cycle.

    1. 1

      A faulty barrier

      Reduced filaggrin and ceramides weaken the stratum corneum, so water escapes (raised transepidermal water loss) and the skin becomes dry, rough and easily irritated.

    2. 2

      Allergens and microbes get in

      Irritants, allergens and S. aureus penetrate the damaged barrier; keratinocytes release alarmins (TSLP, IL-33, IL-25).

    3. 3

      Type 2 inflammation

      Dendritic cells drive Th2 cells and ILC2s to release IL-4, IL-13 and IL-31. IL-4/IL-13 further suppress filaggrin and antimicrobial peptides; IgE rises. In chronic eczema Th1, Th17 and Th22 also contribute.

    4. 4

      Itch and scratching

      IL-31 and other mediators activate itch nerves. Scratching damages the barrier further and releases more alarmins: the itch–scratch cycle.

    5. 5

      Chronic changes

      Repeated rubbing thickens the epidermis (lichenification), with exaggerated skin markings, excoriations and pigment change.

    Histopathology

    • Acute: spongiosis (intercellular oedema in the epidermis), sometimes vesicles; dermal lymphocytes and eosinophils.
    • Subacute: spongiosis with acanthosis and parakeratosis.
    • Chronic: marked acanthosis, hyperkeratosis and dermal fibrosis (lichen simplex pattern).
    • Biopsy is rarely needed; consider it when cutaneous T-cell lymphoma or another diagnosis is suspected.

    Prescriber tip

    Skin of colour

    Erythema can look violaceous or grey-brown and is easily underestimated. Papular and follicular patterns are more common, and post-inflammatory hypo- or hyperpigmentation is often the most visible sign. Judge severity on thickening, scale and symptoms.

    Caution

    Clues to infected eczema

    Weeping, honey-coloured crusts, pustules, rapid worsening, fever or malaise suggest secondary bacterial infection. Punched-out erosions with systemic illness suggest eczema herpeticum, which is an emergency.

    Distribution

      Patterns to recognise

      • Infant

        Red, rough cheeks, scalp and trunk; nappy area often spared. Site: Face, extensors.

      • Child

        Flexural eczema; eyelids and neck. Site: Elbow and knee creases.

      • Adult / chronic

        Lichenified, excoriated; hand and nipple eczema common. Site: Hands, face, flexures.

      • Hand

        Dry, red, scaly dorsal hands and fingers. Site: Hands.

      • Irritant contact

        Dry, fissured, sore skin at exposure sites. Site: Hands especially.

      • Allergic contact

        Matches the contact pattern; may spread. Site: Hands, face, eyelids, jewellery.

      Special groups

      • Skin of colour
        • Redness looks violaceous, grey or dark brown, so severity is often underestimated.
        • Follicular (papular) and extensor patterns are more common; post-inflammatory hyper- and hypopigmentation is marked.
      • Infants
        • Face, scalp and extensors; nappy area usually spared.
      • Older adults
        • Asteatotic (eczema craquelé) and discoid patterns; exclude scabies, drug eruptions and lymphoma.

      Diagnosis and investigations

      Diagnosis is clinical in most cases: itch, plus typical morphology and distribution, plus personal or family atopy.

      • History: onset and course, triggers, occupation and hobbies, products used on the skin, response to past treatment, sleep and quality of life.
      • Examination: look at the whole skin for excoriation, lichenification and infection; check nails and eyes.
      • Investigate when: the diagnosis is uncertain or treatment fails. Consider patch testing for suspected contact allergy, a swab if infection is suspected or recurrent, and rarely a biopsy.
      • Severity: EASI and SCORAD are specialist and audit tools. In practice, grade as mild, moderate or severe using area, itch, sleep loss, exudate and thickening.

      Differential diagnoses

      Management

      Self-care and OTC

      • Apply emollients liberally and often. Use an ointment for very dry skin and a cream or gel if greasiness puts the patient off. Use a soap substitute, and do not use aqueous cream as a leave-on product.
      • Avoid triggers such as fragranced soaps, bubble baths and wool next to the skin. Keep nails short.
      • OTC hydrocortisone 1% for short courses on suitable sites, within product licence age and site limits.
      • Non-sedating antihistamines do little for eczema itch. A short course of a sedating one is sometimes used when sleep is badly disturbed.

      Stepped prescribing (NICE/CKS atopic eczema)

      SeverityEmollientTopical corticosteroid (example class)
      MildAlwaysMild, e.g. hydrocortisone 1%
      ModerateAlways, more liberally in a flareModerate, e.g. clobetasone butyrate 0.05%, betamethasone valerate 0.025%
      SevereAlwaysPotent, e.g. betamethasone valerate 0.1%. On the face and flexures, use moderate potency at most, for a short course
      • Continue the steroid for about 48 hours after the flare settles. Then return to emollient maintenance, with or without intermittent (e.g. weekend) steroid on hotspots. Do not use this approach on the face, genitals or axillae without specialist advice.
      • Face and flexures: mild to moderate potency for short courses.
      • Infants under 12 months: avoid potent steroids without specialist advice. Never use very potent steroids in children without specialist input.
      • Topical calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus) are second line and often started by a specialist or GPwER.

      Infected eczema

      • If the patient is not systemically unwell, do not add an antibiotic routinely. Treating the eczema is often enough.
      • If an antibiotic is needed, flucloxacillin is first line. Use clarithromycin for penicillin allergy, or erythromycin if pregnant and allergic. For small localised areas, consider topical fusidic acid.
      • Replace emollient and steroid pots that may be contaminated.

      Contact dermatitis

      • Identify and avoid the irritant or allergen. Improvement can take weeks.
      • Emollients and soap substitutes. Wear gloves for wet work, with cotton liners under rubber if needed.
      • A short steroid course matched to site and severity.
      • Refer for patch testing if the problem is chronic or recurrent, occupational, on the face or hands, or not responding to treatment.

      By site or situation

      • Infected eczema: Not unwell: no routine antibiotic. If needed: flucloxacillin; clarithromycin if penicillin-allergic (erythromycin in pregnancy). Small area: consider topical fusidic acid. Replace contaminated pots.
      • Contact dermatitis: Identify and avoid the cause; gloves (cotton liners) for wet work; short steroid course. Patch test if chronic, occupational, face or hands.
      • Itch: Non-sedating antihistamines do little. Short sedating course only if sleep badly disturbed.

      Caution

      Prescribing limits and pitfalls

      • Maintenance: Back to emollient; ± weekend steroid on hotspots (not face, genitals, axillae without advice).
      • Infants < 12 months: No potent steroid without specialist advice; never very potent in children.

      Referral

      • Same day

        Eczema herpeticum: clustered punched-out erosions or vesicles, often with fever. Hospital + aciclovir.

      • Same day

        Severe or spreading infection, or a systemically unwell patient.

      • Urgent

        Severe disease not controlled after about a week of stepped treatment.

      • Routine

        Diagnostic doubt, recurrent deep infections, major psychosocial impact.

      • Routine

        Possible phototherapy or systemics; occupational disease needing workplace advice.

      Red flags and when to refer

      • Suspected eczema herpeticum: clustered punched-out erosions or vesicles, often with fever. Needs same-day hospital assessment and aciclovir.
      • Severe or spreading infection, or a systemically unwell patient.
      • Severe disease not controlled after about a week of appropriate stepped treatment.
      • Diagnostic uncertainty, recurrent deep infections, or a major psychosocial impact.
      • Possible need for phototherapy or systemic treatment, or occupational disease needing workplace advice.

      Complications and prognosis

      Complications

      • Bacterial infection (S. aureus, impetiginisation): weeping, crusting, pustules.
      • Eczema herpeticum (HSV): clustered punched-out erosions, fever; an emergency.
      • Molluscum contagiosum and eczema coxsackium spread more widely.
      • Sleep loss, poor school or work performance, anxiety and depression.
      • Contact dermatitis (irritant or allergic, including to treatments) and occupational hand eczema.
      • Erythroderma (rare); growth effects only with very severe disease or excessive steroid use.
      • Steroid side effects (atrophy, striae, perioral dermatitis) with misuse.

      Prognosis

      • Many children improve with age: in a large meta-analysis about 20% still had eczema 8 years later, and under 5% at 20 years.
      • The skin stays dry and sensitive for life; adult relapse is common with irritant exposure.
      • Earlier onset (before age 2) carries a lower risk of persistence.

      Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

      • Prescribe enough emollient. Adults often need several hundred grams a month, and under-prescribing is the commonest reason treatment fails. Apply in the direction of hair growth, ideally after bathing while the skin is still damp.

      • Fire risk: dressings, clothing and bedding soaked in paraffin-based (and other) emollients can catch fire easily. Warn patients to keep away from naked flames.

      • Teach fingertip units (FTUs) so steroid doses are understood.

      • Tackle steroid phobia with clear advice on potency, site and duration. Under-treatment and rebound are far more common than harm.

      • Inflamed is not the same as infected. Weeping crust does not automatically mean an antibiotic.

      • Pregnancy: most emollients and mild to moderate steroids are acceptable. Check the BNF for each product.

      • Counselling: eczema is not caused by poor hygiene, and the barrier tendency is lifelong even when the skin is clear.

      • Hands and feet: see hand eczema for occupational dermatitis, pompholyx and patch-test referral.

      • Potency ladder, fingertip units and steroid side-effects: topical corticosteroids; choosing and quantifying moisturisers: emollients.

      Self-check quiz

      4 questions · optional
      1. Q1How long should a topical steroid continue after a flare?Reveal

        About 48 hours after the flare settles, then back to emollient maintenance.

      2. Q2First-line oral antibiotic for infected eczema, if one is needed?Reveal

        Flucloxacillin (clarithromycin if penicillin-allergic; erythromycin if pregnant and allergic).

      3. Q3What suggests eczema herpeticum, and what do you do?Reveal

        Clustered punched-out erosions or vesicles, often with fever. Same-day hospital assessment and aciclovir.

      4. Q4Commonest reason eczema treatment fails?Reveal

        Under-prescribing emollient: adults often need several hundred grams a month.
