    Inflammatory skin

    Eczema / dermatitis

    Itchy, barrier-driven inflammation. Treat with generous emollients and a topical steroid matched to site and severity, and never miss eczema herpeticum.

    15 images

    Also coversatopic eczemaatopic dermatitiscontact dermatitisirritant dermatitisallergic contact dermatitishand eczemaeczema herpeticuminfected eczema
    Think of it when

    Itchy, ill-defined, dry or weeping patches in the flexures (child/adult) or on the cheeks (infant); a personal or family history of atopy.

    What drives it

    A leaky skin barrier plus Th2-driven inflammation, kept going by the itch–scratch cycle.

    Backbone of care

    Emollient for everyone, every day. Add a topical steroid stepped to severity and body site.

    Never miss

    Eczema herpeticum: punched-out erosions in an unwell patient. Admit the same day.

    01

    What it is

    Atopic eczema (atopic dermatitis) is a chronic, relapsing, itchy inflammatory skin disease built on a faulty skin barrier. It is the commonest inflammatory skin disease. Most cases start in early childhood, usually before age 5, and a sizeable minority carry on into adult life or come back later. It clusters with asthma, hay fever and food allergy.

    Contact dermatitis is eczema caused by something touching the skin. Irritant contact dermatitis is the more common type. Allergic contact dermatitis is a delayed (type IV) hypersensitivity reaction. Contact dermatitis is a major cause of occupational skin disease in cleaners, healthcare staff, caterers and hairdressers.

    Note

    Other eczema patterns you will meet

    Discoid (nummular), asteatotic (eczema craquelé), pompholyx (dyshidrotic), seborrhoeic dermatitis and venous (gravitational) eczema.

    02

    How it’s caused

    Atopic eczema: a gene–environment problem

    • Barrier defect. Filaggrin-related dryness and a disordered lipid layer increase water loss and let irritants and allergens in.
    • Immune dysregulation. Th2-skewed inflammation drives the itch.
    • Triggers: soaps and detergents, S. aureus, allergens, heat, wool, climate and stress.
    • Scratching perpetuates the cycle, causing lichenification and secondary infection.

    Contact dermatitis

    Irritant: direct chemical, toxic or frictional damage from wet work, soaps and solvents. It depends on dose and exposure, so anyone can get it, but atopic skin gets it more easily.

    Allergic: needs prior sensitisation. On re-exposure a delayed eczematous reaction appears, often a day or more later, and it can spread beyond the contact site. Common culprits are nickel, fragrance, rubber accelerants, preservatives and topical medicines themselves.

    Prescriber tip

    Skin of colour

    Erythema can look violaceous or grey-brown and is easily underestimated. Papular and follicular patterns are more common, and post-inflammatory hypo- or hyperpigmentation is often the most visible sign. Judge severity on thickening, scale and symptoms.

    Caution

    Clues to infected eczema

    Weeping, honey-coloured crusts, pustules, rapid worsening, fever or malaise suggest secondary bacterial infection. Punched-out erosions with systemic illness suggest eczema herpeticum, which is an emergency.

    04

    Diagnosis

    Diagnosis is clinical in most cases: itch, plus typical morphology and distribution, plus personal or family atopy.

    • History: onset and course, triggers, occupation and hobbies, products used on the skin, response to past treatment, sleep and quality of life.
    • Examination: look at the whole skin for excoriation, lichenification and infection; check nails and eyes.
    • Investigate when: the diagnosis is uncertain or treatment fails. Consider patch testing for suspected contact allergy, a swab if infection is suspected or recurrent, and rarely a biopsy.
    • Severity: EASI and SCORAD are specialist and audit tools. In practice, grade as mild, moderate or severe using area, itch, sleep loss, exudate and thickening.

    Look-alikeDistinguishing clues

    Cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (rare)Persistent, atypical plaques in an older adult; refer if anything seems odd
    06

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    Pharmacist / IP

    Self-care and OTC

    • Apply emollients liberally and often. Use an ointment for very dry skin and a cream or gel if greasiness puts the patient off. Use a soap substitute, and do not use aqueous cream as a leave-on product.
    • Avoid triggers such as fragranced soaps, bubble baths and wool next to the skin. Keep nails short.
    • OTC hydrocortisone 1% for short courses on suitable sites, within product licence age and site limits.
    • Non-sedating antihistamines do little for eczema itch. A short course of a sedating one is sometimes used when sleep is badly disturbed.

    Stepped prescribing (NICE/CKS atopic eczema)

    SeverityEmollientTopical corticosteroid (example class)
    MildAlwaysMild, e.g. hydrocortisone 1%
    ModerateAlways, more liberally in a flareModerate, e.g. clobetasone butyrate 0.05%, betamethasone valerate 0.025%
    SevereAlwaysPotent, e.g. betamethasone valerate 0.1%. On the face and flexures, use moderate potency at most, for a short course
    • Continue the steroid for about 48 hours after the flare settles. Then return to emollient maintenance, with or without intermittent (e.g. weekend) steroid on hotspots. Do not use this approach on the face, genitals or axillae without specialist advice.
    • Face and flexures: mild to moderate potency for short courses.
    • Infants under 12 months: avoid potent steroids without specialist advice. Never use very potent steroids in children without specialist input.
    • Topical calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus) are second line and often started by a specialist or GPwER.

    Infected eczema

    • If the patient is not systemically unwell, do not add an antibiotic routinely. Treating the eczema is often enough.
    • If an antibiotic is needed, flucloxacillin is first line. Use clarithromycin for penicillin allergy, or erythromycin if pregnant and allergic. For small localised areas, consider topical fusidic acid.
    • Replace emollient and steroid pots that may be contaminated.

    Contact dermatitis

    • Identify and avoid the irritant or allergen. Improvement can take weeks.
    • Emollients and soap substitutes. Wear gloves for wet work, with cotton liners under rubber if needed.
    • A short steroid course matched to site and severity.
    • Refer for patch testing if the problem is chronic or recurrent, occupational, on the face or hands, or not responding to treatment.
    07

    Red flags & referral

    Escalate
    • Suspected eczema herpeticum: clustered punched-out erosions or vesicles, often with fever. Needs same-day hospital assessment and aciclovir.
    • Severe or spreading infection, or a systemically unwell patient.
    • Severe disease not controlled after about a week of appropriate stepped treatment.
    • Diagnostic uncertainty, recurrent deep infections, or a major psychosocial impact.
    • Possible need for phototherapy or systemic treatment, or occupational disease needing workplace advice.
    08

    Prescriber pearls

    • Prescribe enough emollient. Adults often need several hundred grams a month, and under-prescribing is the commonest reason treatment fails. Apply in the direction of hair growth, ideally after bathing while the skin is still damp.
    • Fire risk: dressings, clothing and bedding soaked in paraffin-based (and other) emollients can catch fire easily. Warn patients to keep away from naked flames.
    • Teach fingertip units (FTUs) so steroid doses are understood.
    • Tackle steroid phobia with clear advice on potency, site and duration. Under-treatment and rebound are far more common than harm.
    • Inflamed is not the same as infected. Weeping crust does not automatically mean an antibiotic.
    • Pregnancy: most emollients and mild to moderate steroids are acceptable. Check the BNF for each product.
    • Counselling: eczema is not caused by poor hygiene, and the barrier tendency is lifelong even when the skin is clear.
    • Hands and feet: see hand eczema for occupational dermatitis, pompholyx and patch-test referral.
    • Potency ladder, fingertip units and steroid side-effects: topical corticosteroids; choosing and quantifying moisturisers: emollients.
