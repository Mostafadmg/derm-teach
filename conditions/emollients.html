    Emollients & soap substitutes

    Why emollients are the backbone of dry-skin and eczema care, how to choose between lotions, creams, gels and ointments, how much to prescribe, and the safety messages (fire risk, aqueous cream).

    Also calledmoisturiserleave-on emollientsoap substituteaqueous creamparaffin fire riskemollient quantity

    Key facts

    • Any dry or eczematous skin, at every review, alongside active treatment.
    • They replace lipids and water in the stratum corneum, restore the barrier and reduce itch and flares.
    • The right emollient is the one they’ll use: children 250–500 g / week, also as a soap substitute.
    • Fire risk with any emollient soaked into fabrics (MHRA); slips; stinging from aqueous cream.

    Overview

    Moisturisers that soften and hydrate the stratum corneum.

    • Occlusives (paraffins, petrolatum, dimeticone) reduce transepidermal water loss.
    • Humectants (urea, glycerol, lactic acid) draw water in.
    • Emollient lipids (ceramides, oils) fill gaps in the barrier.

    Formulations range from light lotions/gels → creams → greasy ointments, which work best and contain the least preservative. They are used as leave-on treatments, as soap substitutes, and (now rarely) as bath additives.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    Clinical features

    How it works

    Emollients restore the lipid "mortar" between corneocytes. They reduce water loss, irritant/allergen penetration and itch, and have a steroid-sparing effect (fewer flares, less steroid needed). In atopic eczema, filaggrin loss and soap/surfactant exposure drive barrier failure (see the eczema notes see Vitamin D analogues, coal tar).

    Clinical uses & where it fits

    Step 0 of every eczema, psoriasis and ichthyosis plan. Also xerosis in older people, hand dermatitis and dryness after isotretinoin.

    How to choose / assess

    • "The best emollient is the one the patient will use." Offer 2–4 to try (sample packs or self-selection, photo C03). Consider dryness, site, time of day, school/work, preference and allergens/irritants: avoid fragrance, and check for lanolin (wool alcohols), SLS, methylisothiazolinone and preservatives in creams.
    • Quantity check: NICE CG57 — 250–500 g/week for children; about 500 g/week for adults with widespread dryness. A patient collecting one 500 g tub every 3 months is under-using.
    • Prescribe enough for home and nursery/school (NICE CG57).

    Diagnosis and investigations

    Quantity

    • 250–500 g / week for children; ~500 g / week for adults with widespread dryness.

    Under-use check

    • One 500 g tub every 3 months = under-using.

    Allergy?

    • Worse with a product: suspect contact allergy; patch test.

    Differential diagnoses

    PhotoConditionDistinguishing features
    		Aqueous cream BPNot a leave-on: SLS stings and damages the barrier (MHRA 2013).
    		Colloidal oatmeal creamLeave-on; rare oat allergy; check formulary.
    		Paraffin-based creamsLeave-on; lighter, daytime.
    		Greasy ointmentsLeave-on and soap substitute; high fire risk on fabric.
    		Emulsifying ointmentLeave-on and wash; older versions had SLS: switch if it stings.

    Management

    • Prescribe generously (250–500 g/week for a child; ~500 g for an adult), in pump dispensers to reduce contamination, with a separate tub for the soap substitute.
    • How to apply: smooth on in the direction of hair growth (reduces folliculitis), often (at least 2–4 times daily and after washing), including when the skin is clear. Leave a gap between emollient and steroid.
    • MHRA safety: Fire risk with both paraffin-containing and paraffin-free emollients that dry onto clothing, bedding or dressings (DSU Dec 2018; reinforced 2020). Advise no smoking or naked flames; washing fabrics at high temperature may not remove all residue; tell carers and care homes; Slips in the bath/shower with emollient bath additives. Bath additives add no benefit (BATHE trial), and NICE CG57 (updated 2023) no longer advises offering them routinely
    • Aqueous cream (MHRA DSU 2013): may irritate the skin, particularly in children with eczema, probably because of SLS.

    IP scope

    • Fully within community/primary-care IP and OTC practice. Follow ICB self-care/formulary policies; many ICBs expect OTC purchase for mild dry skin.

    Treatment ladder

    1. 1

      Prescribe

      Pump dispensers; separate tub for soap substitute. Generous quantities (as above).

    2. 2

      Apply

      Smooth on in the direction of hair growth (less folliculitis). At least 2–4 times daily and after washing, even when clear.

    3. 3

      Counsel safety

      Fire risk with paraffin and paraffin-free emollients on clothing, bedding, dressings: no smoking or naked flames; tell carers. Slips with bath additives; BATHE trial: no added benefit.

    Caution

    Prescribing limits and pitfalls

    • Fire: Residue on fabric burns easily; washing may not remove it.
    • Aqueous cream: Wash product at most.

    Referral

    • Routine

      Uncontrolled despite adequate emollient + TCS: refer per NICE CG57.

    • Routine

      Suspected emollient contact allergy: patch testing.

    • Same day

      Erythroderma or infected eczema: assess promptly.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Warn about fire risk: dressings, clothing and bedding soaked in any emollient (paraffin or paraffin-free) can catch fire easily (MHRA).

    Red flags / refer

    • Eczema still uncontrolled despite adequate emollient + TCS (see NICE CG57 referral criteria); suspected emollient contact allergy (patch testing); erythroderma; infection.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • "Emollients are the treatment, not an extra."

    • Show the FTU and a handful of emollient. For a child with widespread eczema, a 500 g tub should last about a week.

    • Fire-safety counselling is an MHRA expectation — record that you gave it.

    Self-check quiz

    4 questions · optional
    1. Q1Weekly emollient for a child with eczema (NICE)?Reveal

      250–500 g a week.

    2. Q2Why not aqueous cream as a leave-on?Reveal

      Contains SLS: stinging and barrier damage (MHRA 2013).

    3. Q3Which direction to apply?Reveal

      In the direction of hair growth, to reduce folliculitis.

    4. Q4Fire risk applies to which emollients?Reveal

      Both paraffin-containing and paraffin-free products soaked into fabric.
