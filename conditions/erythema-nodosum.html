    Erythema nodosum (incl. drug-induced)

    Tender red nodules on the shins that bruise as they fade. Causes include streptococcal infection, sarcoidosis, IBD, pregnancy and drugs such as the combined pill; the work-up looks for the trigger.

    Also calledENtender shin nodulesseptal panniculitisCOCstreptococcalsarcoidosis+1
    Related pagesSarcoidosis (incl. Löfgren syndrome)Cellulitis & erysipelasApproach to cutaneous vasculitisCutaneous tuberculosis (scrofuloderma & others)Pyoderma gangrenosum

    Key facts

    • Erythema nodosum (EN) is a septal panniculitis: tender red nodules on the shins that bruise as they heal.
    • Often with fever, malaise and arthralgia; lasts 3–6 weeks; does not ulcerate or scar.
    • Causes: streptococcal throat, sarcoidosis, IBD, pregnancy, drugs (combined pill), TB; about half idiopathic.
    • Tests: FBC, CRP, ASO titre, chest X-ray (bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy), throat swab; others guided by history.
    • Treat the cause; rest, elevation, compression and NSAIDs.

    Overview

    Erythema nodosum (EN) is the commonest septal panniculitis: a hypersensitivity reaction producing tender red nodules, usually on the shins. It is most common in women aged 20–45.

    Epidemiology

    • Who
      • Women 3–6× more often; age 20–45; commonest panniculitis.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    Risk factors and associations

    • Delayed hypersensitivity to infection (streptococcus, TB, Yersinia), sarcoidosis, IBD, pregnancy, drugs, malignancy (lymphoma).

    Myths to address

    • "It's a bruise or cellulitis": bilateral tender nodules plus systemic symptoms point to EN.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    EN is an immune reaction in the fibrous septa between fat lobules.

    1. 1

      Antigen exposure

      An infection, drug or systemic disease provides the antigen.

    2. 2

      Immune complex / T-cell reaction

      Immune reactions target the septal blood vessels of the subcutaneous fat.

    3. 3

      Septal panniculitis

      Inflammatory cells thicken the septa while lobules are spared.

    4. 4

      Resolution

      Haemoglobin breakdown changes colour from red to purple to yellow-green, like a bruise.

    How it is caused

    A delayed hypersensitivity response to many antigens:

    • idiopathic (~30–50%)
    • streptococcal infection (the commonest identifiable cause, especially in children)
    • sarcoidosis (Löfgren syndrome — the skin signs of systemic disease notes)
    • drugs — oestrogen-containing contraceptives/HRT, sulfonamides, penicillins/amoxicillin, bromides, minocycline, gold, and newer agents (BRAF inhibitors, immune checkpoint inhibitors, isotretinoin rarely)
    • pregnancy
    • IBD (Crohn's/UC)
    • TB; Yersinia, Salmonella, Campylobacter; Chlamydia, Mycoplasma; Behçet's; lymphoma (rare).

    Histopathology

    • Septal panniculitis without vasculitis; Miescher radial granulomas. Biopsy only if atypical (deep incisional).

    Clinical features

    • Crops of tender, warm, ill-defined red nodules 1–5 cm on the anterior shins, usually bilateral. Less often on the thighs, forearms or trunk.
    • They evolve through bruise-like colours ("erythema contusiformis", photo E02) and resolve in 3–6 weeks without ulceration or scarring.
    • Prodrome: fever, malaise, arthralgia (ankles), sometimes before the nodules.
    • Unilateral EN is uncommon but described. A single unilateral lesion makes it essential to exclude the differentials below.

    Special groups

    • Löfgren syndrome
      • EN + bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy + fever + arthritis: acute sarcoidosis with a good prognosis.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    • Clinical diagnosis; look for the cause: FBC, ESR/CRP, U&E/LFT; ASO/anti-DNase B titre ± throat swab; CXR (bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy — sarcoid/TB/lymphoma) ± serum ACE/calcium; IGRA/Mantoux if TB risk; pregnancy test; stool culture if diarrhoea; faecal calprotectin if bowel symptoms; urinalysis
    • Deep incisional/punch biopsy (to fat) if atypical — unilateral, ulcerating or persistent >6–8 weeks.
    • Case-specific: she has a unilateral tender shin lesion and takes a COC + tranexamic acid (both prothrombotic). Apply Wells/D-dimer and venous Doppler if there is calf swelling, cord-like tenderness or leg oedema, to exclude DVT/superficial thrombophlebitis. The course discussion did not mention this.

    Also: insect-bite reactions, cutaneous PAN (nodules + livedo — the vasculitis notes), Sweet syndrome, lipodermatosclerosis.

    Management

    1. Treat the cause. In this case, consider stopping the COC, which is the most likely drug trigger. Tranexamic acid is a less established EN trigger but adds thrombotic risk with the COC. Metformin is not a recognised cause: Contraception and cycle control for PCOS: a progestogen-only method (desogestrel POP, implant, LNG-IUS 52 mg — also helps dysmenorrhoea and heavy bleeding), or cyclical progestogen to protect the endometrium. Weight loss and metformin help cycle regularity; Dysmenorrhoea: an NSAID such as mefenamic acid or naproxen (both effective for dysmenorrhoea and EN pain); LNG-IUS; Note the FSRH/BNF caution about combining tranexamic acid with a COC — prescribe TXA only for heavy menstrual bleeding, at the licensed dose, and not in people at VTE risk
    2. Symptomatic: rest, leg elevation, compression hosiery (if arterial supply is adequate), cool compresses; NSAIDs (ibuprofen/naproxen) for pain if not contraindicated; paracetamol.
    3. Specialist options: potassium iodide (rarely used in the UK); colchicine; systemic corticosteroids only after infection (TB, strep) and malignancy are excluded.
    4. Review in 2–4 weeks. Persistent or recurrent EN → dermatology/rheumatology; biopsy if atypical.

    IP scope

    Investigation, contraceptive switch (within competence and UKMEC), NSAID analgesia, and referral. Thrombosis assessment through local DVT pathways.

    Referral

    • Routine

      EN with cough, breathlessness, weight loss or night sweats: CXR to look for sarcoidosis, TB or lymphoma.

    • Routine

      Ulcerating or one-sided nodules: not typical EN, so think again (erythema induratum, vasculitis, cellulitis).

    Red flags and when to refer

    • EN with cough, breathlessness, weight loss or night sweats: CXR to look for sarcoidosis, TB or lymphoma.
    • Ulcerating or one-sided nodules: not typical EN, so think again (erythema induratum, vasculitis, cellulitis).

    Red flags / refer

    Unilateral swelling with features of DVT (same-day DVT pathway); fever + spreading erythema (cellulitis); BHL on CXR or respiratory symptoms (sarcoid/TB/lymphoma); bowel symptoms (IBD); ulceration (not EN); pregnancy; lesions lasting >6–8 weeks.

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Pain, arthralgia; complications depend on the cause.

    Prognosis

    • Resolves in 3–6 weeks; recurrence possible if the cause persists.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • "EN is a sign, not a diagnosis" — the job is finding the trigger.

    • It heals without scarring, but new crops can appear for several weeks after the trigger is removed.

    • In any woman on a COC + TXA with a tender, red, unilateral leg, think clot before panniculitis.

    Self-check quiz

    5 questions · optional
    1. Q1When should you think of Erythema nodosum (incl. drug-induced)?Reveal

      Painful, warm, ill-defined red nodules on both shins, often with fever or aching joints.

    2. Q2What drives it?Reveal

      A hypersensitivity reaction in the fat septa (septal panniculitis) to an infection, drug, inflammatory disease or pregnancy.

    3. Q3What is the backbone of care?Reveal

      Find the trigger (throat swab/ASO, CXR, FBC, CRP, pregnancy test, drug review); rest, elevation, NSAID if suitable; stop a culprit COC.

    4. Q4What must you never miss?Reveal

      Sarcoidosis with hilar nodes; TB; IBD flare; cellulitis or DVT mistaken for EN.

    5. Q5Name a red flag that needs escalation.Reveal

      EN with cough, breathlessness, weight loss or night sweats: CXR to look for sarcoidosis, TB or lymphoma.
