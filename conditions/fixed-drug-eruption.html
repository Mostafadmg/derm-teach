    Fixed drug eruption

    A round dusky patch or blister that comes back in exactly the same place each time a drug is taken, leaving brown-grey pigmentation. Common triggers are NSAIDs, paracetamol, antibiotics and laxatives.

    Overview

    Module 1 · Week 3FDErecurring rash same placeNSAID

    • Spot it

      A round, dusky red-brown patch on the lip, genitals, hands or face that recurs at the same site, often mistaken for a cold sore.

    • Why

      Memory T cells sitting in the skin at that site are reactivated by the drug within hours.

    • Treat

      Find the intermittent drug (ask about period pain relief, cold remedies, laxatives); stop and avoid it; potent topical steroid; document.

    • Never miss

      Generalised bullous FDE, which can resemble SJS/TEN and needs same-day assessment.

    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    What it is

    A drug reaction that recurs at exactly the same site(s) each time the drug is taken. Usually there are one or a few round, dusky-violet plaques, leaving slate-brown/grey pigmentation.

    How it’s caused

    How it is caused

    Intraepidermal CD8+ tissue-resident memory T cells persist at the original site. On re-exposure they activate within hours and release IFN-γ and granzymes → localised keratinocyte death (type IVc, like a "mini-TEN"). Melanin dropping into the dermis gives the persistent pigment.

    Common culprits: NSAIDs (diclofenac, ibuprofen, naproxen, mefenamic acid — note the menstrual link), co-trimoxazole/sulfonamides, tetracyclines, paracetamol, metronidazole, fluconazole, barbiturates, carbamazepine, sildenafil (genital), antihistamines (cetirizine, hydroxyzine), quinine (in tonic water), phenolphthalein (old laxatives) and food dyes.

    Next: What you’ll see
    3

    Step 3 of 9 · 1 min

    What you’ll see

    • Lips, genitals (glans penis), hands, feet, face; the trunk and limbs less often.
    • Round/oval, sharply demarcated, dusky red-violet plaques, often with a darker/targetoid or bullous centre. Burning or itch appears 30 min–8 h (up to 48 h) after a dose.
    • Each recurrence may add new sites. Lesions resolve over 1–3 weeks with post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, which is especially marked in skin of colour.
    • Generalised bullous FDE (GBFDE): multiple large blistering plaques (>10% BSA) in older patients. It mimics SJS/TEN, carries significant mortality (~20%) and needs admission.

    • Round/oval, sharply demarcated, dusky red-violet plaques, often with a darker/targetoid or bullous centre. Burning or itch appears 30 min–8 h (up to 48 h) after a dose.

    • The case: a 26-year-old with a pigmented patch on the upper lip, after diclofenac during two consecutive periods. Erythema/blistering recurring at the same site, then pigmentation → FDE (diclofenac). It had been treated as a cold sore.

    Confirm the diagnosis

    Lesional patch test

    • (drug applied to the old site) or supervised oral provocation in specialist settings. Not for GBFDE.

    Key points

    • History of recurrence at the same site after intermittent drug use (menstrual NSAIDs, weekend antibiotics, "headache tablets") — ask specifically about intermittent OTC products.
    • Biopsy if doubtful (interface dermatitis, necrotic keratinocytes, dermal melanophages).

    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    1. Stop and avoid diclofenac. Record "diclofenac — fixed drug eruption (lip)", and inform her that structurally related NSAIDs (acetic-acid group: aceclofenac, indometacin, etodolac) may cross-react. Some patients tolerate structurally unrelated NSAIDs, but cross-reactivity is unpredictable — specialist advice before a trial.
    2. Dysmenorrhoea alternatives: paracetamol, local heat, a hormonal method (COC/LNG-IUS/desogestrel) that suppresses ovulation/pain, TENS. Consider gynaecology review if dysmenorrhoea is severe (endometriosis).
    3. Acute lesion: potent topical steroid for a few days (a moderate steroid on the lip, short course), emollient/lip balm; for erosions, non-adherent care. The pigmentation fades slowly (months), and more slowly with repeated exposure — sun protection helps.
    4. Check OTC purchases (diclofenac gel, combination cold remedies, mefenamic acid) and tell her community pharmacy. Yellow Card (FDE is reportable, especially if bullous).

    Red flags / refer

    Multiple/generalised blistering plaques (GBFDE → same-day admission); mucosal erosions beyond the lip; uncertain diagnosis (genital lesions — exclude STI/HSV).

    IP scope

    Fully within IP scope (recognition, stopping the drug, alternative analgesia/contraception, topical steroid, documentation, Yellow Card). Provocation testing is specialist.

    Step 7 of 9 · 1 min

    Red flags & referral

    • Same day

      Many or widespread blistering patches, mucosal involvement or an unwell patient: possible generalised bullous FDE, same-day assessment.

    • Routine

      A 'cold sore' that always appears after the same tablet: ask about over-the-counter NSAIDs and paracetamol combinations.

    Step 8 of 9 · 1 min

    Prescriber pearls

    • "Same place, same drug, every time." Ask what she took in the 24 h before each flare.

    • A "cold sore" that leaves a dark patch and no vesicles is FDE until proven otherwise.

    • Menstrual NSAIDs are a classic hidden culprit; so are the "weekend antibiotics" for UTIs (co-trimoxazole/trimethoprim).
