    Infections — bacterial

    Folliculitis

    Pustules centred on hair follicles, usually from S. aureus, shaving or occlusion. Most cases need hygiene advice and an antiseptic rather than an antibiotic, so think about other causes if it recurs.

    Also calledbacterial folliculitishot tub folliculitispseudomonas folliculitispityrosporum folliculitismalassezia folliculitisshaving rash+1
    Related pagesBoils & carbunclesAcne mimics: rosacea, steroid eruptions and folliculitisAcne vulgarisImpetigo

    Key facts

    • Inflammation of hair follicles: follicular papules and pustules centred on hairs.
    • Most often bacterial (Staphylococcus aureus); also Malassezia, Pseudomonas (hot tubs), Gram-negative, viral and non-infective causes.
    • Triggers: shaving, waxing, occlusion, sweating, friction, topical steroids and antibiotics.
    • Mild: antiseptic washes and stop the trigger; persistent bacterial: topical or oral antibiotic per culture; Malassezia: antifungals.
    • Deep or spreading infection (furuncle, cellulitis) needs review.

    Overview

    Folliculitis is inflammation of the hair follicle, seen as small pustules or papules centred on hairs. Superficial bacterial folliculitis is usually caused by S. aureus.

    Deeper follicular infection becomes a boil (furuncle). Clusters form a carbuncle. See Boils & carbuncles.

    Epidemiology

    • Who
      • Common at all ages worldwide; most often adolescents and young adult men.
      • Pseudofolliculitis barbae is especially common in men with tightly curled hair.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    • Follicular occlusion or damage from shaving, waxing, tight clothing, oils, sweating and friction.
    • Bacterial proliferation in the follicle opening, usually S. aureus. Nasal carriage drives recurrence.
    • Topical steroids and immunosuppression increase risk.

    Not all folliculitis is staphylococcal

    TypeCluePathway
    Hot tub (Pseudomonas)1–2 days after a hot tub or spa; under swimwearUsually self-limiting; check spa hygiene
    Pityrosporum (Malassezia)Itchy, uniform papules on the upper back and chest; young adults; after antibioticsAntifungal, not antibiotic
    Gram-negativeLong-term antibiotics for acneSpecialist

    Risk factors and associations

    • Infective: S. aureus (commonest), coagulase-negative staphylococci, Pseudomonas (spa pools), Gram-negative (after long antibiotic use for acne), Malassezia, dermatophytes, HSV, Demodex.
    • Non-infective: shaving (pseudofolliculitis), occlusion and oils, drugs (steroids, EGFR inhibitors, lithium), eosinophilic folliculitis (HIV).
    • Host factors: atopic eczema, diabetes, obesity, immunosuppression, nasal S. aureus carriage.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    The level of the follicle that is inflamed decides how the lesion looks and how deep the risk goes.

    1. 1

      Follicle damage

      Shaving, friction or occlusion damage the follicular opening and let organisms in.

    2. 2

      Superficial folliculitis

      Organisms multiply in the upper follicle; neutrophils gather into a small pustule pierced by a hair.

    3. 3

      Deep folliculitis

      Inflammation extends down the follicle into the dermis (sycosis, furuncle), giving tender nodules and possible scarring.

    4. 4

      Ingrowing hairs

      In pseudofolliculitis, tightly curled hairs re-enter the skin after shaving, causing a foreign-body reaction.

    Histopathology

    • Neutrophils in and around the follicle; organisms on Gram or PAS stain.
    • Swab pustules for culture when persistent, recurrent or after long antibiotic use.

    Clinical features

    • Follicular-centred pustules, often with a hair visible in the centre, on a red base.
    • Sites: scalp, beard (sycosis barbae), trunk, buttocks, thighs and axillae.
    • Itchy or tender. Systemic upset is unusual.

    Look for

    • Superficial bacterial

      Small pustules centred on follicles. Site: Any hair-bearing skin.

    • Hair-bearing skin

      Scattered inflamed follicular papules. Site: Thighs, buttocks.

    • Axillary

      After shaving. Site: Axillae.

    Special groups

    • Skin of colour
      • Pseudofolliculitis barbae and keloidal scarring are common; post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation is often the main concern.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    • Clinical. The distribution and history (shaving, hot tub, recent antibiotics) point to the type.
    • Swab a pustule for culture if persistent, recurrent, extensive or atypical.
    • Consider a nasal swab for S. aureus carriage in recurrent disease.

    Clinical

    • Distribution + history point to the type.

    Ask

    • Shaving, waxing, hot tub, recent antibiotics, steroid creams?

    Swab

    • A pustule if persistent, recurrent, extensive or atypical.

    Recurrent

    • Nasal swab for carriage; screen for diabetes.

    Differential diagnoses

    Management

    Mild

    • Stop or modify the trigger: change shaving technique (a clean sharp blade, shave with the grain, or a pause in shaving), use loose clothing, and avoid oils and occlusion.
    • Use an antiseptic wash (e.g. chlorhexidine or a benzoyl peroxide wash).
    • Often no antibiotic is needed.

    Persistent bacterial

    • Topical antiseptic or antibiotic in line with local guidance, for short courses.
    • Oral flucloxacillin if extensive (clarithromycin if penicillin allergy).

    Recurrent

    Swab, consider decolonisation (via the local pathway), and screen for diabetes. Think about other causes such as Pityrosporum or Gram-negative folliculitis.

    Treatment ladder

    1. 1

      Mild

      Change shaving technique (clean sharp blade, with the grain, or pause). Loose clothing; avoid oils and occlusion.

    2. 2

      Persistent bacterial

      Topical antiseptic or antibiotic, short course, per local guidance.

    3. 3

      Extensive

      Flucloxacillin (oral)

    4. 4

      Recurrent

      Swab; decolonisation via local pathway; screen for diabetes. Rethink: Pityrosporum (antifungal) or Gram-negative (specialist).

    Referral

    • Urgent

      Progression to boils, carbuncles or cellulitis, or fever.

    • Routine

      Recurrent despite measures: carriage, PVL or MRSA, diabetes, immunosuppression.

    • Routine

      Widespread itchy folliculitis in immunosuppression (eosinophilic): refer.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Progression to boils, carbuncles or cellulitis, or fever.
    • Recurrent disease despite measures: consider carriage, PVL or MRSA, diabetes and immunosuppression.
    • Widespread itchy folliculitis in an immunosuppressed patient (eosinophilic): refer.

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Furuncles and carbuncles; cellulitis.
    • Scarring, keloids and permanent hair loss in deep or chronic forms.
    • Post-inflammatory pigmentation.

    Prognosis

    • Superficial folliculitis usually settles in days to weeks once the trigger is removed.
    • Recurrence is common with ongoing shaving, occlusion or carriage.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • Pustules on the upper back and chest after antibiotics? Think Pityrosporum folliculitis, which needs an antifungal.

    • A hot-tub history explains a sudden ‘swimsuit-distribution’ rash.

    • Most mild bacterial folliculitis settles without antibiotics, which is a useful stewardship opportunity.

    • Pseudofolliculitis barbae is common in curly hair. Discuss shaving technique and beard growth sensitively.

    2. Q2Swimsuit-distribution pustules 1–2 days after a spa?Reveal

    3. Q3Itchy uniform papules on the upper back after antibiotics?Reveal
