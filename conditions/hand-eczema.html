    Hand eczema (chronic, occupational & pompholyx)

    Chronic hand eczema is usually irritant (wet work, detergents, friction) with or without allergy (chromate, rubber, preservatives). Protect the barrier, use potent steroids correctly, and patch test anything persisting beyond 3 months.

    Also calledhand dermatitischronic hand eczemaoccupational dermatitisirritant hand dermatitiswet workpompholyx+4
    Related pagesContact dermatitisEczema / dermatitisPalmoplantar pustulosis & palmoplantar psoriasisKeratolysis exfoliativa

    Key facts

    • Common inflammatory dermatitis of the hands: irritant, allergic, atopic and vesicular (pompholyx) patterns, often mixed.
    • Chronic hand eczema affects about 1 in 10 adults; frequent cause of time off work.
    • High-risk jobs: healthcare, cleaning, catering, hairdressing, construction, metalwork (wet work and chemicals).
    • Management: avoid irritants, gloves, frequent emollients, soap substitute, potent topical steroid short courses; patch test if allergy possible.
    • Refer if severe, not responding, occupational or diagnostic doubt (tinea, psoriasis).

    Overview

    Approach to the palm and sole rash

    Ask five questions — they separate most of these conditions:

    QuestionPoints towards
    Itch?Itchy: eczema (ICD/ACD/atopic), pompholyx, tinea (mild). Non-itchy: keratolysis exfoliativa, palmoplantar psoriasis (often sore/fissured rather than itchy), EM (may burn)
    Inflammation?None (just peeling collarettes): KE. Red ± scale: eczema, psoriasis, tinea. Pustules: PPP. Deep vesicles: pompholyx. Targets: EM
    Symmetry / one hand?One palm (+ both feet) → tinea manuum ("two feet, one hand"). Dominant hand worse → irritant/frictional. Symmetrical → endogenous eczema, psoriasis, PPP, pompholyx, KE
    Border?Sharp edge, silvery/thick scale → psoriasis. Ill-defined, fissured, vesicles at edges → eczema. Raised advancing scaly edge → tinea
    Clues elsewhere?Nails (pits/onycholysis = psoriasis; dystrophy/subungual debris = tinea); toe webs; scalp/elbows; smoking (PPP, psoriasis); job/hobbies & gloves (contact dermatitis); recent infection/drugs (EM); mucosa

    Investigations a pharmacist IP can arrange/advise: skin scrapings for mycology (always before oral antifungal; any unilateral or treatment-resistant palm/sole scale); swab of crusted/weeping areas; refer for patch testing via dermatology when chronic hand eczema persists or a contact allergen is suspected.

    Hand eczema (hand dermatitis) is inflammation of the skin of the hands/wrists; it is chronic when it lasts > 3 months or relapses ≥ 2 times a year despite adequate treatment/adherence (ESCD 2022 definition). It is the commonest occupational skin disease in the UK and Europe. It is usually multifactorial: irritant ± allergic contact ± atopic tendency, and the morphology often doesn't tell you the cause.

    ESCD aetiological categories: irritant contact, allergic contact, atopic hand eczema, protein contact dermatitis/urticaria (e.g. food handlers, latex) — and clinical subtypes: hyperkeratotic (palmar), recurrent vesicular (pompholyx), nummular, pulpitis, and chronic fissured.

    Epidemiology

    • Prevalence
      • Hand dermatitis accounts for 20–35% of all dermatitis; chronic hand eczema affects 10–15% of adults.
      • More common in young adult women and people with atopic eczema.
    • Occupational
      • Wet work (over 2 hours a day or frequent handwashing) is the main risk factor; leading cause of occupational skin disease.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    Irritant contact dermatitis (ICD) — non-immunological, no prior sensitisation needed:

    • Repeated exposure (wet work, detergents, solvents, alkaline cement, friction, abrasion, occlusive gloves) strips stratum-corneum lipids and damages keratinocytes → ↑ transepidermal water loss, barrier failure.
    • Damaged keratinocytes release IL-1α, TNF-α and other cytokines → innate inflammation; cumulative sub-threshold insults add up ("cumulative ICD").
    • Repetition drives hyperkeratosis, acanthosis and lichenification; in darker skin, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation is prominent.
    • Risk modifiers: atopy/filaggrin loss-of-function, low humidity/winter, hot water, dominant-hand exposure.

    Allergic contact dermatitis (ACD) — type IV (delayed, T-cell mediated): sensitisation phase (hapten + skin protein → Langerhans cells → lymph node → memory T cells) then elicitation 24–72 h after re-exposure. A damaged irritant-barrier makes sensitisation more likely — so ICD and ACD frequently coexist.

    Construction-worker exposures:

    • Wet cement — alkaline (pH ~12–13) and abrasive → ICD; contains hexavalent chromium (chromate) and cobalt → ACD. EU/UK REACH restricts soluble Cr(VI) in cement to ≤ 2 ppm (ferrous-sulphate reduction), which reduced but did not abolish cement ACD.
    • Rubber/leather gloves — thiurams, carbamates, mercaptobenzothiazole (rubber accelerators); chromate in leather.
    • Epoxy resins, acrylates, isothiazolinone preservatives, solvents/degreasers, hand cleansers with abrasives.

    Risk factors and associations

    • Irritants: water, soaps, detergents, alcohol rubs, solvents, cutting oils, dusts, cold and friction.
    • Allergens: rubber accelerators (gloves), preservatives, fragrances, chromate, nickel, hair dyes, acrylates (nails), plants.
    • Endogenous: atopy, filaggrin mutations, pompholyx (often triggered by heat, sweating, stress, nickel).

    Myths to address

    • "Alcohol gel is worse than soap": alcohol rubs are usually less irritant than repeated soap and water.
    • "Gloves all day protect the skin": long occlusion causes sweat irritation; use cotton liners.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    Hand eczema is driven by barrier damage with or without an allergic (type IV) response.

    1. 1

      Barrier damage

      Repeated wet work and detergents strip lipids from the stratum corneum, increasing water loss and irritation.

    2. 2

      Inflammation

      Damaged keratinocytes release cytokines; in allergic cases, sensitised T cells respond to specific allergens.

    3. 3

      Vesicles

      In pompholyx, intense spongiosis in thick palmar skin forms deep itchy vesicles that do not easily rupture.

    4. 4

      Chronic changes

      Ongoing exposure causes hyperkeratosis, painful fissures and lichenification.

    Histopathology

    • Spongiotic dermatitis; pompholyx shows intraepidermal vesicles in acral skin; chronic disease shows acanthosis and hyperkeratosis.
    • Scrapings exclude tinea; patch testing identifies allergens.

    Clinical features

    StageFeatures
    AcuteErythema, oedema, papules, vesicles (finger sides/palms) that can coalesce into bullae; oozing, crusting; itch, burning, pain
    SubacuteScaling, crusting, fewer vesicles
    ChronicDryness, scaling, hyperkeratosis, lichenification / palmar hyperlinearity, painful fissures (fingertips, knuckles, finger webs), pigment change (hyper/hypo), nail-fold inflammation and nail ridging/dystrophy

    Pattern clues to cause (only clues — patch testing decides):

    • ICD: finger webs, dorsal hands, fingertips ("pulpitis"), ring site ("ring dermatitis"), dominant hand worse; glazed, fissured.
    • ACD: sites of contact with the allergen (e.g. dorsal hands/wrists with gloves, fingertips with acrylates, palms/fingers with tool handles), may spread beyond contact area, vesicular; flares 1–2 days after exposure, improves on holidays.
    • Atopic hand eczema: dorsal hands/wrists, history of flexural eczema/asthma/hay fever, onset in childhood/youth.
    • Hyperkeratotic palmar eczema: thick, fissured central palms, middle-aged men, little itch — closely mimics psoriasis.

    Worked case: itchy erythematous palms + scale + lichenification + PIH + dominant-hand predominance in a 20-yr cement worker → chronic occupational hand eczema; ICD most likely, ACD to chromate/rubber must be tested.

    Patterns to recognise

    • Chronic irritant

      Dry, fissured, glazed; webs and ring site. Site: Dorsal hands, webs.

    • Pompholyx

      Recurrent crops of deep, very itchy vesicles; peels in 2–3 weeks. Site: Finger sides, palms, soles.

    • Infected

      Crusting, weeping, pain: swab and treat. Site: Anywhere.

    Special groups

    • Healthcare and catering workers
      • Involve occupational health early; alcohol rubs plus emollients reduce irritation.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    1. History: onset vs job change; occupational tasks (wet work definition: hands wet or in occlusive gloves > 2 h/day, or > 20 hand-washes/day); products handled (ask for safety data sheets); gloves (type, duration, lining); hobbies (DIY, gardening, hair dye); improvement on holidays/weekends; atopy; previous treatment and response.
    2. Examination: both hands (dorsal/palmar/webs/nails), wrists, feet (tinea, pompholyx, psoriasis), scalp/elbows/nails (psoriasis), flexures (atopy).
    3. Skin scrapings for mycology if any scale is asymmetric or treatment-resistant (tinea manuum is a real possibility in a manual worker).
    4. Patch testing (dermatology): indicated for all chronic hand eczema (> 3 months) or suspected ACD; baseline series (incl. potassium dichromate, cobalt, nickel, rubber mixes, fragrance mixes, isothiazolinones) plus extended/occupational series and patient's own products. Allergens applied on the back for 48 h, read at ~D2 and D4 (± D7); a positive must be judged for relevance to the exposure history.
    5. Prick test / specific IgE only if immediate (type I) symptoms — latex or food proteins (protein contact dermatitis/urticaria). Prick testing is for type I reactions; patch testing for type IV.
    6. Diagnosis of ICD is largely by exclusion (negative/irrelevant patch tests + irritant history).

    Differential diagnoses

    PhotoConditionDistinguishing features
    		Palmoplantar psoriasisSharp margins, thick silvery scale, nails.
    		Tinea manuum‘Two feet, one hand’; white scale in creases.
    		Keratolysis exfoliativaNo itch, no redness; air-filled peeling collarettes.
    		Palmoplantar pustulosisSterile pustules → brown dots; smokers.
    		ScabiesBurrows in webs and wrists; night itch; contacts.

    Pompholyx (recurrent vesicular hand eczema)

    • What: recurrent, symmetrical, intensely itchy eruption of deep-seated 1–2 mm fluid-filled vesicles ("tapioca"/"sago") on the sides of fingers, palms and soles; resolves with peeling in 2–3 weeks. Accounts for ~1 in 5 hand-dermatitis patch-test referrals. Not caused by blocked sweat glands despite the name.
    • Associations/triggers: atopy (mixed evidence); contact allergy (nickel, chromium, cobalt — oral nickel/cobalt can flare); irritants; heat, hyperhidrosis and season; tinea pedis (id/dermatophytide reaction — treat the feet); stress; smoking; IVIG; UVA.
    • Diagnose: clinical; scrapings if unilateral or feet involved; patch test if recurrent.
    • DDx: KE (air not fluid, no itch), PPP (pustules not vesicles), tinea (vesicular/bullous, unilateral), bullous pemphigoid (older, tense bullae elsewhere), scabies, HFMD.
    • Manage (IP): potassium permanganate 1:10,000 soaks or wet compresses for acute weeping; potent/very potent TCS short course (ointment; cream if very wet); emollients and soap substitute; drain large blisters with sterile needle leaving roof; treat infection; treat tinea pedis; manage hyperhidrosis. Refer if frequent/severe: patch testing, PUVA, alitretinoin, delgocitinib, systemic immunosuppressants.

    Management

    Management for pharmacist/IP

    Step 1 — Skin protection & avoidance (all patients):

    • Identify and reduce/replace irritants and allergens (hierarchy of control: eliminate → substitute → engineering → PPE). For cement: dry handling where possible, low-chromate cement, prompt washing of splashes.
    • Gloves: appropriate material for the chemical (e.g. nitrile; avoid rubber if accelerator-allergic; leather work gloves can contain chromate); keep inside dry; cotton liners; limit occlusion time; don't wear gloves on wet or contaminated skin.
    • Hand washing: lukewarm water, soap substitute/emollient wash, rinse and pat dry thoroughly (including webs); in healthcare/food settings alcohol hand rub is often better tolerated than repeated soap and water.
    • Emollients liberally and frequently (after every wash, at breaks, and greasy ointment under cotton gloves overnight); fragrance-free; preservative-allergic patients need tolerated products. "Barrier creams" are no substitute for gloves/emollients.
    • Remove rings for wet work.

    Step 2 — Topical anti-inflammatory (IP prescribing):

    • Potent topical corticosteroid (e.g. mometasone furoate 0.1% or betamethasone valerate 0.1% ointment) once daily for 2–4 weeks for a flare; very potent (clobetasol propionate 0.05%) often needed for thick palmar skin — short courses, then taper to twice-weekly proactive maintenance on usual sites. Ointments preferred on hands. A history of "topical dexamethasone" with partial temporary relief = undertreatment + continuing exposure.
    • Topical calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus 0.1% ointment, off-label for hands) as steroid-sparing maintenance, especially dorsal skin.
    • Treat secondary infection (swab; oral flucloxacillin or per local guidance if impetiginised). Potassium permanganate 1:10,000 soaks for weeping/vesicular phases (stains; never ingest).
    • Oral antihistamines don't treat the eczema; a sedating one at night only if sleep is disturbed.

    Step 3 — Refer to dermatology (routine; sooner if severe or work at risk):

    • Not controlled after ~4–6 weeks of optimised potent/very potent TCS + protection; or chronic > 3 months (for patch testing); or diagnosis uncertain.
    • Specialist options: delgocitinib 2% cream (topical pan-JAK inhibitor; NICE TA1107, 5 Nov 2025 — moderate–severe CHE in adults when TCS ineffective/unsuitable; started by secondary care); alitretinoin oral 30 mg (10 mg if not tolerated) for 12–24 weeks (NICE TA177 — severe CHE unresponsive to ~4 weeks potent TCS; dermatologist-initiated; teratogenic — Pregnancy Prevention Programme; lipids/headache); hand PUVA / narrowband UVB; systemic ciclosporin, methotrexate, azathioprine (off-label); dupilumab helps atopic hand eczema within its atopic dermatitis licence. (DELTA FORCE, Lancet 2025: delgocitinib cream outperformed oral alitretinoin with fewer adverse events.) Short oral prednisolone only for severe acute flares under specialist advice — not for chronic disease.

    Occupational/UK specifics:

    • Encourage occupational health referral and workplace skin surveillance (HSE guidance; COSHH duty on employers to assess and control skin exposures).
    • RIDDOR: occupational dermatitis is a reportable disease by the employer when a doctor diagnoses it in writing and the work involves significant exposure to a known irritant/sensitiser — advise patient to inform employer/OH; the clinician's role is accurate written diagnosis.
    • Industrial Injuries Disablement Benefit can apply to prescribed occupational dermatitis (PD D5).
    • Fit note if needed; avoid premature advice to quit the job — modification often succeeds, but prognosis is worse with persistent exposure/ACD.

    Treatment ladder

    1. 1

      Protect · every patient

      Reduce or replace irritants and allergens. Right gloves, cotton liners, short occlusion; remove rings for wet work.

    2. 2

      Flare

      Potent TCS (mometasone 0.1% or betamethasone valerate 0.1% ointment); Very potent TCS (clobetasol 0.05%)

    3. 3

      Add-ons

      Tacrolimus 0.1% ointment (off-label) as steroid-sparing maintenance. Potassium permanganate 1:10,000 soaks for weeping or vesicular phases.

    4. 4

      Refer · dermatology

      Not controlled after ~4–6 weeks optimised TCS, chronic > 3 months, or uncertain. Specialist: delgocitinib 2% cream (NICE TA1107), alitretinoin (NICE TA177; Pregnancy Prevention Programme), phototherapy, systemics.

    Caution

    Prescribing limits and pitfalls

    • Antihistamines: Do not treat eczema; sedating one at night only for sleep.
    • Barrier creams: No substitute for gloves and emollients.
    • Oral prednisolone: Severe acute flares only, under specialist advice.

    Referral

    • Same day

      Painful punched-out erosions or sudden worsening: eczema herpeticum.

    • Urgent

      Spreading infection, fever or lymphangitis.

    • Routine

      Work-related: occupational health, document exposures, patch-test referral.

    • Don’t

      One-sided scaly hand + tinea pedis: scrape before steroids.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Painful punched-out erosions or sudden worsening: eczema herpeticum, same-day.
    • Spreading infection, fever or lymphangitis.
    • Work-related dermatitis: advise occupational health, document exposures, patch-test referral.
    • One-sided scaly hand with tinea pedis (‘two feet, one hand’): scrape for tinea manuum before steroids.

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Painful fissures, secondary infection (S. aureus), eczema herpeticum.
    • Loss of work and income; long-term disability.
    • Sensitisation to treatments (gloves, preservatives).

    Prognosis

    • Often chronic and relapsing; only about half are clear years later, especially with atopy, allergy or continued exposure.
    • Early diagnosis, glove use and job adaptation improve outlook.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • Most chronic hand eczema relapses if exposure continues — success = protection + adequate steroid + maintenance, not steroid alone.

    • Fingertip-unit guide: one FTU covers both sides of one hand.

    • Thin palmar skin is uncommon — palms tolerate very potent TCS better than other sites, but dorsal hands thin easily; review every 4 weeks.

    • Dominant-hand predominance, ring-site eczema, and finger-web involvement → irritant. Dorsal hand + wrist cut-off at glove cuff → think glove allergy.

    • Always look at the feet in a hand rash.

    • Healthcare hand hygiene: prefer alcohol rub when hands not visibly soiled, dry hands fully before gloving, emollient at breaks and end of shift, report skin problems early (NHS staff hand-dermatitis pocket guides support this).

    • Concise answers to the six key questions, drawn from UK guidance.

    • One-liner: acute = wet (erythema, oedema, vesicles, oozing); chronic = dry (scale, thickening, lichenification, fissures, pigment and nail change).

    • Exogenous: irritants (wet work, detergents, solvents, friction), contact allergens, proteins (food, latex).

    • Endogenous: atopy/filaggrin variants, family history,.

    • Modifiers: cold dry weather, frequent washing/sanitiser use (sharp rise during COVID-19 — PPE, washing and stress), stress, secondary microbes (S. aureus, dermatophytes).

    • High-risk jobs: hairdressers, healthcare workers, cleaners, catering/food handlers, bakers, metalworkers (metalworking fluids), construction (cement), mechanics, florists/gardeners.

    • Water/wet work (especially hot, hard or chlorinated); soaps, detergents, cleaning agents, bleach/ammonia; antiseptics, disinfectants, hand sanitisers (alcohol more of a stinging than a barrier issue); solvents/degreasers; acids and alkalis (incl. wet cement); oils and metalworking fluids; dusts, powders, soil; foods (citrus, onion, garlic, raw meat/fish juices); plants; occlusion and friction (gloves, tools); topical retinoids/benzoyl peroxide on skin.

    • (Poison ivy/urushiol is a North American allergen — mention for completeness, rare in UK.)

    • Detailed exposure history → patch testing (baseline + targeted series + own materials, read D2/D4 ± D7) → assess current relevance (does the allergen exist in the patient's environment, at the site, with timing that fits?) → trial of avoidance (improvement supports relevance; repeat open application test can help) → education with allergen information sheets and product checking (INCI labels, safety data sheets).

    • Prevention pillars: education, protective gloves (right type, liners, short periods), skin care (emollients, soap substitutes), substitution of hazards, workplace surveillance, early reporting.

    Self-check quiz

    5 questions · optional
    1. Q1When is hand eczema ‘chronic’?Reveal

      Lasting more than 3 months, or relapsing at least twice a year despite adequate treatment.

    2. Q2‘Two feet, one hand’ suggests?Reveal

      Tinea manuum (with tinea pedis): take scrapings before steroids.

    3. Q3Typical first-line steroid for a hand eczema flare?Reveal

      A potent TCS ointment (e.g. mometasone 0.1%) once daily for 2–4 weeks, then twice-weekly maintenance.

    4. Q4Who needs patch testing?Reveal

      All chronic hand eczema (> 3 months) or suspected allergic contact dermatitis.

    5. Q5Painful punched-out erosions on eczematous hands?Reveal

      Eczema herpeticum: same-day assessment.
