    Head lice (pediculosis capitis)

    Only a live louse confirms infestation. Wet combing or dimeticone 4% are first-line; permethrin is not recommended in the UK.

    Also calledhead licenitspediculosisdimeticonewet combingbug busting
    Related pagesScabiesImpetigoPapular urticaria & insect bites

    Key facts

    • Infestation of the scalp hair by the louse Pediculus humanus capitis.
    • Very common in children aged 4–14; spread by head-to-head contact (lice crawl, they cannot jump or fly).
    • Diagnose only by finding a live louse with wet or dry detection combing; nits alone do not prove active infestation.
    • Options: wet combing (bug busting) or a physically acting product such as dimeticone 4%, repeated after 7 days.
    • Check all household members and treat only those with live lice; children need not miss school.

    Overview

    Head lice are scalp infestations with Pediculus humanus capitis, a wingless blood-feeding insect about 2–3 mm long. They are commonest in children aged about 4–11, especially girls. They are not a sign of poor hygiene and do not transmit disease in the UK.

    Epidemiology

    • Who
      • Worldwide, all social groups; commonest in primary-school children, more often girls (longer hair, closer contact).
    • Spread
      • Direct head-to-head contact; sharing brushes or hats is a minor route.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    • Spread is by direct head-to-head contact. Lice crawl; they cannot jump, fly or swim. Hats and brushes rarely matter because lice die within about 1–2 days off the scalp.
    • Females glue eggs near the scalp. Eggs hatch in about 7–10 days; adults live about 3–4 weeks on the host.
    • Nits are empty white egg cases that move out as hair grows. Nits far from the scalp usually mean an old infestation.
    • Itch may take 4–6 weeks to appear at first infestation; many children never itch.

    Risk factors and associations

    • Risk factors: female sex, more children in the family, sharing beds, combs and hats.
    • Resistance to older insecticides (permethrin, malathion) is widespread, so physical or mechanical methods are preferred.

    Myths to address

    • "Lice mean dirty hair": lice prefer clean or dirty hair equally.
    • "Lice jump": they crawl from hair to hair.
    • "Nits mean active lice": empty egg cases stay glued to hair for months.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    The louse lives on the scalp and feeds on blood; itch is an allergic reaction to its saliva.

    1. 1

      Eggs glued to hair

      Females lay up to 10 eggs a day, cemented to the hair shaft close to the scalp; they hatch in 7–10 days.

    2. 2

      Nymphs mature

      Nymphs become adults in about 7–10 days; adults live about 30 days and die within 1–2 days off the head.

    3. 3

      Feeding and itch

      Lice feed on blood several times a day; sensitisation to saliva causes itch after 4–6 weeks of a first infestation.

    4. 4

      Detection

      Combing wet, conditioned hair with a fine-toothed comb is the most reliable way to find live lice.

    Histopathology

    • Not relevant; diagnosis is by detection combing and seeing a live louse.

    Clinical features

    • Scalp itch (often absent), tickling, and excoriations — especially behind the ears and at the nape.
    • Live grey-brown lice that move away from light; eggs/nits cemented to the shaft.
    • Complications: secondary impetigo, occipital lymphadenopathy, eczematisation, sleep disturbance and school stigma.

    Signs to recognise

    • Adult louse

      Six legs with claws for gripping hair. Site: Scalp hair.

    • Eggs / nits

      Cemented to the shaft near the scalp. Site: Hair shafts.

    • Nape dermatitis

      Itchy excoriated skin with nits. Site: Nape.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    Only a live louse confirms active infestation (NICE CKS). Nits alone do not justify treatment.

    • Detection combing with a fine-toothed plastic comb on wet conditioned hair (or dry) is more sensitive than looking alone.
    • Check all household members and treat only those with live lice, all on the same day.
    • Nits stay put; dandruff and hair casts brush or slide off.

    Differential diagnoses

    Management

    Treat only when a live louse is found. Choice depends on preference, age, asthma/eczema and previous treatment.

    UK guidance

    Not recommended in the UK

    Permethrin creme rinses (unsuitable contact time and widespread resistance), shampoos alone, herbal/essential-oil products, electric combs, and “preventive” treatment of people without live lice.

    Checking cure

    • Detection-comb 2–3 days after finishing treatment and again after 7 days.
    • If live lice persist, check technique then switch to a different mode of action.
    • Oral ivermectin or co-trimoxazole for resistant lice are specialist/off-label — refer rather than start them yourself.

    Treatment ladder

    1. 1

      Option for everyone

      Wet combing (Bug Busting): conditioner + detection comb on days 1, 5, 9 and 13; check day 17. Fine for infants, pregnancy, asthma and eczema.

    2. 2

      Physical insecticide

      Dimeticone 4% lotion / spray / gel; Isopropyl myristate + cyclomethicone

    3. 3

      Chemical

      Malathion 0.5% aqueous liquid

    4. 4

      Check cure

      Detection-comb 2–3 days after finishing, and again after 7 days. Live lice persist: check technique, then switch mode of action.

    5. 5

      Resistant

      Oral ivermectin or co-trimoxazole are specialist / off-label: refer.

    Caution

    Prescribing limits and pitfalls

    • Not recommended (UK): Permethrin creme rinse, shampoos alone, herbal oils, electric combs, ‘preventive’ treatment.
    • No exclusion: No school exclusion, boil-washing or fumigation.

    Referral

    • Urgent

      Pubic lice on a child’s eyelashes → safeguarding pathway.

    • Routine

      Infant under 6 months: wet combing only; seek advice if it fails.

    • Routine

      Repeated failure despite correct technique.

    • Routine

      Extensive secondary infection, severe eczematous scalp, or suspected misdiagnosis.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Infant under 6 months (wet combing only; seek advice if this fails).
    • Repeated failure despite correct technique.
    • Extensive secondary infection or severe eczematous scalp.
    • Pubic lice on a child’s eyelashes → safeguarding pathway.
    • Suspected misdiagnosis (tinea capitis, psoriasis).

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Scalp excoriations and dermatitis; secondary bacterial infection (impetigo) and enlarged neck nodes.
    • Anxiety, stigma and school disruption.

    Prognosis

    • Curable; reinfestation from untreated contacts is common.
    • Treatment failure is usually due to incorrect use, resistance or reinfestation.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • No live louse, no treatment.

    • There is no school exclusion and no need to boil-wash bedding or fumigate. Inform school only as local practice.

    • Asthma/eczema: prefer wet combing or aqueous dimeticone; avoid alcohol-based lotions.

    • Itch can persist a few days after cure; leftover nits alone do not mean failure.

    • UK teaching point: course materials that recommend permethrin for head lice are out of step with CKS.

    Self-check quiz

    5 questions · optional
    1. Q1What confirms active head lice?Reveal

      A live louse (found by detection combing). Nits alone don’t.

    2. Q2Wet-combing schedule?Reveal

      Days 1, 5, 9 and 13, with a check on day 17.

    3. Q3How is dimeticone 4% used?Reveal

      On dry hair, left on (often overnight per product), washed out, repeated after 7 days.

    4. Q4Is permethrin recommended for head lice in the UK?Reveal

      No: unsuitable contact time and widespread resistance.

    5. Q5Does a child need to stay off school?Reveal

      No school exclusion is needed.
