    Herpes simplex labialis (cold sores)

    Recurrent grouped vesicles on the lip border triggered by sun, illness, stress or menstruation. Self-limiting in healthy adults; know when to use oral antivirals and when it is an emergency (eczema herpeticum, eye, neonates, immunosuppression).

    Also calledcold soresHSV-1herpes labialisfever blisteraciclovir creamherpetic whitlow+2
    Related pagesHerpes zoster (shingles)Erythema multiformeImpetigoEczema / dermatitis

    Key facts

    • Recurrent HSV-1 infection of the lip border: tingling, then grouped vesicles, crusting and healing in 7–10 days.
    • Most adults carry HSV-1; the virus lies latent in the trigeminal ganglion and reactivates with sun, fever, stress, menstruation or trauma.
    • Infectious from tingling until fully crusted; also spread by asymptomatic shedding.
    • Self-care and OTC aciclovir cream for mild episodes; oral antiviral for severe, frequent or immunocompromised cases.
    • Same-day assessment for eczema herpeticum, eye involvement, neonates and the immunocompromised.

    Overview

    Diagnosis confirmed from the course case: fever + recurrent perioral vesicles at the same site; the diagnosis is recurrent herpes simplex labialis (HSV-1); questions covered confirmation tests and treatment of recurrences.

    Recurrent vesicular eruption of the lips/perioral skin due to reactivation of latent herpes simplex virus, usually HSV-1 (HSV-2 increasingly causes orolabial and HSV-1 genital disease). "Cold sores" / "fever blisters" — fever is a classic trigger. Primary infection is often childhood herpetic gingivostomatitis.

    Epidemiology

    • Very common
      • HSV-1 seroprevalence rises with age: most adults worldwide; under half of UK university entrants (less childhood exposure).
      • About 20–40% of infected people get recurrent cold sores.
    • Primary infection
      • Usually in childhood, often silent or as gingivostomatitis; HSV-2 mainly after puberty (genital).

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    Spread by direct contact with lesions or asymptomatic viral shedding (saliva). After primary infection, HSV travels along sensory nerves to the trigeminal ganglion where it stays latent. Triggers of reactivation: fever/intercurrent infection (URTI), UV light, cold wind, menstruation, stress, fatigue, trauma (dental work, lip procedures, laser), immunosuppression. Recurrences hit the same site each time. Immunosuppression (HIV, transplant, chemo) → larger, longer, chronic ulcerative lesions; zinc deficiency is sometimes suggested as a contributor (weak evidence).

    Risk factors and associations

    • Triggers for recurrence: UV light, fever and colds, stress, fatigue, menstruation, lip trauma, dental work, immunosuppression.
    • Transmission by kissing, sharing cups or towels; infectious for about 7–12 days per episode.

    Myths to address

    • "Cold sores only spread when visible": asymptomatic shedding can transmit HSV.
    • "Antiviral cream cures it": it only shortens episodes if started at the first tingle.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    HSV is a neurotropic DNA virus: it hides in sensory ganglia and travels along nerves to the skin.

    1. 1

      Primary infection

      Virus enters through mucosa or broken skin, replicates in keratinocytes and travels up sensory nerves.

    2. 2

      Latency

      HSV persists latent in the trigeminal ganglion for life, invisible to the immune system.

    3. 3

      Reactivation

      Triggers (UV, fever, stress) reactivate the virus, which travels down the nerve: prodromal tingling or burning for 6–48 hours.

    4. 4

      Vesicles and healing

      Viral replication in keratinocytes causes ballooning and cell fusion, giving clustered vesicles that ulcerate, crust and heal in 7–10 days.

    Histopathology

    • Intraepidermal vesicle with ballooning degeneration, multinucleated giant cells and nuclear moulding (Tzanck smear).
    • Diagnosis is clinical; viral PCR swab if atypical, severe or immunocompromised.

    Clinical features

    • Prodrome (hours–1 day): tingling, burning, itching at the site.
    • Then grouped small vesicles on an erythematous base at the vermilion border/perioral skin → pustules → painful erosions → crust → heal in 7–10 days without scarring.
    • Recurrences: a few per year typical; frequent (>6/year) in some.
    • Other HSV patterns: cheek/nose, herpetic whitlow (finger), herpes gladiatorum (contact sports), eczema herpeticum (atopic skin), HSV keratitis (dendritic ulcer), HSV-associated erythema multiforme (target lesions 1–2 weeks after an outbreak).

    Patterns to recognise

    • Crusting stage

      Crust extending onto the skin above the lip. Site: Lip, perioral.

    • Skin of colour

      Redness less obvious; look for grouped vesicles. Site: Lip.

    • Other facial site

      Recurrent HSV can appear on the cheek. Site: Cheek.

    • Herpetic whitlow

      Painful vesicles and swelling of a fingertip. Site: Finger.

    • Eczema herpeticum

      Monomorphic punched-out erosions on eczema. Site: Eczematous skin.

    Special groups

    • Atopic eczema
      • Risk of eczema herpeticum (widespread punched-out erosions, fever): emergency.
    • Neonates and pregnancy
      • Avoid kissing newborns during an episode; neonatal HSV is life-threatening.
    • Immunocompromised
      • Large, persistent or atypical ulcers; needs oral or IV antivirals and specialist advice.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    Clinical in typical recurrent labialis. If atypical, severe, immunocompromised, near the eye or recurrent erythema multiforme: viral swab for HSV PCR (fast, most sensitive; preferred over culture/Tzanck smear). Serology (IgG) has little role in recurrent labialis.

    Clinical

    • Typical recurrent labialis: clinical.

    Swab

    • HSV PCR if atypical, severe, immunocompromised, near the eye, or recurrent EM.

    Serology

    • Little role in recurrent labialis.

    Who is at risk

    • Eczema, pregnancy near term, neonates nearby, immunosuppression?

    Management

    Based on NICE CKS Herpes simplex – oral:

    Self-care / OTC (immunocompetent)

    • Usually self-limiting; paracetamol/ibuprofen for pain; simple analgesic gels or creams; adequate fluids.
    • Topical aciclovir 5% cream (OTC, five times daily for 5 days, start at the first tingle) — modest benefit (shortens episode by roughly half a day to a day); penciclovir 1% (Rx/OTC per product). Barrier/hydrocolloid cold-sore patches may help comfort and reduce spread.
    • Hygiene: don't touch/pick; wash hands; don't share towels, cups, lip products; avoid kissing, especially neonates and people with eczema or immunosuppression; avoid oral sex during lesions.
    • Sunscreen lip balm (SPF 30+) if UV is a trigger.

    Oral antiviral (prescriber)

    • Consider for severe or frequent episodes in immunocompetent adults, e.g. episodic aciclovir 400 mg five times daily for 5 days (start at prodrome) or valaciclovir 2 g twice daily for 1 day (cold-sore regimen in the UK SmPC; check local formulary).
    • Suppressive therapy for frequent/severe recurrences, HSV-associated erythema multiforme, or before a known trigger: aciclovir 400 mg twice daily, review after 6–12 months (specialist advice often sought; off-label in some uses).
    • Immunocompromised: oral antivirals (higher dose/longer) and seek specialist advice; chronic/large ulcers may need IV therapy.

    Caution

    Prescribing limits and pitfalls

    • No steroid or antibiotic creams: Unless secondary impetigo is confirmed.
    • Timing: Benefit falls sharply once vesicles form.

    Referral

    • Same day

      Punched-out erosions spreading over eczema: eczema herpeticum.

    • Same day

      Red painful eye, photophobia, blurred vision, vesicles on nose tip: ophthalmology. Never topical steroid.

    • Urgent

      Neonate (< 1 month) or primary HSV late in pregnancy.

    • Urgent

      Immunosuppressed with extensive or non-healing lesions (> 2 weeks).

    • Routine

      Child with gingivostomatitis not drinking, or recurrent erythema multiforme.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Painful punched-out erosions spreading over eczema: eczema herpeticum, same-day.
    • Red painful eye, photophobia or blurred vision: same-day ophthalmology (herpetic keratitis).
    • Newborn contact, pregnancy near term, or immunosuppressed with extensive or non-healing lesions: urgent specialist advice.

    Red flags / refer

    • Eczema herpeticum — punched-out erosions spreading over eczema, unwell → same-day assessment/oral or IV aciclovir; involve ophthalmology if periocular.
    • Eye involvement (red painful eye, photophobia, vesicles on eyelid/nose tip) → same-day ophthalmology; never give topical steroid.
    • Neonate (<1 month) with any suspected HSV, or primary HSV in late pregnancy → urgent.
    • Immunocompromised, lesions >2 weeks or very extensive, systemic upset, dehydration in primary gingivostomatitis (child not drinking), recurrent erythema multiforme, diagnostic doubt → GP/specialist.

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Eczema herpeticum (Kaposi varicelliform eruption).
    • Herpetic keratitis and eye involvement.
    • Herpetic whitlow (finger).
    • Secondary impetigo.
    • Erythema multiforme triggered by recurrent HSV.
    • Rarely encephalitis or disseminated infection.

    Prognosis

    • Lifelong latent infection; recurrences often become less frequent with age.
    • Episodes heal without scarring in 7–10 days.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • Start antivirals at the first tingle — benefit falls sharply once vesicles form.

    • Recurrent lesions at the same place + fever = HSV; one-off band of vesicles = zoster.

    • Don't prescribe topical corticosteroid or antibiotic creams for cold sores (unless confirmed secondary impetigo).

    • Patients with frequent severe recurrences: consider underlying immunosuppression (e.g. HIV) and offer testing if risk.

    • Aciclovir: dose-reduce in renal impairment; keep hydrated.

    Self-check quiz

    5 questions · optional
    1. Q1Where does HSV-1 lie latent?Reveal

      In the trigeminal (sensory) ganglion.

    2. Q2OTC aciclovir cream regimen?Reveal

      5% cream five times daily for 5 days, started at the first tingle.

    3. Q3An oral option for a severe cold sore in an adult?Reveal

      Aciclovir 400 mg five times daily for 5 days, or valaciclovir 2 g twice daily for 1 day.

    4. Q4Vesicles on the nose tip with a red painful eye?Reveal

      Possible herpetic eye disease: same-day ophthalmology; no topical steroid.

    5. Q5Which test if atypical?Reveal

      Viral swab for HSV PCR.
