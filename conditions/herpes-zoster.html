    Herpes zoster (shingles)

    Reactivation of varicella-zoster virus in one dermatome: pain, then grouped vesicles on a red base. Start an antiviral within 72 hours when indicated, protect the eye, and think about immunosuppression in severe or recurrent cases.

    Also calledshingleszosterVZVdermatomal rashophthalmic zosterHutchinson sign+4
    Related pagesHerpes simplex labialis (cold sores)Impetigo

    Key facts

    • Reactivation of varicella-zoster virus (VZV) from a sensory ganglion, causing a painful unilateral dermatomal vesicular rash.
    • Lifetime risk about 1 in 4; risk rises sharply after 50 and with immunosuppression.
    • Pain or tingling often precedes the rash by 2–3 days; the rash crusts in 7–10 days.
    • Antiviral (e.g. aciclovir 800 mg five times daily for 7 days) within 72 h for most adults over 50, or with eye, immunosuppression, severe pain or rash (CKS).
    • Ophthalmic zoster (Hutchinson's sign) and immunocompromised patients need same-day assessment; offer the shingles vaccine (Shingrix) to eligible groups.

    Overview

    Reactivation of latent varicella-zoster virus (VZV) from a dorsal root or cranial nerve ganglion, producing a painful, unilateral, dermatomal vesicular eruption. Lifetime risk ~1 in 4; risk rises with age and with reduced cell-mediated immunity. In a young adult (Case 2, 32M), zoster should prompt an HIV test — it is an HIV indicator condition (BHIVA/NICE).

    Epidemiology

    • Who
      • Anyone who has had chickenpox (or, rarely, the live varicella vaccine).
      • Incidence rises with age: most cases after 50; up to half of people reaching 85 have had it.
    • Risk factors
      • Immunosuppression (HIV, cancer, chemotherapy, transplant, high-dose steroids, JAK inhibitors), older age, stress.
      • Recurrence is uncommon (about 1–5%).

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    After chickenpox, VZV stays latent in sensory ganglia. Waning VZV-specific T-cell immunity (age, HIV, chemotherapy, transplant, high-dose steroids, biologics/JAK inhibitors, stress) allows reactivation; virus travels down the sensory nerve → neuritis (prodromal pain) then epidermal infection (vesicles). In HIV, zoster can occur at any CD4 count but is more frequent, more severe, recurrent, multi-dermatomal, necrotic, chronic (verrucous) or disseminated as CD4 falls.

    Risk factors and associations

    • Triggers sometimes identified: local trauma or surgery, radiotherapy, intercurrent infection.
    • Zoster is infectious to people who have not had chickenpox (direct contact with fluid from blisters) until lesions crust.

    Myths to address

    • "You can catch shingles from chickenpox": contacts can catch chickenpox from shingles, not shingles itself.
    • "It can't come back": recurrence is uncommon but possible, especially with immunosuppression.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    After chickenpox, VZV hides in sensory ganglia; when cell-mediated immunity falls, it reactivates and travels down the nerve.

    1. 1

      Latency after chickenpox

      VZV travels up sensory nerves from chickenpox lesions and stays latent in dorsal root or cranial nerve ganglia for decades.

    2. 2

      Reactivation

      Waning VZV-specific T-cell immunity (age, immunosuppression) lets the virus multiply in the ganglion, causing inflammation and prodromal pain.

    3. 3

      Spread to the dermatome

      Virus moves down the sensory nerve to the skin supplied by that ganglion, producing grouped vesicles on a red base in one dermatome, not crossing the midline.

    4. 4

      Nerve damage

      Ganglion and nerve inflammation can cause post-herpetic neuralgia and, with motor nerve involvement, weakness (e.g. Ramsay Hunt facial palsy).

    5. 5

      Healing

      Vesicles become pustules and crust in 7–10 days and heal in 2–4 weeks, sometimes with scarring and pigment change.

    Histopathology

    • Intraepidermal vesicles with ballooning degeneration and multinucleated giant keratinocytes (identical to herpes simplex).
    • Diagnosis is clinical; PCR of vesicle fluid if atypical or immunocompromised.

    Clinical features

    • Prodrome 1–5 days: burning, tingling, itching or severe pain in a dermatome (can mimic MI, pleurisy, renal colic, cholecystitis); malaise, fever.
    • Rash: erythematous papules → grouped vesicles on a red base → pustules → crusts over 7–10 days, unilateral, respecting the midline, one (or two adjacent) dermatomes — Case 2: T9–T10 back.
    • Commonest: thoracic dermatomes, then ophthalmic (V1).
    • Immunocompromised features: haemorrhagic/necrotic lesions, >1–2 dermatomes, >20 vesicles outside the primary dermatome (disseminated), slow healing (>2 weeks), recurrent episodes, chronic verrucous lesions, visceral involvement (pneumonitis, hepatitis, encephalitis).
    • Herpes zoster ophthalmicus (HZO): V1 forehead/scalp/eyelid; Hutchinson's sign (lesions on tip/side of nose → nasociliary nerve → high risk of ocular involvement).
    • Ramsay Hunt syndrome: ear/external canal vesicles + ipsilateral facial palsy ± vertigo, tinnitus, hearing loss.

    Patterns to recognise

    • Thoracic band

      Grouped vesicles, one side. Site: Chest, back.

    • Crusting / haemorrhagic

      Partly haemorrhagic: check for immunosuppression. Site: Dermatome.

    • Ophthalmic (V1)

      Forehead and eyelid; same-day eye review. Site: Forehead, eye.

    Special groups

    • Immunocompromised
      • Multi-dermatomal, disseminated or necrotic zoster; needs urgent specialist advice and often IV aciclovir.
    • Pregnancy
      • Zoster in pregnancy does not cause congenital varicella; seek specialist advice before prescribing antivirals.
    • Skin of colour
      • Erythema may be subtle; pigment change and keloid scarring after healing are more common.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    Usually clinical. Swab vesicle base for VZV PCR if atypical, immunocompromised, or diagnosis uncertain (PCR has replaced Tzanck smear/culture). In immunosuppression (e.g. HIV): VZV PCR, HIV viral load + CD4 count, FBC, U&E/LFT (renal dosing of aciclovir), syphilis/hepatitis screen as part of HIV care; ophthalmic exam if V1.

    Clinical

    • Usually clinical: dermatomal, unilateral, painful.

    Swab

    • VZV PCR of vesicle base if atypical, immunocompromised or unsure.

    Immunosuppressed

    • HIV viral load + CD4, FBC, U&E / LFT (renal dosing).

    V1

    • Check the nose tip and the eye; ophthalmic exam.

    Management

    Based on NICE CKS Shingles themes:

    Self-care / all patients

    • Keep rash clean and dry; loose clothing; non-adherent dressing if needed; avoid topical antivirals/antibiotic creams; paracetamol ± NSAID for mild pain.
    • Infectious (can cause chickenpox in non-immune contacts) until all lesions crusted (usually 5–7 days): cover lesions; avoid pregnant women without chickenpox history, neonates and immunosuppressed people.

    Antiviral (immunocompetent)

    • Offer oral antiviral within 72 h of rash onset if: age ≥50, non-truncal involvement (neck, limbs, perineum), moderate–severe pain or rash, or other risk; consider up to 1 week in those at higher risk.
    • Aciclovir 800 mg five times daily for 7 days (first-line in many formularies); alternatives valaciclovir 1 g three times daily for 7 days or famciclovir 500 mg three times daily for 7 days (better adherence; useful in poor-adherence/renal dosing contexts). Adjust for renal impairment.
    • NHS Pharmacy First (England) includes a shingles clinical pathway for adults — follow the current PGD inclusion/exclusion criteria (e.g. pregnancy, severe immunosuppression, ophthalmic/ear involvement are referral/exclusion points).

    Immunocompromised / HIV (Case 2)

    • Offer antiviral regardless of time since onset (new lesions may keep forming) and seek same-day specialist advice (HIV/ID team). Mild localised disease in stable HIV may be treated orally (often longer course, e.g. aciclovir 800 mg 5×/day, valaciclovir or famciclovir, ≥7–10 days until lesions crust); severe, multi-dermatomal, disseminated, ophthalmic or visceral disease → admission for IV aciclovir (e.g. 10 mg/kg 8-hourly, renally adjusted).
    • Ensure HIV care/ART is optimised (risk of IRIS-related zoster soon after ART start).

    Pain / PHN

    • Acute moderate–severe pain: consider short-term stronger analgesia; neuropathic agents (amitriptyline, duloxetine, gabapentin, pregabalin) for neuropathic pain/PHN per NICE CG173; capsaicin cream for localised PHN once healed; lidocaine plasters only per specialist/local policy.

    Treatment ladder

    1. 1

      Everyone

      Clean, dry, loose clothing; non-adherent dressing; no antiviral or antibiotic creams. Paracetamol ± NSAID.

    2. 2

      Antiviral · within 72 h

      Aciclovir; Valaciclovir; Famciclovir

    3. 3

      Pharmacy First (England)

      Shingles pathway for adults: follow the PGD; pregnancy, severe immunosuppression, eye or ear involvement = refer.

    4. 4

      Immunocompromised

      Antiviral regardless of timing; same-day specialist advice. Severe, disseminated, ophthalmic or visceral → IV aciclovir admission.

    Caution

    Prescribing limits and pitfalls

    • Neuropathic pain / PHN: Amitriptyline, duloxetine, gabapentin or pregabalin (NICE CG173); capsaicin once healed.
    • Vaccination: Shingrix (non-live) for eligible adults: check Green Book ch. 28a. Zostavax (live) contraindicated in immunosuppression.

    Referral

    • Same day

      Forehead, eyelid or nose tip (Hutchinson), eye pain or visual change → ophthalmology.

    • Same day

      Facial palsy with ear vesicles (Ramsay Hunt) → ENT.

    • Urgent

      Immunosuppressed, disseminated or multi-dermatomal: specialist advice, often IV aciclovir.

    • Routine

      Under 50 with no cause, or recurrent zoster: offer an HIV test.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Rash on the forehead, eyelid or tip of the nose (Hutchinson sign), eye pain or visual change: same-day ophthalmology.
    • Immunosuppressed, disseminated or multi-dermatomal zoster: urgent specialist advice, often IV aciclovir.
    • Facial palsy with ear vesicles (Ramsay Hunt): same-day ENT.
    • Zoster in an under-50 without a cause, or recurrent zoster: consider an HIV test.

    Red flags / refer

    • Same-day ophthalmology: V1 rash, Hutchinson's sign, red eye, visual change, photophobia.
    • Same-day ENT/medical: Ramsay Hunt (facial palsy).
    • Admit/urgent: severe immunosuppression, disseminated rash, systemic illness (pneumonitis, hepatitis, encephalitis, meningism), motor weakness, urinary retention (sacral zoster), neonates.
    • Pregnancy: seek specialist advice; non-immune pregnant contacts need assessment for VZIG/antiviral.

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Post-herpetic neuralgia (pain over 3 months): the commonest complication, more likely with age.
    • Herpes zoster ophthalmicus: keratitis, uveitis, visual loss.
    • Ramsay Hunt syndrome: facial palsy, ear vesicles, hearing loss and vertigo.
    • Secondary bacterial infection, scarring.
    • Dissemination, pneumonitis, hepatitis, encephalitis or stroke (vasculopathy), especially when immunocompromised.

    Prognosis

    • Most immunocompetent patients recover fully in 2–4 weeks.
    • PHN affects about 10–20%, more often over 60; it usually improves with time.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • Pain in a dermatome before any rash: consider zoster, but don't miss cardiac/surgical causes.

    • In a patient <50 with no obvious reason for zoster, offer an HIV test (Case 2 learning).

    • Hydrate on aciclovir; check eGFR (neurotoxicity/nephrotoxicity in renal impairment, elderly).

    • Explain postherpetic neuralgia (pain >3 months; commoner >50, HZO, severe acute pain) — early antiviral and good analgesia may reduce burden.

    • Vaccination: Shingrix (recombinant, non-live) is offered on the NHS programme to eligible older adults and to severely immunosuppressed adults (including people with HIV meeting criteria) — check current Green Book chapter 28a for eligibility. Zostavax (live) is contraindicated in immunosuppression.

    Self-check quiz

    5 questions · optional
    1. Q1Antiviral and dose for shingles in an immunocompetent adult?Reveal

      Aciclovir 800 mg five times daily for 7 days (or valaciclovir 1 g TDS / famciclovir 500 mg TDS for 7 days), ideally within 72 h.

    2. Q2What is Hutchinson’s sign?Reveal

      Vesicles on the tip or side of the nose (nasociliary nerve): high risk of eye involvement, same-day ophthalmology.

    3. Q3Ear vesicles with facial palsy?Reveal

      Ramsay Hunt syndrome: same-day ENT.

    4. Q4Zoster in a 32-year-old with no obvious cause?Reveal

      Offer an HIV test: zoster is an HIV indicator condition.

    5. Q5How long is a patient infectious?Reveal

      Until all lesions have crusted (usually 5–7 days).
