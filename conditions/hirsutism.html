    Hirsutism & the Ferriman–Gallwey score

    Module 1 · Week 2

    Male-pattern terminal hair growth in women: scoring with the modified Ferriman–Gallwey scale, causes (mostly PCOS or idiopathic), the tests that matter, and treatment from hair removal and the pill to specialist anti-androgens.

    Coversexcess hairmFG scorePCOSeflornithineVaniqaandrogen excessidiopathic hirsutism
    Think of it when

    A woman distressed by terminal hair on the face, chest, abdomen or back.

    What drives it

    Androgen excess (ovary, adrenal, drugs) or increased follicle sensitivity to normal androgens (idiopathic).

    Backbone of care

    Score and record impact, check testosterone and screen for PCOS; hair removal methods and a COC; eflornithine cream has been discontinued in the UK, so check availability; specialist anti-androgens with contraception.

    Never miss

    Rapid-onset virilisation (deep voice, clitoromegaly, testosterone >5 nmol/L): androgen-secreting tumour.

    What it is

    Hirsutism is excess terminal hair in a woman, in an androgen-dependent (male-pattern) distribution: upper lip, chin, jawline/sideburns, chest, abdomen, back, thighs. It differs from hypertrichosis, which is excess hair in a non-androgen pattern (e.g. drug-induced by ciclosporin, minoxidil or phenytoin, or paraneoplastic lanugo).

    2

    How it’s caused

    How it is caused

    The cause is increased androgen production (ovary, adrenal) and/or increased follicle sensitivity: 5α-reductase in the pilosebaceous unit converts testosterone to DHT.

    • PCOS (~70–80% of hirsutism); idiopathic hirsutism (normal androgens and ovulatory cycles, ~5–15%); idiopathic hyperandrogenaemia.
    • Less common causes: non-classic congenital adrenal hyperplasia (21-hydroxylase deficiency — check 17-OH-progesterone); Cushing's, acromegaly, hyperprolactinaemia, thyroid disease; androgen-secreting ovarian/adrenal tumours (rapid onset, virilisation, testosterone often >5 nmol/L); drugs (anabolic steroids, testosterone, danazol, valproate); menopause
    • Normal hair density varies widely with ethnicity and family. Mediterranean, Middle Eastern and South Asian women often have more terminal hair; East Asian women less.
    3

    Clinical features

    The case: dark terminal hair limited to the jawline and sideburns, FG ≈2 in that area, regular periods, distress, BMI 28, testosterone 1.2 nmol/L (<2), androstenedione 6.5 nmol/L (<10). No virilisation, acne or AN is recorded.

    4

    Diagnosis

    • Scoring: 9 androgen-sensitive areas — upper lip, chin, chest, upper abdomen, lower abdomen, upper arms, thighs, upper back, lower back. Each scores 0 (no terminal hair) to 4 (frankly virile), for a total of 0–36.
    • Cut-offs: ≥8 is the traditional threshold in white and black women; Lower cut-offs (≥4–6) are used in some populations, e.g. East Asian women. The 2018/2023 International PCOS guideline accepts ≥4–6 depending on ethnicity; Also recognise "patient-important hirsutism": localised hair that distresses the patient even with a low total, as here
    • Limitations: subjective, with inter-observer variation; underestimates hair after hair removal; no extra weighting for the face (the most distressing site); does not capture distress, so pair it with a QoL measure; Its main use is to monitor response
    • Work-up (UK — CKS hirsutism / Endocrine Society): History: onset and pace, cycles, fertility, weight change, drugs (including supplements and anabolic steroids), family history; Examination: virilisation (clitoromegaly, deep voice, male-pattern balding, increased muscle bulk), acne, AN, Cushingoid signs, BP, BMI; Bloods:; Total testosterone (early follicular, morning) and SHBG/free androgen index; As indicated: LH/FSH, prolactin, TSH, 17-OH-progesterone; If PCOS is likely: HbA1c/OGTT and lipids; Pregnancy test; Pelvic USS only if PCOS criteria are uncertain. It is not needed when 2 criteria are already met, and is not used within 8 years of menarche
    • Interpreting the case: Normal androgens, regular cycles and mild localised hair are most consistent with idiopathic (peripheral) hirsutism or a constitutional/ethnic hair pattern; PCOS is not excluded: regular cycles can still be anovulatory (check mid-luteal progesterone), and ~20–30% of women with PCOS have a normal total testosterone (check SHBG/FAI); Her BMI of 28 justifies an insulin-resistance work-up (HbA1c)

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    Therapeutic strategy for the case: treat the patient's goals, layer treatments, and set expectations — hormonal effects take 6–12 months, and a hair cycle is about 6 months.

    1. Lifestyle: weight reduction (even 5% improves SHBG, androgens and insulin resistance) and exercise; address any PCOS/metabolic risk.
    2. Cosmetic/physical methods (first-line; mostly self-funded in the UK): Shaving — it does not make hair thicker; Plucking, threading or waxing — risk of folliculitis and PIH; Depilatory creams (irritant) and bleaching; Laser/IPL — best for dark hair on light skin; Nd:YAG for darker skin; needs multiple sessions. NHS funding is very limited and ICB-specific, often only for severe cases or gender services; Electrolysis — permanent but slow; works on any hair colour
    3. Topical eflornithine 11.5% cream (Vaniqa): An irreversible ornithine-decarboxylase inhibitor that slows facial hair growth. Apply twice daily; effect by 6–8 weeks; stop if no benefit at 4 months. The effect is lost about 8 weeks after stopping. Works best combined with laser; 2026 status: NHS dm+d lists Vaniqa as discontinued/not available in the UK, and several ICBs (e.g. Pan Mersey, BSW) class it as not recommended. Check local availability before suggesting it
    4. Systemic hormonal therapy (if distress persists despite ≥6 months of cosmetic measures): A COC is the first-line systemic option (↓ ovarian androgen, ↑ SHBG); any low-VTE COC such as a levonorgestrel pill is a reasonable start; Co-cyprindiol is licensed for severe hirsutism but carries ~1.5–2× the VTE risk (MHRA 2013); stop 3–4 cycles after control; Anti-androgens (spironolactone 50–200 mg/day, higher-dose cyproterone acetate, finasteride) are off-label and specialist-advised, need reliable contraception (feminisation of a male fetus), and are usually added to a COC after 6 months of inadequate response; Higher-dose cyproterone (≥10 mg/day) carries a dose-related meningioma risk (MHRA 2020) — specialist only
    5. Psychological support — hirsutism has a major impact on self-.

    Red flags / refer

    • Rapid onset or progression, virilisation, testosterone >5 nmol/L (or >2× ULN), raised DHEAS, or a pelvic/abdominal mass → urgent endocrinology/gynaecology for a suspected androgen-secreting tumour (NICE NG12 if there is a mass).
    • Features of Cushing's, acromegaly or hyperprolactinaemia; suspected NC-CAH; new hirsutism after the menopause.
    • Distress not responding to primary-care measures → endocrinology/dermatology per the local pathway.

    IP scope

    Assessment, bloods, lifestyle and cosmetic advice, and COC prescribing within contraceptive competence and UKMEC are within IP scope. Anti-androgens, co-cyprindiol for hirsutism (formulary-dependent) and laser referral criteria follow local specialist pathways.

    7

    Red flags & referral

    • Rapid onset over months, virilisation or a very high testosterone: urgent endocrine referral to exclude an androgen-secreting tumour.
    • Anti-androgens (spironolactone, cyproterone, finasteride) are teratogenic to a male fetus: effective contraception is essential.
    8

    Prescriber pearls

    • "Hormonal treatment stops new hair coarsening; it doesn't remove existing hair" — so combine it with physical removal.
    • Shaving is safe and effective, and does not make regrowth thicker or darker.
    • Ask about drugs and supplements: testosterone gel transferred by contact with a partner, anabolic steroids, valproate; minoxidil causes hypertrichosis.
