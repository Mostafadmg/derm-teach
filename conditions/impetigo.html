    Impetigo

    Highly contagious superficial infection with honey-coloured crusts or flaccid blisters. Follow NICE NG153: hydrogen peroxide 1% first for localised disease, oral flucloxacillin for bullous or widespread disease.

    Also calledschool soreshoney crustbullous impetigonon-bullous impetigohydrogen peroxide creamfusidic acid+3
    Related pagesBoils & carbunclesFolliculitisEczema / dermatitisCellulitis & erysipelas

    Key facts

    • Superficial, contagious bacterial skin infection, mostly Staphylococcus aureus, sometimes Streptococcus pyogenes.
    • Most common in young children; spreads by direct contact.
    • Non-bullous: honey-coloured crusts around the nose and mouth; bullous: flaccid blisters from staphylococcal toxin.
    • UK (NICE NG153): localised and well → hydrogen peroxide 1% cream; topical antibiotic if unsuitable; oral flucloxacillin if widespread or unwell.
    • Stay off school or nursery until lesions crust over or 48 h after starting antibiotics.

    Overview

    Impetigo is a superficial bacterial infection of the epidermis. It is highly contagious and most common in children. It is caused by Staphylococcus aureus, sometimes with Streptococcus pyogenes.

    FormDescription
    Non-bullous (about 70%)Crusted, honey-coloured lesions; often around the face and on the limbs
    BullousThin-roofed, flaccid bullae that rupture to leave erosions with a collarette of scale; more often systemic symptoms; common in infants

    Epidemiology

    • Who
      • Most common skin infection in children aged 2–5 years, but any age can be affected.
      • More common in warm, humid weather and in crowded living conditions.
    • Spread
      • Highly contagious by direct contact, towels and toys; outbreaks in nurseries, schools and sports teams.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    • A break in the skin barrier lets bacteria in. Common causes are eczema, scabies, insect bites, minor trauma and herpes simplex.
    • Non-bullous: infection of the epidermis, producing an exudate that dries to a honey-coloured crust.
    • Bullous: staphylococcal exfoliative toxins cleave desmoglein-1 in the upper epidermis, producing flaccid blisters. The same toxins, spread systemically, cause staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome.
    • Spread is by direct contact, towels and fomites, and scratching (autoinoculation, ‘kissing lesions’).

    Risk factors and associations

    • Organisms: S. aureus (most cases in the UK) and S. pyogenes (group A streptococcus), alone or together.
    • Primary impetigo on intact skin, or secondary impetigo (impetiginisation) on damaged skin: eczema, scabies, insect bites, chickenpox, herpes simplex, trauma, burns.
    • Risk factors: nasal carriage of S. aureus, immunosuppression, diabetes, poor hygiene, crowding, contact sports.

    Myths to address

    • "Impetigo means poor hygiene": it is very common in clean, healthy children.
    • "Fusidic acid is first line": NICE now favours hydrogen peroxide 1% cream for localised disease to reduce resistance.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    Intact skin resists these bacteria; a break in the barrier lets them attach and multiply in the epidermis.

    1. 1

      Barrier breach and attachment

      Minor trauma, eczema or bites expose fibronectin receptors; staphylococcal and streptococcal adhesins (e.g. teichoic acids) bind and colonise the surface.

    2. 2

      Non-bullous impetigo

      Bacteria multiply under the stratum corneum, causing a neutrophilic pustule that ruptures; serum dries into golden crusts.

    3. 3

      Bullous impetigo

      S. aureus phage group II releases exfoliative toxins A and B, which cleave desmoglein 1 in the granular layer, so the upper epidermis lifts into flaccid blisters, even on intact skin.

    4. 4

      Local spread

      Scratching and contact carry bacteria to new sites (autoinoculation) and other people.

    Histopathology

    • Subcorneal pustule with neutrophils and Gram-positive cocci.
    • Bullous impetigo: cleavage in the granular layer with few inflammatory cells (the same level as staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome).
    • Diagnosis is clinical; swab only if recurrent, extensive, or not responding.

    Clinical features

    • Honey-coloured (golden) crusts on a red base, often around the nose and mouth, and on the hands and limbs.
    • Satellite lesions, and ‘kissing lesions’ where skin surfaces touch.
    • Bullous: intact or burst thin-walled blisters, shallow erosions and collarette scale. Often in the axillae, trunk or nappy area.
    • Usually mildly itchy or sore. Fever and lymphadenopathy are more likely with bullous or extensive disease.

    Forms to recognise

    • Non-bullous

      Golden ‘honey’ crusts. Site: Nose, mouth.

    • Skin of colour

      Crusted golden lesions; redness less visible. Site: Face.

    • Bullous

      Flaccid blister, collarette edge. Site: Trunk, axillae, nappy.

    Special groups

    • Skin of colour
      • Erythema is less visible; crusting and post-inflammatory hypo- or hyperpigmentation are prominent.
    • Newborns
      • Bullous impetigo in neonates needs prompt assessment (risk of spread and SSSS).

    Diagnosis and investigations

    • Clinical.
    • Swab if: recurrent, treatment has failed, MRSA risk, or widespread disease.
    • Look for and treat an underlying dermatosis (eczema, scabies, head lice).
    • Examine for eczema herpeticum: monomorphic punched-out erosions on eczematous skin, often with an unwell patient.

    Clinical

    • Diagnosis is clinical.

    Swab

    • Recurrent, failed treatment, MRSA risk, widespread, outbreak.

    Find the trigger

    • Eczema, scabies, head lice.

    Exclude HSV

    • Monomorphic punched-out erosions on eczema, unwell → eczema herpeticum.

    Differential diagnoses

    PhotoConditionDistinguishing features

    		Eczema flareItchy, ill-defined; crusts less golden.

    Management

    Aligned with NICE NG153 (antimicrobial prescribing).

    Everyone

    • Hygiene advice to reduce spread: wash the lesions gently, do not share towels, and avoid scratching.
    • School or nursery: keep the child off until lesions are crusted and healed, or for 48 hours after starting antibiotics, in line with UKHSA and local public health advice.

    Localised non-bullous, not unwell

    • Hydrogen peroxide 1% cream 2–3 times daily for 5 days.
    • If unsuitable or ineffective: a short course of topical antibiotic, usually fusidic acid for 5 days. Reserve mupirocin for MRSA in line with the local pathway.

    Widespread non-bullous, not unwell

    Topical or oral antibiotic for 5 days (shared decision). Repeated topical antibiotic use drives resistance.

    Bullous, systemically unwell, or high risk of complications

    • Oral antibiotic for 5 days (up to 7 if severe).
    • First line: flucloxacillin. Penicillin allergy: clarithromycin (erythromycin in pregnancy).

    MRSA and non-response (Module 5)

    • MRSA is becoming more common. Swab if impetigo is not responding, recurrent, part of an outbreak, or there are MRSA risk factors (recent hospital stay, care home, known carriage).
    • Choose the antibiotic using the swab sensitivities and local microbiology advice. Flucloxacillin and fusidic acid will not reliably treat MRSA.
    • Do not keep repeating fusidic acid courses. Resistance develops quickly.
    • Recurrent impetigo: consider nasal carriage and decolonisation for the patient and household, through the local pathway.

    Caution

    Don’t reach for a steroid–antibiotic combination by default

    Use a topical antibiotic plus steroid only when you have actually diagnosed infection on inflamed (e.g. eczematous) skin, and keep the course short.

    4. 4

    Caution

    Prescribing limits and pitfalls

    • Steroid–antibiotic combos: Only for proven infection on inflamed skin, briefly.
    • Safety-net: Worse after 2–3 days, spreading redness or fever → review.

    Referral

    • Same day

      Suspected eczema herpeticum.

    • Same day

      Infant with bullous impetigo, or suspected SSSS (tender widespread redness, peeling).

    • Urgent

      Cellulitis, deeper infection, or systemically unwell.

    • Routine

      Immunocompromised with widespread disease, or frequent recurrence (carriage, MRSA).

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Suspected eczema herpeticum: same-day referral.
    • Signs of cellulitis or deeper infection, or a systemically unwell patient.
    • Infants with bullous impetigo, or suspected staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome (tender, widespread erythema with peeling).
    • Immunocompromised with widespread disease, or frequent recurrence (consider nasal carriage and MRSA).

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Cellulitis, lymphangitis and bacteraemia.
    • Staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome (young children).
    • Ecthyma: deeper, punched-out ulcers that scar.
    • Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis (2–6 weeks later) and, rarely, scarlet fever or streptococcal toxic shock.
    • Post-inflammatory pigment change; scarring is uncommon.

    Prognosis

    • Usually self-limiting; without treatment it heals in about 2–3 weeks, and within about 10 days with treatment.
    • Recurrence suggests nasal carriage or an untreated underlying skin condition.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • Hydrogen peroxide 1% is first line for localised disease. It is a useful stewardship win.

    • Avoid repeated fusidic acid courses because of resistance. Use 5 days, then stop.

    • Crust does not always mean impetigo, so think about HSV first in eczema.

    • Treat the trigger (eczema, scabies, lice) or impetigo will keep coming back.

    • Safety-net: worsening after 2–3 days, spreading redness or fever means review.

    • Skin of colour: crusts and erythema can be subtler. Palpate, and rely on the history (spread to contacts, weeping, crusting).

    • New to skin terms? Describing skin lesions explains crust, erosion, bulla and vesicle.

    2. Q2Bullous or systemically unwell: what antibiotic?Reveal
