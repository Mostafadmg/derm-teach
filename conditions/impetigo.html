    Infections — bacterial

    Impetigo

    Adult core · deepened with Module 5 Week 1

    Highly contagious superficial infection with honey-coloured crusts or flaccid blisters. Follow NICE NG153: hydrogen peroxide 1% first for localised disease, oral flucloxacillin for bullous or widespread disease.

    Coversschool soreshoney crustbullous impetigonon-bullous impetigohydrogen peroxide creamfusidic acidNG153MRSA impetigomupirocin
    Think of it when

    Golden, honey-coloured crusts around the nose and mouth of a child, or thin-roofed blisters leaving collarette erosions.

    What drives it

    S. aureus (± S. pyogenes) in the epidermis. In bullous impetigo, exfoliative toxins split the epidermis at desmoglein-1.

    Backbone of care

    Hygiene, then hydrogen peroxide 1% cream (localised) or flucloxacillin (bullous or widespread).

    Never miss

    Eczema herpeticum mistaken for impetigo, and spreading cellulitis or an unwell infant.

    What it is

    Impetigo is a superficial bacterial infection of the epidermis. It is highly contagious and most common in children. It is caused by Staphylococcus aureus, sometimes with Streptococcus pyogenes.

    FormDescription
    Non-bullous (about 70%)Crusted, honey-coloured lesions; often around the face and on the limbs
    BullousThin-roofed, flaccid bullae that rupture to leave erosions with a collarette of scale; more often systemic symptoms; common in infants
    2

    How it’s caused

    • A break in the skin barrier lets bacteria in. Common causes are eczema, scabies, insect bites, minor trauma and herpes simplex.
    • Non-bullous: infection of the epidermis, producing an exudate that dries to a honey-coloured crust.
    • Bullous: staphylococcal exfoliative toxins cleave desmoglein-1 in the upper epidermis, producing flaccid blisters. The same toxins, spread systemically, cause staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome.
    • Spread is by direct contact, towels and fomites, and scratching (autoinoculation, ‘kissing lesions’).
    3

    Clinical features

    • Honey-coloured (golden) crusts on a red base, often around the nose and mouth, and on the hands and limbs.
    • Satellite lesions, and ‘kissing lesions’ where skin surfaces touch.
    • Bullous: intact or burst thin-walled blisters, shallow erosions and collarette scale. Often in the axillae, trunk or nappy area.
    • Usually mildly itchy or sore. Fever and lymphadenopathy are more likely with bullous or extensive disease.
    4

    Diagnosis

    • Clinical.
    • Swab if: recurrent, treatment has failed, MRSA risk, or widespread disease.
    • Look for and treat an underlying dermatosis (eczema, scabies, head lice).
    • Examine for eczema herpeticum: monomorphic punched-out erosions on eczematous skin, often with an unwell patient.
    5

    Differentials

    Look-alikeDistinguishing clues
    Herpes simplex / eczema herpeticumGrouped vesicles then punched-out erosions; painful; may be unwell. Urgent if on eczema
    Eczema flareItchy, ill-defined, with background eczema; crusts are less golden
    Pemphigus foliaceus (rare)Adult, recurrent superficial erosions; does not respond to antibiotics
    Insect bite reaction / bullous bitesGrouped bites on exposed sites; tense blisters

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    Aligned with NICE NG153 (antimicrobial prescribing).

    Everyone

    • Hygiene advice to reduce spread: wash the lesions gently, do not share towels, and avoid scratching.
    • School or nursery: keep the child off until lesions are crusted and healed, or for 48 hours after starting antibiotics, in line with UKHSA and local public health advice.

    Localised non-bullous, not unwell

    • Hydrogen peroxide 1% cream 2–3 times daily for 5 days.
    • If unsuitable or ineffective: a short course of topical antibiotic, usually fusidic acid for 5 days. Reserve mupirocin for MRSA in line with the local pathway.

    Widespread non-bullous, not unwell

    Topical or oral antibiotic for 5 days (shared decision). Repeated topical antibiotic use drives resistance.

    Bullous, systemically unwell, or high risk of complications

    • Oral antibiotic for 5 days (up to 7 if severe).
    • First line: flucloxacillin. Penicillin allergy: clarithromycin (erythromycin in pregnancy).

    MRSA and non-response (Module 5)

    • MRSA is becoming more common. Swab if impetigo is not responding, recurrent, part of an outbreak, or there are MRSA risk factors (recent hospital stay, care home, known carriage).
    • Choose the antibiotic using the swab sensitivities and local microbiology advice. Flucloxacillin and fusidic acid will not reliably treat MRSA.
    • Do not keep repeating fusidic acid courses. Resistance develops quickly.
    • Recurrent impetigo: consider nasal carriage and decolonisation for the patient and household, through the local pathway.

    Don’t reach for a steroid–antibiotic combination by default

    Use a topical antibiotic plus steroid only when you have actually diagnosed infection on inflamed (e.g. eczematous) skin, and keep the course short.

    7

    Red flags & referral

    • Suspected eczema herpeticum: same-day referral.
    • Signs of cellulitis or deeper infection, or a systemically unwell patient.
    • Infants with bullous impetigo, or suspected staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome (tender, widespread erythema with peeling).
    • Immunocompromised with widespread disease, or frequent recurrence (consider nasal carriage and MRSA).
    8

    Prescriber pearls

    • Hydrogen peroxide 1% is first line for localised disease. It is a useful stewardship win.
    • Avoid repeated fusidic acid courses because of resistance. Use 5 days, then stop.
    • Crust does not always mean impetigo, so think about HSV first in eczema.
    • Treat the trigger (eczema, scabies, lice) or impetigo will keep coming back.
    • Safety-net: worsening after 2–3 days, spreading redness or fever means review.
    • Skin of colour: crusts and erythema can be subtler. Palpate, and rely on the history (spread to contacts, weeping, crusting).
    • New to skin terms? Describing skin lesions explains crust, erosion, bulla and vesicle.
