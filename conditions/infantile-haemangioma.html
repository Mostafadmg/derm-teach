    Infantile haemangioma (strawberry mark)

    A benign vascular tumour that appears in the first weeks, grows fast for a few months, then slowly involutes. Refer high-risk sites early (ideally by about 4 weeks), while propranolol works best.

    Also calledinfantile haemangiomastrawberry naevusstrawberry markhaemangiomaPHACEpropranolol+1
    Related pagesPort-wine stain (capillary malformation)Pyogenic granulomaPigmented & epidermal birthmarksNeonatal pustular rashes

    Key facts

    • Infantile haemangioma is the commonest tumour of infancy (about 4–10% of infants): absent or faint at birth, then rapid growth.
    • Most growth happens in the first 1–3 months; involution over years, often leaving fibrofatty tissue or telangiectasia.
    • High-risk sites: periocular, airway/beard, lip, nasal tip, large segmental facial, perineal, ulcerated, or 5 or more lesions.
    • Refer high-risk lesions early, ideally by about 4 weeks of age; propranolol is first-line and specialist-initiated.
    • Five or more skin haemangiomas: liver ultrasound. Large facial segmental lesions: think PHACE.

    Overview

    An infantile haemangioma (IH) is a benign vascular tumour of infancy. It is absent or a faint mark at birth, grows rapidly (mostly in the first 1–3 months, largely complete by about 5 months), plateaus, then involutes over years. About 4–10% of infants are affected.

    Epidemiology

    • Who
      • More in girls, premature and low-birth-weight babies, and twins.
      • Head and neck in about 60%.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    • Clonal endothelial proliferation, GLUT-1 positive (unlike malformations and congenital haemangiomas).
    • Risk factors: prematurity, low birth weight, female sex, multiple pregnancy, placental anomalies.

    Myths to address

    • "Wait and it will go away": most do, but high-risk ones need treatment in the early growth window.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    A haemangioma is a benign proliferation of immature endothelial cells that later switches off.

    1. 1

      Trigger

      Tissue hypoxia (possibly placental) induces HIF-1α and VEGF in a progenitor cell population.

    2. 2

      Proliferation

      GLUT-1–positive endothelial cells proliferate rapidly in the first months.

    3. 3

      Plateau

      Growth slows by about 5 months.

    4. 4

      Involution

      Endothelial cells undergo apoptosis and are replaced by fibrofatty tissue over years.

    5. 5

      Propranolol effect

      Beta-blockade causes vasoconstriction, reduces VEGF signalling and speeds involution.

    Histopathology

    • Lobules of capillaries lined by GLUT-1–positive endothelium (GLUT-1 negative in congenital haemangiomas and malformations).

    Clinical features

    TypeAppearance
    SuperficialBright red, raised, well-defined (‘strawberry’)
    DeepBluish, rubbery swelling under the skin
    MixedBoth

    Haemangiomas can occur anywhere, most often on the head and neck. 5 or more skin haemangiomas raise concern for liver haemangiomas. White surface change can herald ulceration.

    Special groups

    • PHACE syndrome
      • Posterior fossa anomalies, Haemangioma (large facial segmental), Arterial, Cardiac (coarctation) and Eye anomalies: needs MRI/MRA, echo and eye review.
    • LUMBAR syndrome
      • Lumbosacral segmental haemangioma with spinal and urogenital anomalies: spinal imaging.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    • Usually clinical.
    • Ultrasound if deep, or uncertain against a malformation or soft-tissue mass. MRI for complex facial or segmental lesions.
    • Abdominal ultrasound if there are 5 or more skin lesions.

    Differential diagnoses

    Look-alikeDistinguishing clues
    Port-wine stain (see Port-wine stain)Present at birth, flat, grows with the child, no rapid tumour growth
    Congenital haemangiomaFully formed at birth; different natural history
    Venous or lymphatic malformationSoft, compressible, persistent; imaging differs
    Pyogenic granulomaLater onset; often a pedunculated bleeding papule

    Management

    Uncomplicated small haemangioma

    Active observation with photo documentation. Reassure parents about involution.

    Specialist treatment

    • Oral propranolol is first line for problematic IH. It is started and supervised by a specialist, with heart rate, BP and glucose monitoring. Feed regularly, and hold doses if the baby is unwell or not feeding.
    • Topical timolol for selected small, thin, superficial lesions (off-label; NICE ESUOM47).
    • Wound care, analgesia and antibiotics for ulceration; laser or surgery for selected residual changes.

    Treatment ladder

    1. 1

      Uncomplicated

      Active observation with photographs; reassure.

    2. 2

      Small superficial (specialist)

      Topical timolol (off-label; NICE ESUOM47).

    3. 3

      Problematic / high-risk

      Oral propranolol, specialist-initiated with monitoring; hold doses if not feeding or unwell.

    Referral

    • Urgent

      Periocular, airway/beard, lip, nasal tip, segmental facial, perineal, ulcerated or ≥5 lesions: refer ideally by about 4 weeks of age.

    • Same day

      Stridor with a beard-area haemangioma.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • High-risk haemangioma sites (listed above): do not wait to see if it goes away.
    • Stridor or noisy breathing with a beard-area haemangioma: urgent.
    • Ulcerated or bleeding haemangioma.

    Refer promptly (ideally by about 4 weeks of age if high risk)

    • Periocular (risk to vision and amblyopia)
    • Beard or airway distribution (risk of stridor)
    • Lip or perioral (feeding)
    • Nasal tip, large facial, or segmental (PHACE risk with large facial segmental lesions)
    • Perineal or flexural (ulceration)
    • Ulcerated, bleeding or rapidly enlarging
    • 5 or more skin lesions
    • Breast (girls) or large scalp lesions

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Ulceration (painful, scarring), bleeding, amblyopia, airway obstruction, feeding problems, disfigurement; heart failure with large liver haemangiomas.

    Prognosis

    • Most involute; residual skin changes are common in large or deep lesions.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • The rapid growth window is early, so late referral misses the easiest window for treatment.

    • IP role: recognise high-risk sites, explain that beta-blockers are specialist-supervised, and support dressings for ulceration under the specialist plan.

    • BAD patient leaflet and the Birthmark Support Group are useful for families.

    Self-check quiz

    4 questions · optional
    1. Q1When does most haemangioma growth occur?Reveal

      In the first 1–3 months; largely complete by about 5 months.

    2. Q2Which marker distinguishes IH from malformations?Reveal

      GLUT-1.

    3. Q3What does ≥5 skin haemangiomas prompt?Reveal

      Abdominal (liver) ultrasound.

    4. Q4First-line systemic treatment?Reveal

      Oral propranolol, started by a specialist.
