    Intertrigo & the flexural differential

    Inflammation of opposed skin folds from friction, heat and moisture. It is a description, not a diagnosis: sort out candida, tinea, erythrasma, flexural psoriasis and seborrhoeic dermatitis before reaching for combination creams.

    Also calledintertrigoskin fold rashinframammary rashgroin rashmacerationerythrasma+2
    Related pagesCandidal intertrigoTinea: corporis, cruris & pedisPsoriasisNapkin (nappy) dermatitisPerineal dermatoses

    Key facts

    • Intertrigo is inflammation of skin folds from friction, heat and moisture; it is a pattern, not one disease.
    • Breasts, groin, axillae, abdominal folds, between toes; obesity, diabetes, incontinence and hot weather predispose.
    • Look for secondary Candida (satellite papules), dermatophyte (active edge), erythrasma (coral-red Wood's light) or streptococcus.
    • Keep folds dry, reduce friction, barrier paste; hydrocortisone 1% for 1–2 weeks; topical azole if candida likely.
    • Persistent flexural rashes: think inverse psoriasis, seborrhoeic dermatitis, contact allergy, Hailey–Hailey or extramammary Paget's.

    Overview

    Superficial inflammation of opposed skin surfaces (flexures/folds) driven by heat, moisture, friction, and poor evaporation. May be purely irritant or complicated by yeast/bacteria; often overlaps inflammatory dermatoses (psoriasis, seborrhoeic dermatitis, contact allergy).

    Epidemiology

    • Who
      • Any age; common in obesity, diabetes, immobility, incontinence and infants (napkin and neck folds).

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    Occluded flexures trap sweat → maceration → barrier failure → inflammation ± overgrowth of resident flora (Candida, Corynebacteria, dermatophytes, Staph/Strep). Risk: obesity, diabetes, hyperhidrosis, immobility, incontinence, tight synthetics, hot/humid environments. Infant napkin dermatitis is a related fold problem.

    Risk factors and associations

    • Skin-on-skin friction, trapped sweat, maceration, and alkaline irritants (urine, faeces).

    Myths to address

    • "Talc keeps folds dry": it can clump and irritate; dry gently and use a barrier.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    Folds trap heat and moisture, so the barrier macerates and rubs raw.

    1. 1

      Occlusion

      Skin-to-skin contact traps sweat and raises temperature.

    2. 2

      Maceration and friction

      Wet stratum corneum softens and erodes with movement.

    3. 3

      Secondary infection

      Damaged skin is colonised by Candida, dermatophytes, Corynebacterium (erythrasma) or streptococci.

    4. 4

      Typical sites

      Submammary, axillary, inguinal, abdominal and interdigital folds.

    Clinical features

    • Erythema, itch/burn, moist/macerated skin, fissures in crease depth, peeling.
    • Sites: inframammary, axillae, groin, abdominal apron, natal cleft, neck, finger/toe webs.
    • Worked case: 50-year-old; 3-month itchy red symmetrical inframammary rash; smooth surface, well-defined borders, mild peripheral scale; KOH negative; on HRT; no other sites or new topicals → diagnosis intertrigo (inflammatory/irritant-predominant).

    Secondary infection clues: satellite pustules (candida), foul odour (Gram-negatives), honey crusts (impetigo), coral-red Wood lamp (erythrasma).

    Fold rashes to tell apart

    • Candida

      Beefy red with satellite papules / pustules. Site: Folds.

    • Tinea cruris

      Raised active border, central clearing; often asymmetrical. Site: Groin.

    • Erythrasma

      Red-brown patch, little scale or itch. Site: Axilla, groin.

    • Erythrasma, Wood’s lamp

      Coral-red fluorescence. Site: Wood’s lamp.

    • Seborrhoeic

      Ill-defined salmon-pink; check scalp and face. Site: Axilla.

    • Hailey–Hailey

      Painful erosions, fissures; familial: refer. Site: Axilla, groin.

    Special groups

    • Skin of colour
      • Erythema may look brown or purple; post-inflammatory pigmentation is common.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    Clinical. KOH/mycology if scale, satellites, or treatment failure; bacterial swab if pustules/crust/odour; consider patch testing if allergen suspected; biopsy if atypical or refractory (granular parakeratosis, Hailey–Hailey, etc.).

    Clinical

    • Most are clinical.

    KOH / mycology

    • If scale, satellites or treatment failure.

    Swab / Wood’s lamp

    • Swab if pustules, crust, odour; Wood’s lamp for erythrasma.

    Biopsy / patch test

    • Atypical, one fold that won’t clear, or suspected allergy.

    A KOH-negative picture still overlaps early inverse psoriasis and seborrhoeic dermatitis — reassess if not settling with dry-care + mild steroid.

    Flexural differential table

    Key takeaway: when treating flexural rashes, keep dry, address incontinence/sweating/obesity, and screen secondary bacterial/fungal infection.

    Management

    Cornerstone — keep dry and reduce friction:

    • Shower after sweating; dry folds thoroughly (cool hairdryer useful under breasts/axillae).
    • Loose natural-fibre clothing; address incontinence and obesity where relevant.
    • Barrier pastes (e.g. zinc oxide) for weepy irritant folds; avoid irritating talc as primary strategy.
    • Mild topical corticosteroid short course (e.g. hydrocortisone 1% 1–2 weeks) for inflammatory intertrigo once infection unlikely/treated.
    • If candida likely or empiric after failed dry-care: topical azole (± mild steroid combo products used cautiously in primary care).
    • Bacterial secondary infection: topical or oral antibiotic per local guidance.
    • Avoid potent/very potent steroids in folds (atrophy, striae, rebound).

    Further management: follow-up response; if persists → repeat mycology/culture; reconsider inverse psoriasis, contact allergy, seborrhoeic dermatitis; review HRT/medications only if clinically relevant contributors.

    Treatment ladder

    1. 1

      Keep dry, reduce friction

      Shower after sweating; dry folds thoroughly (cool hairdryer). Loose natural fibres; address incontinence and weight.

    2. 2

      Mild steroid

      Hydrocortisone 1%, 1–2 weeks, once infection unlikely or treated.

    3. 3

      Treat the organism

      Candida likely: topical azole (± mild steroid combo, used cautiously). Bacterial: topical or oral antibiotic per local guidance.

    4. 4

      Not settling

      Repeat mycology / culture; rethink inverse psoriasis, contact allergy, seborrhoeic dermatitis.

    Caution

    Prescribing limits and pitfalls

    • Potent steroids in folds: Atrophy, striae and rebound: avoid.
    • Combo creams: Pragmatic, but reassess if no infection signs and no improvement.

    Referral

    • Same day

      Spreading redness, pain and fever: cellulitis.

    • Urgent

      Single fold that won’t clear, especially older adult: biopsy for extramammary Paget.

    • Routine

      Relapses as soon as treatment stops: think inverse psoriasis.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Rash that fails to clear in one fold, especially in an older adult: biopsy for extramammary Paget disease.
    • Spreading redness, pain and fever: cellulitis.
    • Repeated ‘intertrigo’ that relapses as soon as treatment stops: think inverse psoriasis (see the psoriasis page).

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Secondary infection, fissuring, odour, pain; cellulitis in diabetes.

    Prognosis

    • Recurs unless predisposing factors are managed.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • KOH negative ≠ “never fungal” — sampling error and mixed pictures exist; re-test if course atypical.

    • Combination clotrimazole–HC / similar: pragmatic in primary care but reassess if no clear infection signs and no improvement.

    • Counsel that prevention (dryness) beats endless cream cycles.

    Self-check quiz

    4 questions · optional
    1. Q1Coral-red fluorescence under Wood’s lamp?Reveal

      Erythrasma (Corynebacterium).

    2. Q2First step for KOH-negative irritant intertrigo?Reveal

      Dry-care and friction reduction, plus a short course of hydrocortisone 1%.

    3. Q3Why avoid potent steroids in folds?Reveal

      Natural occlusion increases absorption: atrophy, striae, rebound.

    4. Q4One fold that never clears in an older woman?Reveal

      Biopsy to exclude extramammary Paget disease.
