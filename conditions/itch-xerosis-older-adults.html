    Itch & xerosis in older adults

    Asteatotic eczema, venous and discoid eczema, lichen simplex and prurigo — with scabies and bullous pemphigoid as don’t-miss itch differentials.

    Also calledpruritusxerosisasteatotic eczemaeczema craqueléwinter itchvenous eczema+2
    Related pagesEczema / dermatitisEmollients & soap substitutesSkin fragility & dermatoporosisBullous pemphigoid (incl. MMP & pemphigoid gestationis)Scabies

    Key facts

    • Xerosis (dry skin) is the commonest cause of itch in older adults; asteatotic eczema (eczema craquelé) is its inflamed form, especially on the shins.
    • Look for a cause of itch without rash: iron deficiency, renal or liver disease, thyroid disease, haematological malignancy, drugs.
    • Cornerstone: emollients liberally and often (2–3 times a day and after washing), soap substitutes, short lukewarm showers.
    • Short course of a moderately potent steroid for asteatotic flares; warn about fire risk of emollient-soaked fabric and slippery floors.
    • Do not forget scabies, bullous pemphigoid (prodrome) and drug reactions in itchy older people.

    Overview

    Xerosis (dry skin) is the commonest skin problem and the commonest cause of itch in older adults. Asteatotic eczema (eczema craquelé) is the inflammatory end — polygonal “crazy-paving” red fissures, typically on the shins.

    This page also covers related elderly itch patterns: venous (stasis) eczema, discoid eczema, lichen simplex / nodular prurigo, and the red-flag differentials of scabies and pre-bullous pemphigoid.

    Epidemiology

    • Who
      • Most people over 65 have some xerosis; worse in winter, with central heating and frequent washing.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    • Ageing epidermis: fewer lipids and NMF, slower repair, higher water loss; less sebum/sweat.
    • Environment: low humidity, central heating, hot showers, soaps and antiseptic washes.
    • Drugs: diuretics, statins, retinoids, some antiandrogens, chemotherapy/EGFR inhibitors.
    • Systemic: hypothyroidism, CKD, diabetes, malnutrition, HIV, malignancy (acquired ichthyosis).

    Risk factors and associations

    • Ageing skin: less lipid and filaggrin, slower barrier repair, reduced sweat and sebum.
    • Diuretics, statins and other drugs; hypothyroidism; low humidity.

    Myths to address

    • "Itching in older people is just age": always examine for scabies, pemphigoid and systemic causes.
    • "Antihistamines help dry-skin itch": sedating ones cause falls and confusion and do little for xerotic itch.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    Ageing skin loses its lipid barrier, lets water escape and becomes itchy and cracked.

    1. 1

      Barrier lipid loss

      Ceramides, cholesterol and natural moisturising factor fall with age.

    2. 2

      Water loss and scaling

      Transepidermal water loss rises; corneocytes shrink and scale.

    3. 3

      Cracking

      Fine fissures form a 'crazy-paving' pattern on the shins (eczema craquelé).

    4. 4

      Itch–scratch cycle

      Nerve fibres in dry skin become sensitised; scratching damages the barrier further.

    5. 5

      Repair

      Regular emollient restores lipids and breaks the cycle.

    Clinical features

    • Dull rough scale, tight feel, nocturnal itch; winter flares.
    • Asteatotic eczema: shallow red fissures in a cracked-porcelain network on shins (± thighs, arms).
    • Venous eczema: medial gaiter area with haemosiderin, oedema, varicose veins — see also venous leg ulcer / red leg.
    • Discoid eczema: coin-shaped weeping plaques.
    • Lichen simplex / prurigo: thick scratched plaques or nodules at reachable sites.

    Prescriber tip

    Skin of colour

    Erythema may look violaceous or grey-brown; judge on scale, thickening and symptoms.

    Patterns to recognise

    • Eczema craquelé

      Shallow red fissures between plates. Site: Shins.

    • Winter itch

      Excoriations, little primary rash. Site: Back, limbs.

    • Venous eczema

      Haemosiderin, oedema, varicose veins. Site: Gaiter area.

    • Discoid eczema

      Coin-shaped itchy plaques. Site: Limbs.

    • Nodular prurigo

      Excoriated firm nodules at reachable sites. Site: Limbs, back.

    • Crusted scabies

      Thick crusts; care-home outbreaks. Site: Hands, body.

    Special groups

    • Itch without rash (generalised pruritus)
      • Check FBC, ferritin, U&E, LFT, TFT, HbA1c; consider chest X-ray if there are systemic symptoms.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    • Clinical: season, bathing habits, products, drugs, care-home contacts.
    • Itch >> rash, new generalised dryness, or resistance → systemic screen and “don’t miss” list below.
    • Swab only if infected; patch test if products seem to worsen it.

    History

    • Season, bathing, products, drugs, care-home contacts.

    Management

    Self-care (cornerstone)

    • Emollients liberally and often (≥2–3× daily and after washing). Greasier ointments for legs; urea 5–10% for scale.
    • Soap substitutes; short lukewarm showers; pat dry; no bubble bath.
    • Humidify rooms; cotton clothes.
    • Fire risk: paraffin (and other) emollients on fabrics — no smoking/naked flames; wash clothing often (MHRA).
    • Falls risk: slippery bathroom floors — non-slip mats; apply after stepping out.

    Prescribing

    • Asteatotic flare: short moderately potent topical steroid (e.g. clobetasone butyrate) 1–2 weeks plus ongoing emollient; potent short-term for thick leg plaques.
    • Infected eczema: treat per NICE guidance — avoid routine topical antibiotics.
    • Drug review with the GP (diuretic/statin) — do not stop cardiovascular drugs unilaterally.
    • Venous eczema: emollient + steroid + compression after ABPI assessment pathways.
    • Lichen simplex/prurigo: potent steroid ± occlusion; address itch driver; refer if refractory.

    Treatment ladder

    1. 1

      Self-care

      Emollients liberally and often (≥ 2–3 times daily, after washing); greasier on legs; urea 5–10% for scale. Soap substitutes; short lukewarm showers; pat dry; humidify; cotton clothes.

    2. 2

      Flare

      Asteatotic flare: short moderately potent steroid (e.g. clobetasone butyrate) 1–2 weeks + emollient. Thick leg plaques: potent, short term.

    3. 3

      Other patterns

      Venous eczema: emollient + steroid + compression after ABPI. Lichen simplex / prurigo: potent steroid ± occlusion; refer if refractory.

    4. 4

      Drug review

      Review diuretics / statins with the GP: don’t stop cardiovascular drugs unilaterally.

    Caution

    Prescribing limits and pitfalls

    • Fire risk: Emollients on fabrics: no smoking or naked flames; wash clothing often (MHRA).
    • Falls: Slippery floors: non-slip mats; apply after stepping out.

    Referral

    • Urgent

      Intense itch with urticated plaques: consider pemphigoid; refer.

    • Urgent

      Generalised itch with weight loss, night sweats or nodes: systemic work-up.

    • Same day

      Suspected scabies outbreak in a care home.

    • Routine

      Non-healing ‘eczema’: patch test or rethink the diagnosis.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Generalised itch with little rash, weight loss, night sweats or lymphadenopathy → systemic work-up.
    • Suspected scabies outbreak in a care home.
    • New intense itch with urticated plaques in an older adult → consider pemphigoid; refer.
    • Non-healing “eczema” → patch testing or alternative diagnosis.

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Infected eczema; skin tears and dermatoporosis.
    • Sleep disturbance, depression.
    • Falls from slippery emollients; burns from emollient-soaked fabrics.

    Prognosis

    • Chronic and seasonal; controlled with daily skin care.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • “Steroid settles it but it always comes back” usually means no maintenance emollient.

    • Help older people reach their legs — carers, pumps, long-handled applicators.

    • Teach fingertip units; steroid treats the flare, emollient prevents the next one.

    Self-check quiz

    4 questions · optional
    1. Q1Commonest cause of itch in older adults?Reveal

      Xerosis (dry skin).

    2. Q2Two drug classes that dry the skin?Reveal

      Diuretics and statins (also retinoids, antiandrogens, EGFR inhibitors).

    3. Q3Itch with urticated plaques in an 80-year-old?Reveal

      Consider pre-bullous pemphigoid: refer.

    4. Q4Steroid for an asteatotic flare?Reveal

      Short moderately potent course (e.g. clobetasone butyrate) 1–2 weeks with ongoing emollient.
