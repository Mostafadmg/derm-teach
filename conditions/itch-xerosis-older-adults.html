    Ageing skin & older adults

    Itch & xerosis in older adults

    Asteatotic eczema, venous and discoid eczema, lichen simplex and prurigo — with scabies and bullous pemphigoid as don’t-miss itch differentials.

    Module 6 · Week 4 · 17 images

    Also coverspruritusxerosisasteatotic eczemaeczema craqueléwinter itchvenous eczemalichen simplexnodular prurigo
    Think of it when

    Dry, cracked “crazy-paving” shins or generalised itch in an older person, often worse in winter after hot baths and soaps.

    What drives it

    Age-related barrier lipid loss plus environment, drugs and comorbidities drive xerosis → fissures → itch–scratch.

    Backbone of care

    Liberal greasy emollients and soap substitutes every day; short moderately potent steroid for asteatotic flares; review diuretics/statins with the GP.

    Never miss

    Itch out of proportion to rash — think scabies, bullous pemphigoid, and systemic disease (CKD, cholestasis, lymphoma).

    01

    What it is

    Xerosis (dry skin) is the commonest skin problem and the commonest cause of itch in older adults. Asteatotic eczema (eczema craquelé) is the inflammatory end — polygonal “crazy-paving” red fissures, typically on the shins.

    This page also covers related elderly itch patterns: venous (stasis) eczema, discoid eczema, lichen simplex / nodular prurigo, and the red-flag differentials of scabies and pre-bullous pemphigoid.

    02

    How it’s caused

    • Ageing epidermis: fewer lipids and NMF, slower repair, higher water loss; less sebum/sweat.
    • Environment: low humidity, central heating, hot showers, soaps and antiseptic washes.
    • Drugs: diuretics, statins, retinoids, some antiandrogens, chemotherapy/EGFR inhibitors.
    • Systemic: hypothyroidism, CKD, diabetes, malnutrition, HIV, malignancy (acquired ichthyosis).
    03

    Clinical features

    • Dull rough scale, tight feel, nocturnal itch; winter flares.
    • Asteatotic eczema: shallow red fissures in a cracked-porcelain network on shins (± thighs, arms).
    • Venous eczema: medial gaiter area with haemosiderin, oedema, varicose veins — see also venous leg ulcer / red leg.
    • Discoid eczema: coin-shaped weeping plaques.
    • Lichen simplex / prurigo: thick scratched plaques or nodules at reachable sites.

    Prescriber tip

    Skin of colour

    Erythema may look violaceous or grey-brown; judge on scale, thickening and symptoms.

    04

    Diagnosis

    • Clinical: season, bathing habits, products, drugs, care-home contacts.
    • Itch >> rash, new generalised dryness, or resistance → systemic screen and “don’t miss” list below.
    • Swab only if infected; patch test if products seem to worsen it.
    05

    Differentials

    Look-alikeClues
    ScabiesBurrows, nocturnal itch, itchy contacts/care home
    Bullous pemphigoid (pre-bullous)Urticated plaques, intense itch, older adult → refer
    Venous / discoid eczemaGaiter staining or coin plaques
    Contact dermatitisFollows medicaments/emollient ingredients
    TineaAnnular active edge; scrapings
    Systemic itchLittle rash; weight loss, night sweats, nodes
    06

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    Pharmacist / IP

    Self-care (cornerstone)

    • Emollients liberally and often (≥2–3× daily and after washing). Greasier ointments for legs; urea 5–10% for scale.
    • Soap substitutes; short lukewarm showers; pat dry; no bubble bath.
    • Humidify rooms; cotton clothes.
    • Fire risk: paraffin (and other) emollients on fabrics — no smoking/naked flames; wash clothing often (MHRA).
    • Falls risk: slippery bathroom floors — non-slip mats; apply after stepping out.

    Prescribing

    • Asteatotic flare: short moderately potent topical steroid (e.g. clobetasone butyrate) 1–2 weeks plus ongoing emollient; potent short-term for thick leg plaques.
    • Infected eczema: treat per NICE guidance — avoid routine topical antibiotics.
    • Drug review with the GP (diuretic/statin) — do not stop cardiovascular drugs unilaterally.
    • Venous eczema: emollient + steroid + compression after ABPI assessment pathways.
    • Lichen simplex/prurigo: potent steroid ± occlusion; address itch driver; refer if refractory.
    07

    Red flags & referral

    Escalate
    • Generalised itch with little rash, weight loss, night sweats or lymphadenopathy → systemic work-up.
    • Suspected scabies outbreak in a care home.
    • New intense itch with urticated plaques in an older adult → consider pemphigoid; refer.
    • Non-healing “eczema” → patch testing or alternative diagnosis.
    08

    Prescriber pearls

    • “Steroid settles it but it always comes back” usually means no maintenance emollient.
    • Help older people reach their legs — carers, pumps, long-handled applicators.
    • Teach fingertip units; steroid treats the flare, emollient prevents the next one.
