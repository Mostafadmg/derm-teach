    Leprosy (Hansen disease) & lepra reactions

    A chronic mycobacterial infection of skin and nerves: pale or red patches with reduced sensation, thickened nerves and, in multibacillary disease, nodules and facial infiltration. Curable with WHO multidrug therapy; reactions are emergencies for the nerves.

    Also calledHansen diseaseleprosyMycobacterium lepraenumb patchlepra reactionerythema nodosum leprosum+2
    Related pagesCutaneous tuberculosis (scrofuloderma & others)The returning traveller with a skin problemHypopigmentation & skin of colourCutaneous leishmaniasis

    Key facts

    • Chronic infection of skin and peripheral nerves by Mycobacterium leprae; curable with multidrug therapy, but nerve damage is permanent.
    • WHO: about 200,000 new cases a year (India, Brazil, Indonesia); UK about 10–15 cases a year, all acquired abroad.
    • Cardinal signs: hypopigmented or red patch with loss of sensation, thickened peripheral nerves, acid-fast bacilli on smear.
    • Spectrum from tuberculoid (few lesions, strong immunity) to lepromatous (many lesions, weak immunity).
    • Refer every suspected case to a national leprosy centre; reactions (new nerve pain, weakness, eye symptoms) are urgent. Notifiable.

    Overview

    Leprosy is a chronic granulomatous infection of the skin and peripheral nerves (also the nasal mucosa, eyes and testes). It is caused by Mycobacterium leprae (and M. lepromatosis). It is curable with multidrug therapy (MDT), but nerve damage already present is permanent, so early diagnosis prevents disability. WHO registers about 200,000 new cases a year, mostly in India, Brazil and Indonesia, with others in Nigeria, Bangladesh, Nepal, Ethiopia, the DR Congo, Myanmar and the Pacific.

    UK: around 10–15 notifications a year, all acquired abroad, mostly from the South-East Asia, Americas and Africa regions. Recent UKHSA data show 255 cases from 2000–2025 and no UK transmission. It is statutorily notifiable in England.

    Classification:

    • WHO (operational): paucibacillary (PB) = 1–5 skin lesions and a negative smear. Multibacillary (MB) = more than 5 lesions, or nerve involvement (pure neuritis or any lesions with neuritis), or a positive slit-skin smear.
    • Ridley–Jopling spectrum (immunological): TT – BT – BB – BL – LL, plus indeterminate and pure neural forms.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    • M. leprae is an obligate intracellular acid-fast bacillus. It divides extremely slowly (about every 12–14 days) and prefers cooler body sites (skin, superficial nerves, nose, earlobes, testes). Incubation averages around 5 years (range from months to over 20 years).
    • Transmission: mainly respiratory droplets from the nasal secretions of untreated MB patients, after prolonged close (household) contact. Most exposed people (around 95%) are naturally resistant. Armadillos are a zoonotic source in the southern USA and Brazil. Patients become non-infectious within days of starting rifampicin-containing MDT.
    • Immune spectrum: Tuberculoid pole (TT/BT): strong Th1 cell-mediated immunity, few bacilli, few well-defined lesions, early and severe nerve damage near the lesions; Lepromatous pole (LL/BL): weak cell-mediated immunity, a Th2/antibody response, huge numbers of bacilli, widespread symmetrical infiltration, and slower but extensive nerve involvement (glove and stocking)
    • Nerve damage: bacilli invade Schwann cells. This leads to sensory loss (temperature first, then light touch, pain, pressure), then autonomic loss (dry skin, anhidrosis), then motor loss (claw hand, foot drop, lagophthalmos).
    • Lepra reactions are immune flare-ups and cause most of the disability. They occur in 30–50% of patients, before, during or after MDT: Type 1 (reversal) reaction: delayed-type hypersensitivity in borderline disease (BT/BB/BL). Existing lesions become red, swollen and tender, with acute neuritis; Type 2 reaction = erythema nodosum leprosum (ENL): an immune-complex (type III) systemic vasculitis/panniculitis in LL/BL (high bacillary load). Antigens from dying bacilli form antibody complexes that deposit in vessels and tissues, with high TNF-α. This is why Case 4 has recurrent bouts of fever, joint pain and painful red nodules on the legs and abdomen

    Myths to address

    • "Leprosy is highly contagious and makes fingers fall off": it is hard to catch; digit loss follows numbness and injury.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    M. leprae lives in Schwann cells, and the host's immunity shapes the disease.

    1. 1

      Spread

      Droplets from untreated patients after prolonged close contact; long incubation (years).

    2. 2

      Nerve invasion

      Bacilli infect Schwann cells in cool peripheral nerves.

    3. 3

      Immune spectrum

      Strong Th1 response → few granulomatous lesions; weak response → many bacilli.

    4. 4

      Sensory loss

      Nerve damage causes numb patches and injuries.

    Clinical features

    Cardinal signs (WHO). Leprosy is diagnosed if ≥1 is present:

    1. Definite loss of sensation in a pale (hypopigmented) or reddish skin patch
    2. Thickened/enlarged peripheral nerve with sensory loss and/or weakness of the muscles it supplies
    3. Acid-fast bacilli on slit-skin smear

    Spectrum:

    Nerves to palpate: ulnar (elbow), median (wrist), radial cutaneous (wrist), common peroneal / lateral popliteal (fibular neck), posterior tibial (medial malleolus), great auricular (neck) and supra-orbital.

    Case 4: findings in favour of leprosy:

    • Infiltrated facial lesions for 2 years (LL infiltration, early leonine facies)
    • Glove-and-stocking loss of temperature and touch (temperature lost first)
    • Thickened, tender ulnar and lateral popliteal nerves
    • Painless ulcer after a thorn prick (sensory neuropathy → trophic ulcer)
    • "Slipping footwear" (insensitive feet ± early foot drop from common peroneal palsy)
    • Recurrent fever + arthralgia + crops of painful nodules = ENL (type 2 reaction), typical of LL/BL
    • He is a farmer, likely from an endemic rural setting

    Other organs in LL:

    • Eyes: corneal anaesthesia, lagophthalmos, keratitis, iritis, blindness
    • Testes: orchitis, atrophy, gynaecomastia, infertility
    • Nose: septal perforation, saddle nose
    • Bones: resorption of digits
    • Kidney: amyloidosis, glomerulonephritis

    ENL (type 2):

    • Crops of tender red subcutaneous nodules/plaques on the face, extensor arms, thighs, trunk and abdomen. The distribution is wider than classic EN. Nodules may pustulate or ulcerate (necrotic ENL).
    • Fever, malaise, arthralgia/arthritis, with neuritis, iritis/uveitis, orchitis, dactylitis, lymphadenitis, hepatitis or nephritis.
    • It is often recurrent or chronic, as in Case 4 (every 15 days or so).

    Type 1 reaction:

    • Existing patches suddenly become red, swollen and tender, with new lesions, oedema of the hands, feet and face, and acute painful neuritis with new weakness or numbness.
    • This is an emergency because of the risk of permanent nerve damage.

    Types to recognise

    • Tuberculoid

      Few defined anaesthetic plaques. Site: Any.

    • Borderline

      ‘Swiss-cheese’ annular plaques. Site: Trunk, limbs.

    • Lepromatous

      Diffuse facial infiltration. Site: Face.

    • Earlobe

      Thickening: LL site, smear site. Site: Ears.

    • Type 1 reaction

      Plaques become red, swollen. Site: Existing lesions.

    • Trophic ulcer

      Painless, from sensory loss. Site: Feet, hands.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    UKHSA advice: discuss every suspected case with a Consultant Advisor in Leprosy before smears or biopsies are taken, and refer to a national leprosy centre.

    Investigations:

    • Clinical examination: sensory testing of lesions (cotton wool for touch, warm/cold tubes for temperature, monofilaments), nerve palpation, and a voluntary muscle test.
    • Slit-skin smear (SSS) from the earlobes, forehead and lesions. Stain with Ziehl–Neelsen/Fite. Report the bacterial index (BI) and morphological index. The smear is positive in MB and negative in TT.
    • Skin biopsy (deep incisional, from the active edge, down to fat) for histology. This shows granulomas, nerve involvement and AFB on Fite–Faraco stain, and helps classify on the Ridley–Jopling scale. Lepromatous disease shows foamy macrophages (Virchow/lepra cells) packed with bacilli and globi, with a Grenz zone.
    • M. leprae* PCR on tissue or smear is highly specific. It can also detect drug-resistance mutations (rifampicin rpoB, dapsone folP1, fluoroquinolone gyrA).
    • Nerve studies: nerve conduction, ultrasound or nerve biopsy (pure neural leprosy).
    • Lepromin (Mitsuda) test: not diagnostic. It helps classify (positive in TT, negative in LL).
    • Serology (anti-PGL-I): positive in most MB disease, but limited for diagnosis.
    • Before MDT: FBC, LFTs, U&E, G6PD (dapsone), and screening for HIV, TB (before steroids and rifampicin), hepatitis B/C, Strongyloides (before prolonged steroids) and pregnancy.

    "Leonine facies":

    • Meaning: a lion-like face from diffuse infiltration and thickening of the facial skin, with deepened furrows on the forehead and cheeks, thickened earlobes and nose, and often loss of eyebrows.
    • Causes: Lepromatous leprosy (classic); Cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (mycosis fungoides / Sézary syndrome), leukaemia cutis, other lymphomas; Diffuse cutaneous leishmaniasis / PKDL; Sarcoidosis; Scleromyxoedema / lichen myxoedematosus; Amyloidosis, lipoid proteinosis; Actinic reticuloid / chronic actinic dermatitis; Mastocytosis; Paget's disease of bone / fibrous dysplasia ("leontiasis ossea", where the cause is bony); Severe rosacea/rhinophyma

    Management

    Primary-care role: suspect, refer, support adherence, and spot reactions and adverse drug effects.

    • Refer every suspected case to a national leprosy centre via a Consultant Advisor in Leprosy (e.g. HTD, UCLH, London; see the UKHSA Memorandum on leprosy 2023, Appendix 5). The diagnosing doctor notifies the case to the local authority proper officer / UKHSA health protection team (England), handled confidentially. Contact management goes through the CCDC and UKHSA TB Unit.
    • Urgent/same-day referral: a reaction (type 1 with new nerve pain or weakness; ENL with fever), acute eye symptoms (red painful eye, reduced vision, cannot close the eye), or a new foot drop or claw hand.
    • WHO MDT 2018. Three drugs for all patients (adult doses, specialist-supervised): Rifampicin 600 mg once a month (supervised); Clofazimine 300 mg once a month (supervised) + 50 mg daily; Dapsone 100 mg daily; Duration: PB 6 months; MB 12 months. Case 4 = MB, 12 months; Rifampicin-resistant leprosy: clofazimine + at least two of clarithromycin, minocycline or a fluoroquinolone (e.g. ofloxacin/moxifloxacin) for 6 months, then clofazimine + one of these for 18 months; UK centres may use modified regimens (e.g. daily rifampicin, or longer courses for high bacterial index)
    • Management of ENL: Continue MDT. Reactions are not a reason to stop; Mild ENL: rest, NSAIDs/aspirin for fever and arthralgia (with GI protection); Moderate–severe ENL (or neuritis, iritis, orchitis): oral prednisolone (e.g. starting around 40–60 mg/day, then tapered over weeks–months by the specialist); Clofazimine at higher anti-inflammatory doses (e.g. up to 300 mg/day for limited periods): a steroid-sparing option for chronic or recurrent ENL. Its effect is slow (4–6 weeks); Thalidomide (100–400 mg/day, then tapered) is very effective for recurrent ENL. It is highly teratogenic: in the UK it is supplied under a Pregnancy Prevention Programme, with neuropathy and VTE risks. It is used when steroids fail or cause problems; Refractory ENL: methotrexate, ciclosporin, TNF-α inhibitors (specialist, case-based); Treat iritis urgently with ophthalmology (topical steroid + mydriatic)
    • Type 1 reaction: prednisolone (typically starting around 40 mg/day and tapering slowly over about 3–6 months under specialist supervision) to prevent permanent nerve damage. Splint the affected limb at rest.
    • Prevention of disability (POD): Daily self-examination of hands, feet and eyes; Soak and oil dry skin; Protective footwear (microcellular rubber, well-fitting) and gloves for cooking or hot objects; Prompt dressing and rest of any wound; never walk on an ulcer; Eye protection (glasses; blink exercises; lubricants for lagophthalmos); Physiotherapy, splints, and reconstructive surgery later
    • Contacts: examine household contacts. WHO recommends single-dose rifampicin post-exposure prophylaxis (SDR-PEP) for eligible contacts (adults 600 mg once) after excluding leprosy and TB. This reduces risk by around 50–60%. UK use is per the UKHSA/Consultant Advisor. BCG gives partial protection.

    Treatment ladder

    1. 1

      Refer & notify

      Refer every suspected case to a national leprosy centre (e.g. HTD, UCLH). Notify to UKHSA (confidential); contacts via CCDC / UKHSA.

    2. 2

      WHO MDT 2018 (specialist)

      Rifampicin; Clofazimine; Dapsone

    3. 3

      Reactions (specialist)

      Continue MDT. Type 1: prednisolone (~40 mg/day tapered over 3–6 months) + splint. ENL: NSAIDs if mild; prednisolone (~40–60 mg/day) if moderate–severe; clofazimine high-dose; thalidomide under a Pregnancy Prevention Programme.

    4. 4

      Your safety checks

      Rifampicin: enzyme induction (contraception → IUD / condoms per FSRH; warfarin, DOACs, ARVs, azoles, ciclosporin, methadone, steroids); orange urine and tears. Dapsone: haemolysis, methaemoglobinaemia, hypersensitivity syndrome in first 4–6 weeks; FBC, LFT.

    Caution

    Prescribing limits and pitfalls

    • Prevent disability: Daily self-examination of numb hands, feet and eyes; protective footwear.

    Referral

    • Same day

      Reaction: new nerve pain or weakness, ENL with fever, new foot drop or claw hand.

    • Same day

      Eye pain, redness, reduced vision or can’t close the eye: same-day ophthalmology.

    • Urgent

      Any suspected leprosy: Consultant Advisor / national leprosy centre.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • New nerve pain, numbness or weakness, or painful nodules with fever on treatment: lepra reaction, urgent specialist contact.
    • Eye pain or redness: same-day ophthalmology.
    • Thalidomide for ENL is specialist-only with a strict pregnancy prevention programme.

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Disability, ulcers, eye damage, stigma; leprosy reactions.

    Prognosis

    • Cured with MDT (6–12 months); nerve damage persists.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • Rifampicin (even monthly) induces enzymes, which matters around the dose day: It reduces combined/progestogen-only pill, implant and emergency contraception efficacy. Advise a copper IUD/LNG-IUS or condoms, and follow FSRH guidance; It interacts with warfarin, DOACs, antiretrovirals, azoles, ciclosporin, methadone and steroids (a higher prednisolone dose may be needed); It colours urine, tears and contact lenses orange-red

    • Dapsone: Check G6PD before starting. It causes dose-related haemolysis and methaemoglobinaemia (breathlessness, cyanosis); Dapsone hypersensitivity syndrome usually appears in the first 4–6 weeks: fever, rash, lymphadenopathy, hepatitis. Stop and seek urgent help. HLA-B*13:01 is a risk marker in some Asian populations; Monitor FBC and LFTs; agranulocytosis has been reported

    • Clofazimine: Red-brown to grey-black skin pigmentation (most marked in lesions and on dark skin), which fades slowly over months to years after stopping. Warn patients in advance, as it adds to stigma; Dry, ichthyotic skin (use emollients), red-coloured sweat, tears and urine; GI upset and, at high doses, enteropathy (crystal deposition), so take with food. QT prolongation

    • Steroid courses for reactions are often long. Give bone protection, GI protection where indicated, glucose and BP checks, steroid card and sick-day rules. Screen for and treat Strongyloides and latent TB first.

    • Adherence: the course is long (6–12 months) and skin lesions fade slowly. Explain that reactions are not treatment failure, and that numbness may not recover even when the infection is cured.

    • Stigma: Use person-first language ("person affected by leprosy", not "leper"); Emphasise that leprosy is curable, that people are not infectious once on MDT, and that casual contact (touch, sharing meals, sex) does not spread it; Maintain strict confidentiality (notification is handled in confidence); Signpost LEPRA and The Leprosy Mission England & Wales for patient support
