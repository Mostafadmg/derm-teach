    Foundations: describing skin

    Describing skin lesions: the language of dermatology

    Module 1 · Week 1

    The core vocabulary for describing a rash: primary lesions, secondary changes, configuration and distribution. A precise description drives the differential and makes referrals and photo requests useful.

    Coversprimary lesionssecondary lesionsmaculepapuleplaquevesiclebullascalecrustlichenificationannulardermatomalKoebner
    Think of it when

    You need to describe a rash in notes, a referral, an advice-and-guidance request or a photo request.

    What drives it

    The level of the skin involved (epidermis, dermis, subcutis) decides what you see and feel.

    Backbone of care

    Describe lesion type → colour → surface → configuration → distribution, then build the differential from that description.

    Never miss

    Non-blanching purpura, blisters or erosions with mucosal involvement, and any changing pigmented lesion.

    What it is

    Primary lesions (macule → cyst)

    A primary lesion is the initial morphological change produced directly by the disease process, before scratching, infection, treatment or time alter it. Correctly naming it is the first step to a differential diagnosis.

    TermDefinition (paraphrased; DermNet terminology)Palpable?Size cut-off
    MaculeFlat, circumscribed change in colour/texture onlyNo<1 cm
    PatchFlat area of colour/texture change (a large macule)No>1 cm
    PapuleSolid, raised, palpable lesionYes<1 cm
    PlaqueRaised, palpable, flat-topped area, wider than it is tall (papules may coalesce into plaques)Yes>1 cm
    NoduleSolid, palpable lump, often deeper (dermis/subcutis) — may be felt more than seenYes>1 cm (some: >0.5 cm)
    VesicleSmall fluid-filled (clear) blisterYes<1 cm
    BullaLarge fluid-filled blisterYes>1 cm
    PustuleVisible collection of pus (neutrophils ± debris) — can be infective or sterileYesUsually <1 cm
    Wheal (weal)Transient, itchy, oedematous, pale-centred/pink papule or plaque from dermal oedema; individual lesions last <24 hYesAny
    CystClosed sac lined by epithelium containing fluid or semi-solid material (e.g. keratin)Yes (fluctuant/firm)Any

    Related terms worth knowing: purpura/petechiae (non-blanching bleeding into skin: petechiae <2–3 mm, purpura larger, ecchymosis = bruise), telangiectasia (visible dilated superficial vessels), burrow (scabies), comedone (blocked follicle: open/blackhead, closed/whitehead), abscess (deep localised pus collection), erythema (blanching redness), tumour (large nodule — avoid alarming patients with the word).

    Secondary lesions (scale → scar)

    Secondary lesions develop from primary lesions over time or because of external factors — scratching, rubbing, infection, healing or treatment. They tell you about chronicity, itch, and depth of tissue damage.

    TermDefinition (paraphrased)Depth / key point
    ScaleVisible flakes of excess or abnormally shed stratum corneum (hyperkeratosis/parakeratosis)Epidermal surface
    CrustDried serum, blood or pus on the skin surface ("scab")Surface — often after vesicles/pustules/erosions break
    ErosionPartial loss of epidermis (dermis not breached)Heals without scarring
    UlcerFull-thickness loss of epidermis plus part of dermis ± deeperHeals with scarring
    FissureLinear crack or split through epidermis, often into dermisPainful; thick/dry skin over joints, heels, angles of mouth
    LichenificationThickened, leathery skin with exaggerated normal skin markings from chronic rubbing/scratchingEpidermal hyperplasia (acanthosis) + hyperkeratosis
    AtrophyThinning of epidermis (fine wrinkling, translucency, visible vessels) and/or dermis/subcutis (depression)Steroid atrophy, striae, lichen sclerosus, ageing
    ExcoriationLinear or punctate erosions/ulcers caused by scratching or pickingMarker of itch (or skin picking)
    ScarFibrous tissue replacing normal dermis after injury that breached the dermisAtrophic, hypertrophic (within wound) or keloid (beyond wound)

    Other secondary/descriptive terms: maceration (soggy white skin in occluded folds/webs), xerosis (dryness), post-inflammatory hyper-/hypopigmentation, eschar (black adherent necrotic crust).

    Distribution & configuration

    • Distribution = where lesions are on the body and how widespread.
    • Configuration (arrangement/shape) = how individual lesions are shaped or grouped relative to one another.

    Lichenification in chronic atopic eczema

    Case: an 8-year-old boy with longstanding atopic dermatitis, worst in the flexures, troubled by itch and poor sleep. Image shows thickened skin, accentuated skin creases and hyperpigmentation.

    How it’s caused

    Primary lesions (macule → cyst)

    • Macule/patch: change only in pigment or vascularity without thickening — more/less melanin (freckle, lentigo, café-au-lait; vitiligo) or vascular change (erythema, port-wine stain).
    • Papule/plaque: epidermal thickening (acanthosis, hyperkeratosis) and/or dermal inflammatory infiltrate (psoriasis, lichen planus, eczema) or cellular proliferation.
    • Nodule: deeper dermal/subcutaneous infiltrate, proliferation or deposit (tumour, granuloma, prurigo).
    • Vesicle/bulla: fluid accumulates within or beneath the epidermis when cell–cell (desmosome) or cell–basement-membrane (hemidesmosome) adhesion fails, or when keratinocytes are damaged (viral ballooning, spongiosis). - Pustule: neutrophils collect in the epidermis or follicle — bacteria (folliculitis, impetigo), or sterile inflammation (pustular psoriasis, acne, PPP).
    • Wheal: mast-cell degranulation (histamine) → transient vasodilation and leaky superficial dermal capillaries → dermal oedema without epidermal change (hence no scale, resolves without mark).
    • Cyst: epithelial-lined cavity, e.g. epidermoid cyst from infundibular epithelium producing keratin.

    Secondary lesions (scale → scar)

    • Scale: accelerated epidermal turnover (psoriasis — keratinocytes reach the surface in days and retain nuclei = parakeratosis) or abnormal desquamation (ichthyosis, eczema, tinea).
    • Crust: serous exudate from damaged epidermis dries on the surface; golden/honey colour suggests S. aureus/streptococcal impetigo.
    • Erosion/ulcer: loss of a blister roof, trauma, ischaemia (arterial), venous hypertension, neuropathy (diabetic foot), infection or malignancy. Depth relative to the basement membrane decides scarring.
    • Fissure: dry, inelastic thickened stratum corneum splits under movement/tension.
    • Lichenification: repeated mechanical trauma (rubbing/scratching) → keratinocyte proliferation, thickened epidermis and dermal fibrosis — the visible footprint of the itch–scratch cycle.
    • Atrophy: reduced keratinocyte proliferation and collagen synthesis (potent topical/systemic corticosteroids, ageing, photo-damage) or inflammatory destruction (lichen sclerosus, discoid lupus).
    • Excoriation: scratching removes epidermis; may be the only visible sign in itch without rash (e.g. cholestasis, iron deficiency, scabies).
    • Scar: fibroblasts lay down collagen to repair dermis; excess collagen → hypertrophic/keloid; insufficient → atrophic (acne, varicella).

    Distribution & configuration

    Pattern reflects mechanism: nerve (dermatomal = reactivated VZV in a sensory ganglion), external contact (sharp lines, geometric shapes = contact/irritant/artefact), light (photo-exposed sites with sparing under chin/behind ears), trauma (Koebner), blood/lymph vessels, embryological lines (Blaschko), friction/moisture (flexures), centrifugal fungal spread (annular).

    Lichenification in chronic atopic eczema

    Barrier dysfunction (e.g. filaggrin loss-of-function, reduced ceramides) + type-2 inflammation (IL-4/IL-13/IL-31 itch signalling) → itch → scratch → further barrier damage and inflammation → keratinocyte hyperproliferation → lichenification. Pigment change follows inflammation (PIH), more marked in darker phototypes.

    Lichenification in chronic atopic eczema

    • Age pattern: infants — face/cheeks, scalp, extensors (napkin often spared); children — flexures (antecubital/popliteal fossae, wrists, ankles, neck); adults — flexures, hands, face/neck, generalised.
    • Chronic phase: lichenification, excoriations, xerosis, fissures, PIH/hypopigmentation; acute phase: erythema, papulovesicles, weeping, crust.
    • Sleep disturbance = marker of severity and impact (POEM, CDLQI).

    Diagnosis

    Primary lesions (macule → cyst)

    • Inspect in good light with magnification; palpate (wear gloves where appropriate).
    • Decide flat vs raised vs fluid-filled vs pus-filled vs deep; then measure (ruler/photo with scale) to choose the correct size-based term.
    • Use diascopy (press a glass slide): blanching = erythema/vascular dilation; non-blanching = purpura.
    • Document primary lesion first, then the secondary change (e.g. "erythematous plaques with silvery scale").

    Secondary lesions (scale → scar)

    • Describe in order: primary lesion + secondary change (e.g. "lichenified, excoriated plaques in both antecubital fossae").
    • Gently lift scale/crust — bleeding points under psoriatic scale (Auspitz sign; not routinely elicited).
    • Assess ulcer: site, size, edge (sloping = venous; punched-out = arterial/neuropathic; rolled = BCC; undermined violaceous = pyoderma), base, exudate, surrounding skin, pulses/ABPI (per local wound pathway).
    • Atrophy: shiny, wrinkled "cigarette-paper" surface, visible vessels; ask about steroid use, potency, duration.

    Distribution & configuration

    Undress adequately and inspect the whole skin (plus scalp, nails, mouth) — distribution clues are missed in partial examinations. Note sparing as well as involvement (e.g. napkin-area sparing in infant atopic eczema; under-chin sparing in photosensitivity).

    Lichenification in chronic atopic eczema

    Clinical diagnosis (NICE CG57 / UK Working Party criteria: itchy skin + ≥3 of flexural involvement history, asthma/hay fever history, generally dry skin, onset <2 years, visible flexural eczema). Assess severity (clear/mild/moderate/severe — NICE) noting lichenification and excoriation as features of moderate–severe disease; check for infection (bacterial and herpetic).

    Differentials

    Primary lesions (macule → cyst)

    Often confusedDistinguish by
    Macule vs patchSize (<1 cm vs >1 cm) — both flat
    Papule vs noduleSize and depth; nodules are deeper/firmer
    Plaque vs patchPlaque is palpable (thickened); patch is flat
    Vesicle vs bullaSize (<1 cm vs >1 cm) — tutor's Day 1 prompt
    Pustule vs vesicleContents: yellow/white pus vs clear fluid (vesicles may become pustular with time)
    Wheal vs plaqueWheals are transient (<24 h), shifting, no surface change
    Cyst vs nodule/lipomaPunctum, fluctuance, epithelial lining; lipoma is soft, rubbery, no punctum

    Secondary lesions (scale → scar)

    Often confusedDistinguish by
    Erosion vs ulcerDepth — erosion is epidermal only (no scar); ulcer reaches dermis (scar)
    Scale vs crustScale = keratin flakes (white/silver/yellow); crust = dried exudate (honey, brown, haemorrhagic)
    Lichenification vs plaqueLichenification = secondary thickening with exaggerated skin lines from rubbing; plaque is the primary raised lesion
    Lichenification vs PIHBoth often coexist in skin of colour; PIH is colour only (flat), lichenification is palpable thickening
    Excoriation vs fissureExcoriation = linear scratch marks (patient-made); fissure = crack from dryness/tension
    Hypertrophic scar vs keloidKeloid extends beyond wound and rarely regresses; hypertrophic stays within and may improve
    Atrophy vs atrophic scarAtrophy can be reversible (steroid epidermal thinning early); scars are permanent dermal repair

    Distribution & configuration

    • Annular + scale at edge + asymmetry → tinea (scrape before steroids); annular smooth, no scale → granuloma annulare; annular evanescent → urticaria.
    • Flexural red, well-demarcated, little scale → inverse psoriasis; flexural with satellite pustules → candida; flexural lichenified → atopic eczema.
    • Dermatomal pain before rash → zoster (early antivirals).

    Lichenification in chronic atopic eczema

    Lichen simplex chronicus (localised, adult, no atopy needed); psoriasis (well-demarcated, silvery scale, extensors); scabies (burrows, family itch, web spaces); contact dermatitis; tinea (asymmetric, scaly edge).

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    Primary lesions (macule → cyst)

    • The primary lesion steers the differential and therefore whether you can treat (e.g. plaque psoriasis, acne papules, urticarial wheals) or must refer (new growing nodule, unexplained bullae, rapidly evolving pigmented patch).
    • Bullae, widespread pustules with systemic upset, or eczema herpeticum vesicles → same-day medical review (not OTC management).
    • Suspicious lesions: follow NICE NG12 suspected-cancer recognition and local 2WW/urgent pathways (melanoma, SCC); BCC usually routine unless high-risk site/behaviour.

    Secondary lesions (scale → scar)

    • Scale: keratolytics (e.g. salicylic acid preparations, urea) and emollients; choose ointments for thick scale.
    • Crust (impetigo): hydrogen peroxide 1% cream for localised non-bullous impetigo per NICE NG153; topical/oral antibiotics per extent, local guidance. (Pharmacy First in England covers impetigo in eligible patients — check current service specification.)
    • Erosions/ulcers: wound care, infection review, refer unexplained erosions/ulcers (pemphigus, malignancy, non-healing >2 weeks/suspicious edge). Leg ulcers → local leg-ulcer/tissue-viability pathway; diabetic foot ulcer → urgent foot protection/MDT service.
    • Fissures: greasy emollients, occlusion overnight, treat underlying eczema; angular cheilitis — consider candida/staph cover, dentures, iron/B12/folate.
    • Lichenification: needs potent topical corticosteroid for adequate duration under emollient regimen (ointment, occlusion may help), break itch–scratch cycle; refer if refractory.
    • Atrophy: prevention — right potency, right site, right duration, fingertip units; avoid potent steroids on face/flexures/genitals unless specialist directed.
    • Excoriation: treat the itch cause (eczema, scabies, systemic causes); keep nails short; consider infection.
    • Scar/keloid: counsel early treatment of inflammatory acne; keloid → refer (intralesional steroid/specialist).

    Distribution & configuration

    Pattern changes management: e.g. zoster (antiviral within 72 h, ophthalmic branch → same-day eye review), tinea (antifungal; avoid steroid-alone "tinea incognito"), photo-distributed (review photosensitising drugs), contact pattern (identify/avoid trigger; patch test referral).

    Lichenification in chronic atopic eczema

    Management for pharmacist/IP (UK — NICE CG57 / CKS)

    • Emollients liberally and often (250–500 g/week for a child, as soap substitute; leave-on).
    • Topical corticosteroid by severity and site: lichenified flexural areas in a child usually need a moderate (or short-course potent, per CKS/specialist guidance on limbs, not face) steroid once or twice daily until clear, then step down; consider weekend "proactive" maintenance for frequent flares. Mild potency for face/neck (moderate short courses only if severe).
    • Topical calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus/pimecrolimus) as second line for face/flexures in children ≥2 years when steroids unsuitable — prescriber/specialist initiation per local policy.
    • Itch and sleep: sedating antihistamine short-term only (≥6 months; not routine), cool environment, cotton clothing, short nails, bandaging/wet wraps under specialist advice.
    • Infection: bacterial — see NICE NG190 (secondary bacterial infection of eczema); eczema herpeticum → same-day referral, start aciclovir per guidance (ophthalmology if periocular).
    • Refer if uncontrolled despite optimal therapy, diagnostic doubt, recurrent infections, significant sleep/psychosocial impact, or suspected contact allergy.
    7

    Red flags & referral

    • Non-blanching spots or purpura in an unwell patient: think meningococcal sepsis or vasculitis and escalate the same day.
    • Blisters or erosions with mouth, eye or genital involvement: SJS/TEN or immunobullous disease.
    • Punched-out erosions on eczema: eczema herpeticum, same-day assessment.
    • A new or changing pigmented lesion: refer on the suspected-cancer pathway (NICE NG12).
    8

    Prescriber pearls

    Primary lesions (macule → cyst)

    • Write the description before you write the diagnosis — it protects against anchoring.
    • Use plain language with patients ("small raised spots", "flat brown mark") but precise terms in notes and referrals.
    • In skin of colour, erythema may look violaceous, grey, dark brown or be subtle — rely more on texture, warmth and palpation.

    Secondary lesions (scale → scar)

    • Secondary changes tell you chronicity: lichenification and atrophy take weeks–months to develop and to reverse — set expectations.
    • Crust or weeping on eczema = think infection (bacterial or herpetic). Punched-out erosions/monomorphic vesicles = eczema herpeticum → urgent same-day review.
    • Explain atrophy risk honestly but avoid "steroid phobia": under-treatment drives lichenification.

    Distribution & configuration

    • Ask "where did it start and how did it spread?" Geometric or "outside-in" shapes suggest something external. Bilateral symmetrical = more likely endogenous.

    Lichenification in chronic atopic eczema

    • Show the family the difference between lichenification and PIH: thick skin should soften with adequate steroid + emollient over weeks; pigment change takes months and is not a sign of steroid damage.
    • Lichenified skin is less permeable — ointments and adequate potency/duration are needed; under-treatment due to steroid fear prolongs the itch–scratch cycle.
    • Fingertip-unit teaching; written eczema action plan; "steroids first, then emollient after ~15–30 min" (order matters less than adequate quantities).
    • Red flag script: "If the eczema suddenly becomes painful with clusters of small blisters or punched-out sores, or your child has a fever, seek same-day help."
