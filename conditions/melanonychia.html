    Nails

    Melanonychia & nail melanoma

    Module 3 · Week 2

    A brown or black band in the nail is usually benign (ethnic, drug or trauma-related), but a new, widening or irregular band in one nail, or pigment spreading onto the nail fold, needs urgent assessment for nail-unit melanoma.

    Coversmelanonychialongitudinal melanonychiabrown nail stripenail melanomasubungual melanomaHutchinson signABCDEF nail
    Think of it when

    A brown or black longitudinal stripe in one or more nails.

    What drives it

    Melanocyte activation in the nail matrix (ethnic, drug, trauma, inflammation) or proliferation (naevus, melanoma).

    Backbone of care

    Use the nail ABCDEF rule, photograph with a ruler, and refer suspicious single-digit bands on the suspected-cancer pathway.

    Never miss

    Subungual melanoma: often diagnosed late, especially in darker skin where bands are common.

    What it is

    Melanonychia = brown/black nail discolouration, often a longitudinal band (melanonychia striata) from melanin deposited in the growing nail plate. May be diffuse. Pigment arises from:

    1. Melanocytic activation (more melanin, normal melanocyte numbers) — racial/physiological, trauma, drugs, inflammatory disease, endocrine, infection, etc.
    2. Melanocytic hyperplasia — lentigo, naevus, or melanoma of the nail unit.
    2

    How it’s caused

    Who gets it / risk context

    • Common in skin of colour (Fitzpatrick V–VI); nearly all Afro-Caribbean adults develop some nail pigmentation by mid-life; also frequent in some Asian populations. Multiple regular bands = often benign racial pattern.
    • Nail-unit melanoma is uncommon but important: any age possible; relatively higher proportion of melanomas in people with darker skin types are acral/nail; classic teaching ages ~50–70; digits = thumb, hallux, index most often. Family history of melanoma increases risk. Trauma association is debated — trauma may draw attention to a lesion already present (Day 4 Bob Marley teaching anecdote).
    • Up to a substantial fraction of nail melanomas are amelanotic/hypomelanotic — do not wait for a black streak.
    3

    Clinical features

    Benign vs concerning features

    More reassuring (often benign)More concerning (refer urgently)
    Multiple nails, stable, similar bandsSingle nail, new or changing band
    Narrow (<~3 mm), regular parallel lines, homogeneous brownWidth ≥3 mm, especially widening proximally; irregular borders
    Stable over yearsChange in colour/width/speed of growth; dystrophy, splitting, bleeding, nodule
    No periungual pigmentHutchinson’s sign — pigment on proximal/lateral nail fold/cuticle
    Child with stable congenital naevus pattern (still specialist if unsure)Adult new single-digit band without clear racial/multi-nail pattern

    Hutchinson’s sign: periungual extension of pigment historically linked to nail melanoma. Valuable but not pathognomonic — pseudo-Hutchinson’s can occur (e.g. pigment showing through thin fold, Bowen disease, some benign naevi). Still, Hutchinson’s + irregular dermoscopy in an adult single nail = urgent specialist pathway, not watchful waiting in primary care IP practice.

    ABCDE / ABCDEF of nail melanoma (forum teaching)

    Forum used both ABCDE (Seebacher-style teaching) and full ABCDEF:

    LetterMeaning (paraphrased)
    AAge ~50–70; higher relative concern in African, Asian, Native American ancestry (any ethnicity can be affected)
    BBand brown-black, width ≥3 mm, blurred/irregular borders
    CChange — size, colour, or nail structure not improving
    DDigit — thumb, hallux, or index finger most often
    EExtension — Hutchinson’s sign (pigment onto periungual skin)
    FFamily history of melanoma (ABCDEF version)

    Use as a memory aid, not a scoring rule that replaces clinical judgement.

    Day 3 case teaching (incidental toe pigment)

    Adult of African descent; incidental second-toe band; football (trauma history possible); dermoscopy heterogeneous irregular brown/black pigment extending to proximal fold and cuticle (= Hutchinson’s). Differentials include subungual haematoma and fungal melanonychia, but irregular pigment + Hutchinson’s → do not reassure on trauma alone.

    4

    Diagnosis

    Dermoscopic clues (onychoscopy)

    Benign pattern (typical): regular brown lines, even spacing/thickness/colour, parallel, sharp margins.

    Suspicious pattern: irregular lines (colour, spacing, thickness, parallelism), grey-black colours, width occupying large fraction of plate, granular pigmentation, micro-Hutchinson (pigment only visible on dermoscopy), associated dystrophy. Dermoscopy helps target biopsy site but does not replace histology when melanoma is plausible.

    Diagnosis & next step for UK IP

    • History: onset, change, trauma, drugs (e.g. hydroxyurea, chemotherapies, antimalarials), endocrine, family history, occupation.
    • Examine all nails, skin, mucosa if syndromic clues (Laugier–Hunziker, Peutz–Jeghers).
    • Next step if suspicious: urgent specialist dermatology / skin-cancer pathway referral for consideration of nail-unit biopsy (matrix ± bed as directed by dermatosurgeon; histopathology is gold standard).
    • IP role: recognise red flags, photograph if helpful, do not attempt nail-matrix biopsy in community pharmacy/primary-care IP settings; avoid prolonged antifungal “trials” that delay cancer referral.
    • Subungual haematoma usually migrates distally with growth; static/widening proximal pigment does not behave like simple blood.
    5

    Differentials

    • Ethnic (racial) melanonychia: several nails, often thumbs and index fingers, in darker skin types; stable, even bands.
    • Drug-induced: several nails after chemotherapy (hydroxyurea, cyclophosphamide), zidovudine, antimalarials or minocycline.
    • Frictional or traumatic: toenails rubbed by footwear; subungual haematoma is red-purple to black, grows out and does not reach the cuticle as a band.
    • Fungal melanonychia (Trichophyton rubrum var. nigricans, moulds): often wider distally, with nail-plate debris.
    • Nail matrix naevus or lentigo: a single even band in a child or young adult; still needs specialist assessment if changing.
    • Nail-unit melanoma and pigmented SCC (Bowen’s disease of the nail).
    6

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    Management outlook

    • Benign activation/racial melanonychia: explanation, photo-monitor, safety-net for change.
    • Confirmed nail melanoma: specialist surgical excision pathways (may include digit amputation depending on stage); prognosis historically worse than many cutaneous melanomas — early referral saves lives.
    7

    Red flags & referral

    • Single nail, band >3 mm or widening, irregular colour, blurred borders, Hutchinson sign (pigment on the nail fold), nail dystrophy or bleeding: urgent suspected-cancer referral (NICE NG12).
    • Any new band in an adult over 50, or a history of melanoma.
    8

    Prescriber pearls

    • Photograph the nail with a ruler and date it: change over time is the most useful sign.
    • Mark the top of a suspected haematoma with a small notch: if the pigment moves out with the nail, it is blood.
    • Pigment on the nail fold (Hutchinson sign) can be faked by a see-through cuticle (pseudo-Hutchinson sign), so let a specialist decide.
    • Ask about change, family history and previous melanoma; examine all nails and the skin.
