    Pigment & colour change

    Melasma & facial melanosis

    Module 6 · Week 3

    Acquired brown facial patches driven by hormones, UV and visible light. Strict tinted sunscreen is the foundation; avoid long-term hydroquinone (ochronosis risk).

    Coversmelasmachloasmafacial melanosisexogenous ochronosishydroquinonedrug photosensitivity
    Think of it when

    Symmetrical brown patches on cheeks, forehead or upper lip in an adult (often after pregnancy or hormonal contraception), worse with sun and heat.

    What drives it

    Melanocyte hyperactivity with epidermal ± dermal pigment; UV and visible light, hormones, heat and inflammation drive it.

    Backbone of care

    Daily tinted broad-spectrum SPF 30–50, trigger avoidance, and UK-available lighteners (azelaic acid, retinoid). Refer for specialist combinations.

    Never miss

    Exogenous ochronosis from prolonged hydroquinone or illegal bleaching creams — stop HQ and refer; do not escalate lighteners.

    What it is

    Melasma is an acquired disorder of facial hyperpigmentation — symmetrical brown to grey-brown patches on sun-exposed face. It is commoner in women of reproductive age and in darker skin phototypes, but men are affected too.

    This page also covers exogenous ochronosis (hydroquinone-related blue-black pigment) and a brief note on drug photosensitivity/pigmentation, which sit in the same facial-melanosis teaching week.

    2

    How it’s caused

    • Genetic predisposition plus UV and visible light, heat, pregnancy and hormonal contraception, thyroid disease, and cosmetics/irritation.
    • Melanocytes overproduce melanin; pigment may sit in epidermis, dermis, or both (Wood’s lamp / clinical pattern).
    • Exogenous ochronosis: prolonged topical hydroquinone (often unregulated bleaching creams) → dermal ochre pigment; can be permanent.
    • Drug photosensitivity: tetracyclines, thiazides, amiodarone, NSAIDs, quinolones, sulfonylureas and others cause photo-distributed eruptions or slate pigment.
    3

    Clinical features

    • Centro-facial, malar or mandibular patterns; upper lip common.
    • Usually asymptomatic; psychological impact can be high.
    • Exogenous ochronosis: speckled blue-black “caviar” papules on treated sites.
    • Drug photo-eruptions: sharp cut-off at clothing, sparing under chin and upper eyelids.

    Skin of colour

    Melasma is commoner and often mixed epidermal–dermal. Judge severity on colour contrast and area, not redness.

    4

    Diagnosis

    • Clinical. Ask about hormones, pregnancy plans, drugs, cosmetics and skin-lightening (hydroquinone, steroids, mercury).
    • Wood’s lamp may help map epidermal vs dermal pigment but is imperfect in darker skin.
    • Biopsy if exogenous ochronosis, lichenoid pigment or malignancy is suspected.
    • Drug timeline for photo-distributed pigment.

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    Foundation for everyone

    • Daily tinted broad-spectrum SPF 30–50 (iron oxides block visible light); reapply outdoors; hat and shade; reduce heat exposure (saunas, hot kitchens).
    • Stop irritating acids/scrubs and unregulated bleaching products.
    • Camouflage cosmetics if wanted.

    UK primary-care lightening options

    • Azelaic acid 15–20% (also useful in pregnancy as a safer option).
    • Topical retinoid (adapalene/tretinoin) off-label for pigment — avoid in pregnancy.
    • Specialist: short Kligman-type triple combinations, tranexamic acid, chemical peels, carefully chosen lasers — slow response; dermal pigment is hardest.

    Exogenous ochronosis & drug pigment

    • Stop hydroquinone and illegal bleaching creams immediately; strict tinted sunscreen; refer — lasers are specialist and results incomplete.
    • Drug photo-reactions: medication review with the prescriber, UVA-strong sunscreen, short topical steroid for acute dermatitis; Yellow Card if appropriate.

    Hydroquinone caution

    Long-term or high-strength HQ (especially from unregulated creams) risks exogenous ochronosis. Prefer time-limited specialist courses only.

    7

    Red flags & referral

    • Blue-black speckled pigment after bleaching creams → stop HQ, refer.
    • Diagnostic doubt with lichenoid or contact pigment → dermatology (biopsy ± patch testing).
    • Sudden photo-distributed pigment with systemic drug — review the culprit with the original prescriber.
    8

    Prescriber pearls

    • The single most important advice is strict photoprotection including visible light (tinted sunscreen), not another cream.
    • Melasma in pregnancy: sunscreen, camouflage and azelaic acid; defer stronger agents.
    • Ask specifically about imported “fairness” creams — they may hide steroids, mercury or HQ.
    • Set expectations: months of improvement, and relapse is common without ongoing sun protection.
