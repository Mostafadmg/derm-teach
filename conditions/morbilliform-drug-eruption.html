    Morbilliform drug eruption & drug urticaria

    The commonest drug rash: symmetrical pink macules and papules 4–14 days after a new drug, and drug-induced urticaria. How to tell them from SCARs and viral rashes, what to stop, and how to label the allergy.

    Step 1 of 9 · 1 min

    Overview

    Module 1 · Week 3maculopapular drug rashexanthematous eruptionamoxicillin rash

    • Spot it

      A symmetrical, itchy measles-like rash on the trunk 1–2 weeks after an antibiotic, anticonvulsant or allopurinol.

    • Why

      Delayed T-cell reaction (morbilliform) or immediate IgE/mast-cell reaction (urticaria) to the drug.

    • Treat

      Stop the culprit if possible; emollient, antihistamine and a moderate steroid; review in 48 h; record the reaction type precisely.

    • Never miss

      Fever, facial oedema, mucosal or skin pain, blisters or organ involvement: it is not a simple exanthem.

    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    What it is

    • Morbilliform/maculopapular exanthem (MPE): the commonest cADR (~75–95%). Symmetrical, itchy, pink macules and papules starting on the trunk.
    • Drug urticaria/angioedema: wheals lasting <24 h ± angioedema, in the minutes to hours after a dose.

    Step 2 of 9 · 1 min

    Why it happens

    • MPE is a delayed type IVb/IVc T-cell reaction; latency is typically 4–14 days.
    • The amoxicillin–EBV rash occurs in most patients with infectious mononucleosis given aminopenicillins. It is usually not a true allergy.
    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    How it’s caused

    How it is caused

    • MPE is a delayed type IVb/IVc T-cell reaction; latency is typically 4–14 days.
    • Urticaria is IgE-mediated (β-lactams), pseudo-allergic (NSAIDs via COX-1 inhibition → leukotriene shift; opioids, vancomycin, contrast → direct mast-cell degranulation), or part of a serum-sickness-like reaction (cefaclor, amoxicillin in children; 1–3 weeks).
    • The amoxicillin–EBV rash occurs in most patients with infectious mononucleosis given aminopenicillins. It is usually not a true allergy.
    Next: What you’ll see
    3

    Step 3 of 9 · 1 min

    What you’ll see

    • MPE: begins on the trunk/flexures and spreads to the limbs, symmetrically. It often spares the face and does not involve mucosae. Mild fever is possible. It resolves in 1–2 weeks after stopping, often with fine desquamation.
    • Urticaria: wheals with a flare, individual lesions lasting <24 h; ± angioedema of lips, eyelids or genitals.

    Diagnosis

    Clinical diagnosis plus the drug timeline. For MPE, FBC/LFTs help exclude early DRESS (eosinophilia, transaminitis). With urticaria, check for anaphylaxis. Refer to allergy for possible β-lactam allergy testing/de-labelling.

    Differentials

    Viral exanthem; early DRESS (facial oedema, fever, eosinophilia, longer latency); early SJS/TEN (skin pain, dusky lesions, mucositis); urticarial vasculitis (the vasculitis notes: wheals >24 h, bruising); acute GVHD; scarlet fever.

    6

    Step 6 of 9 · 1 min

    Treat

    Doses as in the source notes. Always confirm in the current BNF and local formulary.

    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    • Stop the drug (or "treat through" only if essential and benign, in hospital with specialist agreement).
    • MPE: emollient + moderate/potent TCS twice daily for 5–7 days, and an antihistamine for itch.
    • Urticaria: a non-sedating antihistamine (e.g. cetirizine 10 mg; up to 4× the licensed dose off-label for chronic urticaria — BSACI). A short oral prednisolone course only for severe acute urticaria. Anaphylaxis → adrenaline.
    • NSAID-exacerbated urticaria/angioedema: avoid all COX-1 inhibitors. Paracetamol is usually tolerated (below 1 g); a selective COX-2 inhibitor may be tolerated after specialist challenge.
    • Document, Yellow Card if serious, and arrange allergy referral if the label matters (e.g. penicillin).

    Red flags / refer

    Mucosal involvement, skin pain, blistering, fever >38.5 °C with facial oedema/lymphadenopathy, eosinophilia >1.5 × 10⁹/L, deranged LFTs → treat as a possible SCAR. Anaphylaxis → 999.

    IP scope

    Within IP scope (symptomatic treatment, stopping/switching drugs, documentation, referral to allergy).

    Red flags & referral

    • Routine

      Rash with fever, facial swelling, eosinophilia or abnormal LFTs: treat as possible DRESS.

    • 999 / A&E

      Urticaria with breathing, throat or circulation symptoms: anaphylaxis, IM adrenaline and 999.

    Prescriber pearls

    • The rash may worsen for a couple of days after stopping — that does not mean the stop failed.

    • "Amoxicillin rash in glandular fever" is not usually penicillin allergy — but label carefully and refer for de-labelling rather than ignoring it.
