    Tropical & travel

    Mycetoma (Madura foot)

    A slowly progressive, painless swelling of the foot (or other site) with sinuses discharging grains, after soil organisms enter through a minor wound. Refer to tropical medicine early; fungal and bacterial forms are treated very differently.

    Module 5 · Week 4 · 5 images

    Also coversMadura footeumycetomaactinomycetomadischarging sinusesgrainsNocardia
    Think of it when

    A painless, slowly enlarging swollen foot with multiple discharging sinuses in someone from an endemic tropical area.

    What drives it

    Soil fungi (eumycetoma) or actinomycete bacteria (actinomycetoma) implanted by a thorn or wound form grains in deep tissue.

    Backbone of care

    Specialist diagnosis (grain culture, biopsy, imaging); long antibiotic courses for actinomycetoma; antifungals plus surgery for eumycetoma.

    Never miss

    Bone involvement and late presentation leading to amputation; other causes of discharging sinuses (TB, osteomyelitis, HS).

    What it is

    Mycetoma is a chronic, slowly progressive, granulomatous infection of the skin, subcutaneous tissue, fascia and eventually bone. It is caused by organisms from soil or plants introduced through minor injury. Its hallmark is a triad:

    1. painless firm subcutaneous swelling
    2. multiple sinuses
    3. discharge containing grains (colonies of the organism)

    There are two forms:

    • Eumycetoma is fungal (e.g. Madurella mycetomatis, the most common cause worldwide).
    • Actinomycetoma is bacterial, from filamentous aerobic actinomycetes (Nocardia, Actinomadura, Streptomyces).

    WHO recognised mycetoma as a neglected tropical disease in 2016 (WHA69.21). It is endemic in the "mycetoma belt" between latitudes about 15°S and 30°N: Sudan (the highest burden), Senegal, Mauritania, Chad, Niger, Somalia, Yemen, India, Mexico (mainly actinomycetoma, Nocardia brasiliensis), Venezuela and Brazil. In the UK it is seen only in migrants or long-term residents from endemic areas, often years after exposure.

    02

    How it’s caused

    • Inoculation happens through thorn pricks, splinters or minor cuts, typically on the feet of barefoot rural workers such as farmers, herders and labourers (Case 5 is a 52-year-old farmer). Most patients are men aged 20–40, though any age can be affected.
    • There is no person-to-person spread and no known vector or animal reservoir. Incubation runs from months to years.
    • The organism forms grains (compact colonies in a matrix). These provoke suppurative and granulomatous inflammation with abscesses that track to the skin as sinuses, and also spread along fascial planes into muscle and bone (osteomyelitis, periosteal reaction, lytic lesions).
    • Actinomycetoma tends to be more inflammatory, spreads faster and invades bone earlier. Eumycetoma is slower, better encapsulated and more localised.
    • Grain colour hints at the cause: black = eumycetoma (Madurella, Trematosphaeria, Falciformispora); white/pale = eumycetoma (Scedosporium, Fusarium, Acremonium) or actinomycetoma (Nocardia, Actinomadura madurae); red/pink = Actinomadura pelletieri; yellow/brown = Streptomyces somaliensis
    03

    Clinical features

    • Site: about 70% on the foot (forefoot, dorsum, sole), then hand, leg and knee. The back, shoulder, head and neck can be affected in carriers of loads or sacks (often Nocardia). The chest wall and scalp are rare sites but dangerous.
    • Course: A small, painless, firm subcutaneous nodule, easily dismissed as a "boil" or "cyst"; Gradual swelling, then multiple nodules that soften and open as sinuses; Discharge of serous, serosanguinous or purulent fluid containing grains; Sinuses heal and new ones open (old sinuses scar); The skin becomes hyperpigmented, shiny and stretched
    • Pain is mild or absent until bone involvement or secondary bacterial infection. Lymph nodes are usually not involved, except sometimes with actinomycetoma.
    • The patient is typically systemically well (fever is rare unless there is secondary infection), which helps explain the late presentation.
    • Late features: massive swelling and deformity, bone destruction, joint ankylosis, disuse atrophy, chronic lymphoedema, disability, sometimes amputation, and rarely death from cranial, spinal or thoracic spread or sepsis.
    04

    Diagnosis

    • Clinical triad + endemic exposure. Look for grains in the discharge (shake gauze in saline; dermoscopy may show grains).
    • Grains and tissue: collect by deep surgical biopsy or FNA (not superficial swabs) for: direct microscopy (KOH: fine filaments under 1 µm = actinomycete; broad septate hyphae 2–5 µm = fungus); Gram/PAS/Grocott stains; culture (slow, up to 6 weeks; tell the lab); histology (grains in abscesses surrounded by neutrophils, then granuloma; Splendore–Hoeppli material); PCR/sequencing for species ID, now standard at reference centres
    • Imaging: Plain X-ray: soft-tissue mass, periosteal reaction, lytic "moth-eaten" bone, cortical thinning; Ultrasound: grains as bright hyper-reflective echoes in cavities (helps tell eumycetoma from actinomycetoma); MRI: extent and bone involvement, with the characteristic "dot-in-circle" sign; CT: bony involvement
    • Baseline before long antimicrobial courses: FBC, LFTs, U&E, glucose and HIV status.
    05

    Differentials

    Infective mimics of mycetoma:

    DifferentialDistinguishing points
    BotryomycosisGrain-forming S. aureus or Gram-negatives (looks like actinomycetoma); responds to standard antibiotics
    Chronic osteomyelitisSinus over bone, prior fracture, metalwork or diabetic foot; no grains
    Actinomycosis (Actinomyces israelii)Anaerobic; cervicofacial/dental, thoracic or abdominal; "sulphur granules"
    Cutaneous TB (scrofuloderma), non-tuberculous mycobacteriaOverlying lymph nodes or bone; AFB; chronic undermined sinuses
    Chromoblastomycosis, sporotrichosis, other deep fungiVerrucous plaques (chromo) or lymphangitic nodules (sporo); no sinuses with grains
    Leprosy, tertiary syphilis/yaws, leishmaniasisNeuropathy (leprosy); gummas
    Dermatophyte pseudomycetomaUsually scalp; immunosuppressed

    Non-infective mimics:

    • Podoconiosis (non-filarial "mossy foot" from irritant volcanic soil) or lymphatic filariasis/lymphoedema: swelling without sinuses or grains
    • Kaposi sarcoma, soft-tissue/bone sarcoma, SCC in a chronic sinus (Marjolin ulcer), foreign-body granuloma

    Causes of discharging sinuses in general (tutor follow-up):

    • Skin follicular/inflammatory: hidradenitis suppurativa, acne conglobata, dissecting cellulitis of the scalp (the follicular occlusion tetrad, with pilonidal sinus), pyoderma vegetans, Crohn's disease (perianal/cutaneous fistulae)
    • Dental (odontogenic): chronic dental abscess draining on the chin or jaw skin, often misdiagnosed as a skin lesion
    • Infective: osteomyelitis, mycetoma, actinomycosis, botryomycosis, scrofuloderma/TB, NTM, chronic furuncles/carbuncles
    • Other: foreign body, post-surgical (suture/mesh), congenital (preauricular sinus, branchial cleft, thyroglossal duct), malignancy (carcinoma breaking down)
    06

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    Pharmacist / IP

    Primary-care role: recognise, avoid inappropriate repeated antibiotic courses or incision, and refer.

    • Refer every suspected case to Infectious Diseases / tropical medicine (e.g. HTD London, which runs a fungal infections service, or a regional ID unit), working jointly with orthopaedic/plastic surgery and dermatology. Mark it urgent if there is spread toward the trunk or scalp, rapid progression, or secondary sepsis.
    • Do not perform simple incision and drainage or partial excision in primary care. Inadequate excision causes recurrence and spread. Do not label the condition "recurrent cellulitis/abscess".
    • Interim: treat acute secondary bacterial infection per NICE cellulitis guidance, keep wounds clean and dressed, give simple analgesia and limb elevation, check tetanus status, and offer footwear advice.
    • Specialist treatment: Actinomycetoma (bacterial): usually curable medically. Prolonged combination antibiotics for months, e.g. co-trimoxazole + amikacin cycles (the "Welsh regimen"). Alternatives include co-amoxiclav, dapsone, rifampicin, doxycycline/minocycline, linezolid or carbapenems depending on species and severity. Surgery is rarely needed; Eumycetoma (fungal): prolonged oral itraconazole (e.g. 200 mg twice daily for at least 6–12 months, often longer). This shrinks and encapsulates the lesion, which is then followed by wide surgical excision and continued antifungal. Alternatives include voriconazole, posaconazole and terbinafine (ketoconazole is no longer used systemically in the UK because of hepatotoxicity). Recurrence is common and cure rates are modest. Advanced disease may need amputation. Fosravuconazole is under evaluation (DNDi/Sudan trial)
    07

    Red flags & referral

    Escalate
    • Bone pain, deformity or rapid spread: urgent specialist imaging and referral.
    • Do not treat repeatedly with short courses of antibiotics without a diagnosis.
    08

    Prescriber pearls

    • The farmer with a foot that keeps "discharging" despite antibiotics: think mycetoma, chronic osteomyelitis or TB. Ask where they grew up and worked, and whether they went barefoot.
    • Itraconazole (if co-managing): Capsules need food and stomach acid, so avoid PPIs/H2 blockers/antacids around the dose or use the oral solution (empty stomach). Formulations are not interchangeable; It is a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor. Many interactions, some contraindicated: simvastatin, some DOACs, certain calcium-channel blockers, colchicine in renal/hepatic impairment, some antiarrhythmics, ergot alkaloids, midazolam; Monitor LFTs. It is a negative inotrope, so avoid in heart failure. Take TDM levels if available
    • Co-trimoxazole for months: FBC (folate antagonism, marrow), potassium and renal function, rash (SJS/TEN warning); Avoid with methotrexate; interacts with warfarin and phenytoin; Folic acid supplementation per specialist
    • Amikacin: therapeutic drug monitoring, renal function, and audiometry (ototoxicity is cumulative over cycles).
    • Adherence: therapy lasts months to years. Explain that stopping early = relapse, and that swelling and scarring may persist after cure. Arrange physiotherapy.
    • Prevention: wear shoes and protective clothing during farming and herding, clean and dress minor wounds promptly, and seek early care for any slowly enlarging painless lump on the foot.
