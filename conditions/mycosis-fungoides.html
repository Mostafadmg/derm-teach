    Lumps, lesions & skin cancer

    Mycosis fungoides & cutaneous lymphoma

    The commonest cutaneous lymphoma: fixed, slowly changing eczema- or psoriasis-like patches in sun-protected sites that do not settle with treatment. Most patients have early-stage disease with a normal life expectancy.

    Module 2 · Week 3 · 5 images

    Also coversMFCTCLcutaneous T-cell lymphomaSézary syndromeparapsoriasispatch-stagebathing-trunk distribution
    Think of it when

    Persistent, scaly, salmon-pink patches or thin plaques on the buttocks, hips, trunk or breasts that ‘never quite clear’ despite steroids.

    What drives it

    Skin-homing clonal helper T cells; progresses slowly from patch to plaque to tumour; Sézary syndrome is the leukaemic form with erythroderma.

    Backbone of care

    Suspect, stop repeated empirical treatment, refer for multiple biopsies; specialist skin-directed therapy (steroids, phototherapy) for early disease.

    Never miss

    Tumour-stage disease, erythroderma with lymph nodes (Sézary), misdiagnosis as eczema or psoriasis for years.

    01

    What it is

    MF is the commonest primary cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (CTCL), about half of all primary cutaneous lymphomas. It is usually indolent over years to decades, with a minority progressing. Sézary syndrome is the leukaemic, erythrodermic variant.

    02

    How it’s caused

    Clonal skin-homing CD4+ memory T-cells (CD4+, often CD7- and CD26-). Cause unknown; links to chronic antigen stimulation have been proposed. Pautrier microabscesses (intraepidermal collections of atypical lymphocytes) and epidermotropism without spongiosis are the histological hallmark, but they are absent in many early biopsies.

    03

    Clinical features

    • Patch stage: persistent, variably shaped, often finely wrinkled ("cigarette-paper") pink-brown patches in sun-protected "bathing-trunk" sites (buttocks, hips, breasts, inner thighs). Itchy or asymptomatic, steroid-resistant or relapsing.
    • Plaque stage: infiltrated, scaly, annular plaques.
    • Tumour stage: nodules that may ulcerate.
    • Erythroderma, poikiloderma (atrophy, mottled pigmentation, telangiectasia), and variants (folliculotropic, hypopigmented in skin of colour and children, granulomatous slack skin, pagetoid reticulosis).
    04

    Diagnosis

    • Multiple biopsies (ideally after a 2–4-week washout of topical steroids) with immunohistochemistry and TCR gene rearrangement (clonality). Diagnosis often takes years.
    • ISCL/EORTC algorithm for early MF. Diagnosis needs ≥4 points: Clinical: 2 points for the basic criterion (persistent and/or progressive patches/plaques) plus 2 of the additional criteria (non-sun-exposed distribution; size or shape variation; poikiloderma), or 1 point for the basic criterion plus 1 additional; Histopathology: 2 points for a superficial lymphoid infiltrate plus both epidermotropism without spongiosis and lymphoid atypia, or 1 point for the infiltrate plus one of those; Molecular: clonal TCR rearrangement = 1 point; Immunopathology: CD2/3/5 <50% of T-cells, CD7 <10%, or epidermal/dermal discordance = 1 point
    • Staging (ISCL/EORTC TNMB):
    StageSkin (T)Notes
    IAT1: patches/plaques <10% BSALife expectancy similar to the general population
    IBT2: ≥10% BSA
    IIAT1–2 with dermatopathic (N1–2) nodes
    IIBT3: ≥1 tumour (≥1 cm)"Advanced" begins at IIB
    IIIT4: erythroderma (≥80% BSA)
    IVA–IVBBlood B2 (Sézary), N3 nodes, or visceral (M1)
    • Staging investigations (specialist): FBC + blood film/flow cytometry for Sézary cells, LDH, LFTs, HTLV-1 serology, and CT (or PET-CT) for stage ≥IIA or as indicated. Biopsy suspicious nodes.
    05

    Differentials

    Chronic eczema (including occupational contact dermatitis, relevant to a mechanic; patch test), psoriasis, tinea corporis (scrape), parapsoriasis (small- and large-plaque, where large-plaque can progress), pityriasis lichenoides, drug-induced pseudolymphoma / MF-like drug eruptions (reported with, among others, anticonvulsants, ACE inhibitors/ARBs, amlodipine and other calcium-channel blockers, and dupilumab-associated CTCL unmasking), lichen planus, cutaneous lupus.

    06

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    Pharmacist / IP

    IP role

    • A rash not responding to an appropriate potent topical steroid over several weeks, especially in sun-protected sites, should be referred for biopsy. Do not repeat or escalate steroids indefinitely, as happened for 12 months in Case 5. Refer routinely to urgently depending on stage clues (tumours, erythroderma, lymphadenopathy, systemic symptoms: urgent). Primary cutaneous lymphoma is managed in specialist supra-network skin lymphoma MDTs.
    • Review drug triggers. Stopping a suspected culprit (e.g. amlodipine) and reviewing after 6–12 weeks may be appropriate before or alongside biopsy. This is a joint decision with the GP/dermatologist.
    • Case 5 medicines: canagliflozin (SGLT2 inhibitor) has no link to MF but carries genital mycotic infection and DKA risk (check the patient's wellbeing). Metformin: none. Simvastatin + amlodipine: the BNF/MHRA cap simvastatin at 20 mg with amlodipine (CYP3A4 interaction), so check the dose.

    Specialist treatment (BAD/UKCLG Gilson 2019)

    • Early (IA–IIA): skin-directed therapy. Potent/very potent topical steroid; phototherapy (narrowband UVB for patches, PUVA for thicker plaques; the course answer); topical chlormethine gel (Ledaga®); localised radiotherapy; topical bexarotene (not UK-licensed); and for many IA patients, "expectant" skin-directed care.
    • Advanced (IIB–IV): Total-skin electron beam therapy; Systemic bexarotene (hypertriglyceridaemia and central hypothyroidism; needs a lipid-lowering agent and levothyroxine; avoid gemfibrozil); Low-dose methotrexate; Interferon-α; Mogamulizumab (anti-CCR4; NICE TA754: Sézary after ≥1 systemic therapy, MF stage IIB+ after ≥2); Brentuximab vedotin for CD30+ (NICE TA577); Extracorporeal photopheresis (Sézary); Chemotherapy; Allogeneic stem-cell transplant in selected patients
    • Quality of life: itch (emollients, antihistamines, gabapentin/pregabalin, mirtazapine, aprepitant for refractory itch), and work and psychological impact.

    07

    Red flags & referral

    Escalate
    • Fixed patches or plaques in sun-protected sites that fail to respond to treatment over months: refer for biopsy (MF).
    • Erythroderma with lymph nodes, intense itch and weight loss: urgent referral (Sézary syndrome).
    08

    Prescriber pearls

    • Explain that early MF is usually a chronic, indolent condition (stage IA has near-normal life expectancy). Avoid the "advanced cancer" framing without staging.
    • Cumulative UV raises skin cancer risk.
    • Dupilumab caveat: CTCL has been unmasked or accelerated in patients misdiagnosed with atopic dermatitis. In an atypical adult-onset "eczema" that is failing treatment, biopsy before biologics.
