    Napkin (nappy) dermatitis

    Irritant rash on the convex skin with the folds spared, or Candida with fold involvement and satellites. Use barrier ointment at every change, with short-course hydrocortisone and/or an azole.

    Also callednappy rashdiaper rashnapkin dermatitiscandidal nappy rashirritant napkin dermatitisbarrier cream+1
    Related pagesCandidal intertrigoEczema / dermatitisPsoriasisNeonatal pustular rashes

    Key facts

    • Inflammation of the skin under a nappy, most often irritant contact dermatitis from urine, faeces and occlusion.
    • Peaks at 9–12 months (range 3–15 months); much less common with modern breathable disposables.
    • Irritant: convex surfaces affected, skin creases spared; Candida: creases involved with satellite papules.
    • Frequent changes, nappy-free time, gentle cleansing and a barrier preparation; topical imidazole if candidal; hydrocortisone 1% short term if inflamed.
    • Refer if atypical, nodular, ulcerated or not responding (e.g. psoriasis, Langerhans cell histiocytosis, zinc deficiency).

    Overview

    Inflammation in the nappy area, most often irritant contact dermatitis from occlusion, urine and faecal enzymes. Secondary Candida is common. Peak age is about 3–15 months. It also affects older children and adults with incontinence.

    Epidemiology

    • Who
      • Up to a third of babies in nappies at any time; peak 9–12 months.
      • Also affects incontinent older children and adults (incontinence-associated dermatitis).

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    • Occlusion and urine cause over-hydration and maceration.
    • Faecal enzymes and bile salts damage the barrier, and ammonia raises the pH.
    • Friction, and an atopic or seborrhoeic tendency.
    • Candida albicans thrives on damaged, moist skin.
    • It is not usually an allergy to the nappy, and it is not tinea.

    Risk factors and associations

    • Prolonged contact with urine and faeces; diarrhoea (faecal enzymes) and antibiotics (Candida) are common triggers.
    • Infrequent changes, tight nappies, harsh wipes and soaps.

    Myths to address

    • "Leave the cream thick and never wipe it off": a barrier layer is fine, but clean skin gently at each change.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    Wet, occluded skin with a high pH lets faecal enzymes break down the barrier.

    1. 1

      Overhydration

      Occlusion keeps the stratum corneum wet, making it fragile and more permeable.

    2. 2

      Alkaline pH and enzymes

      Bacteria convert urine urea to ammonia, raising pH, which activates faecal proteases and lipases that digest the skin barrier.

    3. 3

      Irritant dermatitis

      Friction and enzymes inflame the convex skin in contact with the nappy; creases are protected.

    4. 4

      Secondary Candida

      After about 3 days, Candida from the gut often colonises damaged skin, spreading into the folds.

    Histopathology

    • Not needed; diagnosis is clinical. Swab satellite pustules if unsure or recurrent.

    Clinical features

    PatternFeatures
    Irritant napkin dermatitisRedness ± scale or erosions on convex surfaces in contact with the nappy; folds often spared
    CandidalBright red, shiny plaques involving the folds, with satellite papules and pustules
    ImpetigoIrregular blisters, pustules or honey crust
    SeborrhoeicSalmon-pink patches in folds ± cradle cap
    Psoriasis (‘napkin psoriasis’)Persistent, well-demarcated, shiny plaques
    Atopic eczemaItchy rash elsewhere; nappy area often relatively spared

    Special groups

    • Skin of colour
      • Redness is less obvious; look for scaling, erosion and post-inflammatory pigmentation.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    Clinical. Swab if severe, atypical or not responding. In refractory cases, consider rarities such as zinc deficiency (acrodermatitis enteropathica) or Langerhans cell histiocytosis, which need specialist input.

    Clinical

    • Diagnosis is clinical.
    • Check the folds and look for satellites.

    Swab

    • If severe, atypical or not responding.

    Think rare

    • Refractory: zinc deficiency (acrodermatitis enteropathica) or Langerhans cell histiocytosis: specialist.

    Safeguarding

    • Injury pattern that does not fit dermatitis: document and escalate locally.

    Management

    Aligned with NICE CKS nappy rash and PCDS. Re-check the live CKS page and the BNF for Children.

    Barrier and self-care

    • Change nappies frequently, with nappy-free time when practical.
    • Cleanse gently with water or a non-soap cleanser, and pat dry.
    • Apply a thick barrier ointment (petrolatum ± zinc oxide) at every change.
    • Use modern absorbent disposables. With cloth nappies, use liners and avoid plastic pants. Avoid fragranced wipes.

    Topical prescribing

    • Inflamed irritant rash: hydrocortisone 1%, thin layer, for a short course (usually up to 7 days).
    • Suspected Candida: a topical imidazole (clotrimazole, miconazole, econazole), continued after the skin looks clear (often 7–14 days in total).
    • Combination steroid and antifungal products: short courses only. No potent steroids on nappy skin.
    • Secondary bacterial infection: follow local guidance (oral flucloxacillin if needed; topical fusidic acid only for very localised areas).

    Caution

    Prescribing limits and pitfalls

    • Potent steroids: Not for the nappy area in primary care.
    • How much barrier?: Parents often use too little: show them.

    Referral

    • Same day

      Infant under 1 month with significant rash, or a systemically unwell infant.

    • Urgent

      Bruising or a pattern suggesting non-accidental injury: safeguarding.

    • Routine

      Severe or ulcerated rash, or no improvement with good care and treatment.

    • Routine

      Diagnostic doubt, or suspected nutritional deficiency or rare disease.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Infant under 1 month with significant rash, or a systemically unwell infant.
    • Severe or ulcerated rash, or failure to improve with good barrier care and appropriate treatment.
    • Diagnostic uncertainty, or suspected nutritional deficiency or rare disease.
    • Bruising or a pattern suggesting non-accidental injury.

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Secondary Candida or bacterial (impetigo) infection.
    • Erosive Jacquet dermatitis and granuloma gluteale infantum (steroid misuse).
    • Distress and sleep disturbance.

    Prognosis

    • Usually settles within days with good nappy care; recurs with diarrhoea or infrequent changes.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • Folds involved with satellites: treat Candida. Folds spared: irritant pattern.

    • Parents often use too little barrier ointment, so show them how much.

    • A short course of mild hydrocortisone is appropriate. Potent steroids are not for the nappy area in primary care.

    • Breastfed babies’ stools are often less alkaline, which may reduce the irritant load (supportive counselling, not a treatment claim).

    Self-check quiz

    5 questions · optional
    1. Q1Folds spared or involved: which suggests Candida?Reveal

      Involved folds with satellite papules/pustules suggest Candida; spared folds suggest irritant.

    2. Q2What goes on at every nappy change?Reveal

      A thick barrier ointment (petrolatum ± zinc oxide).

    3. Q3Steroid choice and duration for inflamed irritant nappy rash?Reveal

      Hydrocortisone 1%, thin layer, short course (usually up to 7 days). No potent steroids.

    4. Q4Rash refractory to good care: which rarities?Reveal

      Zinc deficiency (acrodermatitis enteropathica) and Langerhans cell histiocytosis: specialist input.

    5. Q5Which infants need same-day assessment?Reveal

      Under 1 month with significant rash, or systemically unwell.
