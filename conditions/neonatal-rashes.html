    Neonatal pustular rashes

    Erythema toxicum, TNPM and miliaria are benign and need reassurance. Neonatal HSV is the one you must never ‘wait and see’ on. Includes a one-table differential.

    Also callederythema toxicum neonatorumETNtoxic erythema of the newborntransient neonatal pustular melanosisTNPMmiliaria+7
    Related pagesNeonatal & infantile acneMiliaNapkin (nappy) dermatitisInfantile haemangioma (strawberry mark)Herpes simplex labialis (cold sores)

    Key facts

    • Most neonatal rashes are benign and transient: erythema toxicum neonatorum (ETN), transient neonatal pustular melanosis (TNPM), miliaria, milia and neonatal acne.
    • ETN: blotchy red macules with central pustules in days 1–3; spares palms and soles; eosinophils.
    • TNPM: pustules at birth that rupture to leave collarettes and brown macules, more in darker skin.
    • Miliaria: blocked sweat ducts from overheating; clear vesicles (crystallina) or red papules (rubra).
    • Red flags: unwell baby, vesicles (HSV: emergency aciclovir), purpura, blistering, widespread pustules with fever.

    Overview

    RashIn one line
    Erythema toxicum neonatorum (ETN)Common, benign, self-limiting rash in up to about half of full-term babies; uncommon in premature babies
    Transient neonatal pustular melanosis (TNPM)Uncommon benign pustular rash present at birth, more common in darker skin; leaves pigmented macules
    Miliaria (heat rash)Blocked or inflamed eccrine sweat ducts; common with immature ducts, heat and overbundling

    Epidemiology

    • Who
      • ETN affects up to half of term babies; TNPM about 5% of Black infants; miliaria in hot, overwrapped babies.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    • ETN: the exact cause is unknown (theories include a reaction to mechanical or thermal stimuli, or a transient immune response). Pustules are sterile and eosinophil-rich. It is not infection and not poor hygiene.
    • TNPM: cause unknown, possibly a ‘precocious’ form of ETN. Pustules are sterile and neutrophil-rich.
    • Miliaria: the level of sweat-duct blockage determines the type (below).
    Miliaria typeLevel of blockClinical gist
    CrystallinaStratum corneumClear ‘dewdrop’ vesicles, no inflammation
    RubraMid-epidermisItchy red papules or papulovesicles
    PustulosaVariant of rubraPustules
    ProfundaDermo-epidermal junction or deeperRare; flesh-coloured deep papules (mainly adults)

    Myths to address

    • "Spots in a newborn mean infection": most are harmless, but vesicles or an unwell baby need urgent review.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    Newborn skin is adapting to air, bacteria and temperature regulation.

    1. 1

      Immune adaptation

      ETN reflects an innate immune response to colonising bacteria at hair follicles (eosinophils).

    2. 2

      Sweat duct immaturity

      Immature ducts block easily, causing miliaria when overheated.

    3. 3

      Maternal hormones

      Maternal androgens stimulate sebaceous glands (neonatal acne, sebaceous hyperplasia).

    Clinical features

    Erythema toxicum neonatorum

    • Onset in the first few days of life (up to about 2 weeks).
    • Blotchy red macules, papules and small white or yellow pustules (a ‘flea-bitten’ look). Lesions wax and wane, each lasting under 24 hours.
    • Face, trunk and proximal limbs. Palms and soles spared.
    • The baby is well.

    TNPM

    • Present at birth.
    • Pustules on a non-red base rupture to leave a collarette of scale and hyperpigmented macules, which fade over weeks to months.
    • Can involve the forehead, neck, trunk, limbs, palms and soles.

    Miliaria

    • Crystallina: 1–2 mm clear fragile vesicles on the head, neck and upper trunk, often in the first week.
    • Rubra: itchy red non-follicular papules on the trunk and in skin folds.
    • Triggered by heat, fever, synthetic or tight clothing, and occlusion.

    Special groups

    • Neonatal HSV
      • Grouped vesicles, especially on the scalp or presenting part, with or without fever: emergency IV aciclovir.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    • Clinical in a well baby with typical morphology.
    • If uncertain: a smear of pustule contents (eosinophils in ETN, neutrophils in TNPM), Gram stain and culture, and HSV PCR where HSV is possible.
    • Biopsy is rarely needed.

    Management

    ETN and TNPM

    • Reassure: benign and self-limiting. ETN usually clears within days to about 2 weeks and may briefly recur. TNPM pigment fades over weeks to months.
    • No specific treatment. Do not pick the pustules. Use gentle cleansing and avoid unnecessary antiseptics.
    • Do not treat as infection if the diagnosis is clear and the baby is well.

    Miliaria

    • Cool the baby: fewer layers, a cooler room, breathable cotton, and prompt removal of wet clothing.
    • Cool baths or compresses. For itch, short-term calamine or a light emollient. A short course of mild topical steroid is sometimes used for miliaria rubra on suitable sites.
    • Usually clears within 1–2 days once cool.

    Referral

    • 999 / A&E

      Any neonate with vesicles who is febrile, lethargic, poorly feeding, or has eye involvement: same-day emergency pathway. Never ‘wait and see’ for possible HSV.

    • Routine

      Clustered or grouped vesicles, mucosal or eye involvement.

    • Routine

      A premature or unwell infant, heavy involvement of the palms and soles (outside typical TNPM), or diagnostic doubt.

    • Don’t

      Fever plus rash: do not assume miliaria.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Any neonate with vesicles who is febrile, lethargic, poorly feeding, or has eye involvement: same-day emergency pathway. Never ‘wait and see’ for possible HSV.
    • Clustered or grouped vesicles, mucosal or eye involvement.
    • A premature or unwell infant, heavy involvement of the palms and soles (outside typical TNPM), or diagnostic doubt.
    • Fever plus rash: do not assume miliaria.

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Rarely, misdiagnosis of infection; miliaria pustulosa can become infected.

    Prognosis

    • ETN and TNPM resolve within days to weeks (TNPM pigment within months).

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • Parents often fear allergy or sepsis. Explain that ETN pustules are sterile and the baby should stay well.

    • Document ‘well neonate with typical ETN’ carefully, and keep a low threshold to investigate anything atypical.

    • Post-TNPM pigmented macules can be mistaken for bruises or Mongolian spots. The history of preceding pustules helps.

    • Skin of colour: ETN erythema is easily missed, so look and feel for papules and pustules.

    • Overbundling advice is the main ‘prescription’ for miliaria.

    Self-check quiz

    5 questions · optional
    1. Q1When should you think of Neonatal pustular rashes?Reveal

      A well term baby with blotchy red macules and small pustules (ETN), pustules present at birth on a non-red base (TNPM), or dewdrop vesicles or red fold papules with overheating (miliaria).

    2. Q2What drives it?Reveal

      ETN: sterile eosinophilic pustules. TNPM: sterile neutrophilic pustules. Miliaria: blocked eccrine sweat ducts.

    3. Q3What is the backbone of care?Reveal

      Reassure, give no specific treatment, cool the baby for miliaria, and document that the baby is well.

    4. Q4What must you never miss?Reveal

      Neonatal HSV: grouped vesicles, or an unwell, febrile or poorly feeding baby. Same-day emergency pathway.

    5. Q5Name a red flag that needs escalation.Reveal

      Any neonate with vesicles who is febrile, lethargic, poorly feeding, or has eye involvement: same-day emergency pathway. Never ‘wait and see’ for possible HSV.
