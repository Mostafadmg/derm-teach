    Fungal nail infection & nail discolouration

    Thick, discoloured, crumbly nails are often fungal but not always: trauma, psoriasis and onycholysis look the same. Confirm with clippings before committing to months of oral terbinafine.

    Also calledonychomycosisfungal nailtinea unguiumterbinafineamorolfineonycholysis+4
    Related pagesTinea: corporis, cruris & pedisNail signs: psoriatic nails, Beau’s lines & clubbingParonychia (acute and chronic)Psoriasis

    Key facts

    • Fungal infection of the nail: dermatophytes (over 75%, mostly T. rubrum), moulds or Candida.
    • Affects about 10% of adults, rising to 20% over 60 and higher in diabetes; toenails far more than fingernails.
    • Thickened, discoloured (white or yellow), crumbly nail with onycholysis and subungual debris.
    • Confirm with nail clippings (microscopy and culture) before oral treatment; about half of abnormal nails are not fungal.
    • Oral terbinafine is first line for dermatophyte infection (CKS); amorolfine lacquer for limited distal disease.

    Overview

    Onychomycosis = fungal infection of the nail plate/bed. Dermatophyte infection is also called tinea unguium. It accounts for a large share of all nail disease presentations in primary care.

    Epidemiology

    • Prevalence
      • About 10% overall; up to 20% over 60 and 50% over 70; rare in children.
    • Risk groups
      • Older adults, diabetes, peripheral vascular disease, immunosuppression, psoriasis, athletes and communal-shower users.
      • Often coexists with tinea pedis.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    Organisms & patterns

    CategoryTypical organismsClinical clues
    Dermatophyte (~majority)T. rubrum, T. interdigitale, othersToenails ≫ fingernails; often with tinea pedis/manuum; distal lateral subungual (DLSO) commonest
    Yeast (Candida)C. albicans ± non-albicansFingernails; chronic paronychia first; proximal change near cuticle; wet work
    Non-dermatophyte mouldsScopulariopsis, Fusarium, Aspergillus, etc.Similar to dermatophyte; culture/PCR needed; treatment harder
    Trauma / non-fungal dystrophyN/ASingle nail; footwear/sport; negative mycology; ± subungual haematoma that grows out

    Clinical subtypes (know the names): DLSO; white superficial (WSO); proximal subungual (PSO — consider immunosuppression/HIV); endonyx; total dystrophic; mixed.

    Risk factors and associations

    • Organisms: dermatophytes (T. rubrum, T. interdigitale), non-dermatophyte moulds (about 10%; Scopulariopsis, Fusarium, Aspergillus), yeasts (fingernails with chronic paronychia).
    • Predisposing: nail trauma, occlusive footwear, hyperhidrosis, smoking, genetic susceptibility; biofilm makes cure harder.

    Myths to address

    • "It's just cosmetic": it can cause pain, mobility problems and cellulitis risk in diabetes.
    • "Every thick yellow nail is fungal": trauma, psoriasis and ageing nails mimic it, so confirm first.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    Fungi invade the nail bed and plate keratin, usually starting under the free edge.

    1. 1

      Entry

      Fungi from tinea pedis or the environment invade under the free edge (distal), from the surface (white superficial) or from the cuticle (proximal).

    2. 2

      Keratin digestion

      Keratinases digest nail-bed and plate keratin; the bed responds with subungual hyperkeratosis, lifting the plate (onycholysis).

    3. 3

      Spread and dystrophy

      Infection spreads proximally towards the matrix, causing thick, crumbly, discoloured nails; longitudinal 'spikes' and dermatophytomas (fungal balls) resist treatment.

    4. 4

      Reservoir

      The infected nail reseeds the feet and groin, causing recurrent tinea.

    Histopathology

    • Fungal hyphae in the nail plate on PAS-stained nail clipping (sensitive test), with parakeratosis and neutrophils in the bed.
    • Sample correctly: clip the affected nail back as far as possible and scrape subungual debris.
    • See the sampling diagram in Diagnosis.

    Clinical features

    • Distal and lateral subungual (commonest): yellow-white thickening spreading back from the free edge, with crumbly debris under the nail and lifting (onycholysis).
    • White superficial: chalky white patches on the nail surface, usually toenails.
    • Proximal subungual: white change starting near the cuticle; think of immunosuppression, including HIV.
    • Candidal: fingernails, usually after chronic paronychia, with nail-fold swelling and onycholysis.
    • Usually several toenails, often with scaly soles or toe webs (tinea pedis). A single dystrophic nail after injury is more often trauma.

    Types to recognise

    • DLSO

      Commonest dermatophyte pattern. Site: Toenail.

    • White superficial

      Chalky white surface. Site: Toenail.

    • Subungual haematoma

      Trauma mimic; grows out. Site: Toenail.

    • Candidal nail

      Onycholysis after paronychia. Site: Fingernail.

    Special groups

    • Diabetes and peripheral arterial disease
      • Treat more actively and refer to podiatry; thick nails cause pressure ulcers.
    • Children
      • Rare; check parents and siblings for tinea pedis.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    • Examine all nails and feet/hands/groin for associated tinea.
    • Mycology before long systemic therapy (NICE/CKS): clip crumbling free edge + scrape most proximal accessible subungual debris; KOH microscopy + culture (± PCR where available).
    • Dermoscopy may support fungal vs traumatic onycholysis but does not replace mycology when oral therapy is planned.

    Nail sampling technique

    • Clean the nail with an alcohol wipe, and stop topical antifungals 2–4 weeks before (and oral ones longer, if feasible).
    • Distal/lateral subungual pattern: clip back to the most proximal affected nail and collect subungual debris with a curette. Superficial white pattern: scrape the surface. Proximal subungual pattern: punch through the plate, or send a nail biopsy (specialist).
    • Send generous full-thickness clippings + debris in a folded dark paper packet (e.g. a Dermapak), not a sealed plastic pot, because that encourages bacterial overgrowth.
    • Yield (approximate): KOH microscopy ~50–60% sensitive. A negative result does not exclude disease; Culture takes up to 2–4 weeks and has a significant false-negative rate; Histology with PAS stain of clippings is the most sensitive (up to ~90–95%) but doesn't give the species; PCR is quick and species-specific where the lab offers it
    • Repeat sampling if negative but clinically suspicious, before starting 3–6 months of terbinafine/itraconazole (CKS).

    Differential diagnoses

    PhotoConditionDistinguishing features
    		Trauma / gait dystrophyAvoid months of needless terbinafine.
    		Nail psoriasisPits, oil-drop patches, skin or scalp signs.
    		Pseudomonas green nailGreen-black, onycholytic, wet work: dry, antiseptic soaks.

    Management

    Management sketch for IP

    • Mild distal disease (<50% plate, few nails, no matrix): consider topical lacquer (e.g. amorolfine) per BNF — expect long courses and modest cure rates.
    • More extensive dermatophyte disease: oral terbinafine often preferred (fingernails shorter course than toenails — check BNF); alternatives itraconazole/fluconazole in selected cases.
    • Candida-predominant: prefer azoles (itraconazole/fluconazole), not terbinafine first-line.
    • Always counsel duration, LFTs/interaction checks, and that “cure” waits for nail outgrowth.
    • Relapse common if footwear/hygiene/diabetes not addressed.

    Practical prescribing points

    • Treatment is optional for mild, asymptomatic toenail disease in a healthy adult: discuss the long course, the cure rate (complete cure is much lower than mycological cure) and recurrence.
    • Oral terbinafine for confirmed dermatophyte infection: fingernails 6 weeks, toenails 3–6 months (BNF). Ask about liver disease; counsel to report jaundice, dark urine, severe tiredness, rash or taste disturbance.
    • Oral itraconazole (pulsed) for Candida or non-dermatophyte moulds, or if terbinafine is unsuitable: check interactions (CYP3A4) and heart failure history.
    • Amorolfine 5% lacquer for superficial or distal disease involving under about half of one or two nails; file the nail first and use weekly for 6–12 months.
    • Treat co-existing tinea pedis, and advise on footwear, drying between toes and not sharing nail tools.

    Treatment ladder

    1. 1

      Prevent relapse

      Footwear, foot hygiene, treat tinea pedis, diabetes control.

    2. 2

      Limited distal

      Amorolfine 5% lacquer

    3. 3

      Extensive dermatophyte

      Oral terbinafine

    4. 4

      Candida / moulds

      Oral itraconazole (pulsed) or fluconazole; check CYP3A4 interactions and heart failure.

    Referral

    • Urgent

      Pigment that doesn’t grow out, a widening band, or nail-fold pigment: possible melanoma (NG12).

    • Urgent

      Diabetic foot with nail disease and skin breaks: foot-protection team.

    • Routine

      Stop terbinafine if hepatitis symptoms or severe skin reaction.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Pigment that does not grow out, a widening band or pigment on the nail fold: refer as possible melanoma (NG12).
    • Diabetic foot with nail disease and skin breaks: foot-protection team.
    • Check liver function history and interactions before oral terbinafine; stop if hepatitis symptoms or severe skin reactions.

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Pain, difficulty walking and footwear problems.
    • Secondary bacterial infection and cellulitis (especially diabetes).
    • Recurrent tinea pedis and cruris (reservoir).
    • Psychosocial distress.

    Prognosis

    • Oral terbinafine gives the best cure rates, but nails take 6 months (fingers) to 12–18 months (toes) to grow out.
    • About 20–25% relapse after treatment.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • Get mycology before a long oral course: many thick nails are trauma or psoriasis.

    • The nail looks abnormal for months after cure, because it grows out slowly (toenails 12–18 months). Set expectations at the start.

    • A negative result does not exclude infection, so repeat sampling if the picture is convincing.

    Self-check quiz

    4 questions · optional
    1. Q1Commonest pattern of onychomycosis?Reveal

    2. Q2Oral terbinafine duration for toenails?Reveal

      3–6 months (fingernails 6 weeks).

    3. Q3How should clippings be sent?Reveal

      Generous clippings + subungual debris in a folded dark paper packet.

    4. Q4White change starting at the cuticle?Reveal
