    Paronychia (acute & chronic) and ingrowing toenail

    Acute paronychia is a painful bacterial nail-fold infection; chronic paronychia is an irritant, wet-work inflammation of the nail fold with loss of the cuticle, often colonised by Candida. Ingrowing toenail is a common, related problem.

    Module 3 · Week 2 · 5 images

    Also coversparonychiawhitlownail fold infectionchronic paronychiacandida paronychiawet workingrowing toenailonychocryptosisflucloxacillin
    Think of it when

    A red, swollen, tender nail fold: hours to days (acute) or weeks to months with a lost cuticle (chronic).

    What drives it

    Acute: S. aureus or streptococci after minor trauma. Chronic: wet work and irritants break the cuticle seal, with Candida as a passenger or driver.

    Backbone of care

    Acute: warm soaks, drain pus, antibiotic only if spreading (flucloxacillin). Chronic: keep hands dry, cotton-under-rubber gloves, potent topical steroid ± topical azole.

    Never miss

    Herpetic whitlow (do not incise), osteomyelitis or tendon sheath infection, and nail-unit melanoma or SCC presenting as ‘paronychia’.

    01

    What it is

    Paronychia = inflammation of the proximal and/or lateral nail folds. Acute (<~6 weeks) is usually bacterial (S. aureus, streptococci, sometimes Pseudomonas) or herpetic whitlow; chronic (>~6 weeks) is often related to irritant dermatitis of the nail fold plus secondary Candida (and sometimes Gram-negative bacteria).

    Candidal nail disease usually follows chronic paronychia: yeast involves the fold first, then may spread to the nail plate (onychomycosis / onycholysis). Typical case: candidal onychomycosis with paronychia in a wet-work hairdresser with KOH yeast cells.

    02

    How it’s caused

    • Repeated wet work, cold hands, manicure trauma, cuticle picking, and irritants lift/lose the cuticle → gap between fold and plate → colonisation by Candida albicans (± bacteria).
    • Occupations: hairdressers, cleaners, bartenders, food handlers, dairy/fishing, home wet work.
    • Host factors: hand dermatitis, diabetes, immunosuppression, poor peripheral circulation.
    • Candida nail-plate infection is less common than dermatophyte onychomycosis overall, and is relatively more often fingernails than toenails. Relevance of a positive Candida culture must be judged clinically (Candida can secondarily colonise dystrophic nails from psoriasis/trauma).
    03

    Clinical features

    FeatureTypical findings
    Chronic paronychiaSwollen, tender, erythematous proximal (± lateral) folds; loss of cuticle; fold lifted off plate; intermittent white/yellow/green discharge
    Nail plateThickening, yellow/white/green/black discolouration, transverse ridging, onycholysis starting near the fold
    SitesFingers (thumbs common); often several digits over time; wet-work hands
    Case cluesHairdresser; 6 months yellow thumbnails; painful cuticles; yellow malodorous secretion; onycholysis; KOH yeast

    Acute bacterial paronychia: rapid hours–days, usually one digit, marked pain, possible abscess — different tempo from chronic Candida-associated disease.

    04

    Diagnosis

    • Clinical: chronicity, wet-work history, cuticle loss, multi-digit tendency, discharge.
    • KOH / microscopy ± culture of nail clippings and/or fold swab — yeast on KOH supports Candida; culture identifies species and co-pathogens.
    • Correlate lab with clinic: Candida in a dystrophic nail without fold disease may be secondary.
    • Consider bacterial swab if acute flare/abscess; viral swab if grouped vesicles (herpetic whitlow).
    05

    Look-alikeDistinguishing clues

    Herpetic whitlowGrouped vesicles; severe pain; HSV history/exposure

    Traumatic onycholysisHistory of injury/footwear; mycology often negative
    Drug-related paronychiaEGFR/BRAF inhibitors, retinoids — drug history
    06

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    Pharmacist / IP

    Non-drug (essential — often determines success):

    • Keep hands dry and warm; cotton-lined waterproof gloves for wet work; change when damp.
    • Avoid cuticle cutting/picking and harsh manicures.
    • Emollient barrier frequently; trim nails short.
    • Occupational advice for hairdressers: PPE, dry between clients, treat hand dermatitis.

    Drug therapy (align with BNF/local formulary; confirm organism when possible):

    • Chronic paronychia: treat dermatitis of the fold (short courses of topical corticosteroid ointment to the fold are often used in specialist pathways) plus topical antifungal/antiseptic solutions to the nail fold for weeks–months (e.g. imidazole solutions — check BNF).
    • Confirmed Candida nail/fold disease not settling: oral itraconazole or fluconazole (Candida-active). Itraconazole is usually preferred (broader spectrum vs Candida than terbinafine; fingernail regimens often ~6 weeks continuous or pulsed per product licence — verify current BNF/SmPC).
    • Terbinafine is first-line for many dermatophyte nail infections but is less reliable for Candida — do not default to it when yeast is the culprit.
    • Topical nail lacquers alone are often insufficient if matrix/proximal disease or significant fold infection.
    • Abscess: drainage pathway; antibiotics only when bacterial infection is clear.

    If no improvement after appropriate oral course: revisit adherence, ongoing wet exposure, alternative diagnoses (psoriasis, trauma), re-mycology, and dermatology referral.

    Acute (bacterial) paronychia

    • No abscess: warm water (± saline) soaks 3–4 times a day for 10–15 min, keep the finger dry between soaks, rest/elevate, and give analgesia. Most cases settle without antibiotics.
    • Abscess (fluctuant, pointing pus): incision and drainage (or lifting the eponychium with a blade) in primary care, minor injuries or urgent care. Partial nail removal if pus has tracked under the plate. Antibiotics are often not needed after adequate drainage.
    • Antibiotics only if there is spreading cellulitis, the patient is systemically unwell, or they are immunocompromised or diabetic: flucloxacillin 500 mg four times daily for 5–7 days; clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily if penicillin-allergic (erythromycin in pregnancy); co-amoxiclav if from a finger-sucking child or a bite (anaerobes, Eikenella)
    • Refer same day for suspected flexor tenosynovitis (Kanavel signs: fusiform swelling, flexed posture, tenderness along the sheath, pain on passive extension), felon (pulp-space abscess), osteomyelitis, or spreading infection in diabetes.
    • Recurrent acute episodes in the same fold should make you think of an underlying chronic paronychia, an ingrowing nail, HSV, or (single digit, persistent, in an older adult) SCC of the nail unit or amelanotic melanoma.

    Ingrowing toenail (onychocryptosis)

    • Causes: cutting nails curved or too short, tight shoes, hyperhidrosis, a naturally involuted ("pincer") nail. Commonest in teenagers and young men. Retinoids (isotretinoin) and some EGFR inhibitors cause periungual granulation tissue that looks the same.
    • Stages: inflammation → infection with granulation tissue ("proud flesh") → chronic hypertrophy of the fold.
    • Conservative care: cut the nail straight across, wear wide-toe-box shoes, warm salt soaks, lift the edge (cotton wisp or dental floss), use a gutter splint. Topical antiseptic. Antibiotics only for spreading cellulitis.
    • Definitive: partial nail avulsion with phenol matricectomy by podiatry. Success is ~95% and lower recurrence than avulsion alone. Total avulsion is for recurrent or bilateral disease. Diabetes or PAD → refer early to podiatry (foot-protection service).
    07

    Red flags & referral

    Escalate
    • Spreading cellulitis, lymphangitis, fever or a tense swollen finger pulp (felon) or tendon-sheath signs: same-day surgical assessment.
    • Grouped vesicles on the finger: herpetic whitlow, so do not incise.
    • Diabetes, peripheral arterial disease or immunosuppression with a toe infection: low threshold for referral.
    • A single nail with persistent ‘paronychia’, nail destruction or pigment: exclude nail-unit melanoma or SCC.
    08

    Prescriber pearls

    • Treat the fold environment, not only the tablet — wet-work continuation is the common reason for “failed” antifungals.
    • Warn about itraconazole/fluconazole interactions (CYP3A4), LFTs, pregnancy/contraception issues — counsel and check interactions software.
    • Nails grow slowly: cosmetic improvement lags mycological cure by months.
    • Yellow-green nails: consider Pseudomonas co-colonisation.

    YoursChronic paronychia is caused by streptococci/PseudomonasChronic paronychia (>6 weeks) is mainly an irritant/eczematous condition of the nail fold (wet work, cuticle loss) with secondary Candida (± bacterial) colonisation. Strep/staph cause acute paronychia, and Pseudomonas is a colonist (green nail). Treat with barrier repair: dry, gloves (cotton under rubber), emollient, potent TCS ± topical azole (CKS)
