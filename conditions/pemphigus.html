    Pemphigus vulgaris & the pemphigus family

    A rare, serious autoimmune disease with painful mouth erosions and fragile flaccid blisters that quickly become raw areas. It needs urgent or same-day dermatology; UK first-line treatment is rituximab with prednisolone.

    Overview

    Module 4 · Week 6 · Module 4 · Week 1PVpemphigus vulgarispemphigus foliaceus

    • Spot it

      Persistent painful mouth ulcers with or without fragile blisters and raw erosions on the scalp, face, chest or back.

    • Why

      IgG against desmoglein 3 (mucosal) ± desmoglein 1 (skin) causes acantholysis and an intraepidermal split.

    • Treat

      Recognise and refer urgently; specialists start rituximab + prednisolone (BAD 2017); the IP supports steroid safety, infection prevention and monitoring.

    • Never miss

      Dehydration and sepsis from extensive erosions; paraneoplastic pemphigus; drug-induced pemphigus (thiol drugs: penicillamine, captopril).

    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    What it is

    Pemphigus vulgaris is a rare, potentially fatal autoimmune intraepidermal blistering disease of skin and mucosa, caused by IgG against desmogleins. In the UK it typically affects people aged 40–60, and it is commoner in people of Ashkenazi Jewish, Indian subcontinent and Mediterranean descent. Before steroids the mortality was very high. Modern mortality is mostly from treatment (sepsis and steroid complications), around the ~5–10% the course quotes.

    Pemphigus vulgaris: primary-care spot diagnosis

    Pemphigus vulgaris (PV) is a rare, potentially life-threatening autoimmune intraepidermal blistering disease. Autoantibodies against desmogleins disrupt keratinocyte adhesion → flaccid blisters that rupture to painful erosions and crusts. Mucosal disease is common; oral erosions often precede skin. Module 4 Case 5: months of itchy crusted trunk sores + hoarseness (laryngeal mucosal involvement) after failed antibiotic courses → discussion diagnosis PV.

    Step 2 of 9 · 1 min

    Why it happens

    • Case 1 has severe oral disease and widespread skin erosions, so it is mucocutaneous, with both Dsg3 and Dsg1. "Dsg3" alone is half the story.
    • Paraneoplastic pemphigus is linked to lymphoproliferative disease (NHL, CLL, Castleman), with intractable stomatitis, polymorphous skin lesions and bronchiolitis obliterans. It needs a malignancy workup.
    • IgG autoantibodies to desmoglein 3 (± desmoglein 1) → acantholysis.
    • Flaccid blisters; Nikolsky sign may be positive (shear → extension of blister/erosion).
    • Without treatment historically high mortality (infection, fluid/protein loss); modern immunosuppression transformed prognosis.
    • Triggers/associations: idiopathic; drugs rarely (e.g. penicillamine, some ACEIs — uncommon); paraneoplastic pemphigus is a separate severe entity.
    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    How it’s caused

    • Desmoglein compensation theory: Dsg3 dominates in mucosa and the lower epidermis, and Dsg1 in the upper epidermis: Anti-Dsg3 alone gives mucosal-dominant PV; Anti-Dsg3 + anti-Dsg1 gives mucocutaneous PV; Anti-Dsg1 alone gives pemphigus foliaceus (superficial skin erosions and no mucosal disease, because Dsg3 compensates in mucosa)
    • Case 1 has severe oral disease and widespread skin erosions, so it is mucocutaneous, with both Dsg3 and Dsg1. "Dsg3" alone is half the story.
    • Drug-induced pemphigus is mainly due to thiol drugs (penicillamine, captopril) and less often non-thiol drugs (ACE inhibitors such as ramipril/enalapril, rifampicin, NSAIDs). Thiol-induced disease is usually foliaceus-like and often remits when the drug is stopped. In Case 1, ramipril is worth noting and discussing with the specialist, but it is a weak association.
    • Paraneoplastic pemphigus is linked to lymphoproliferative disease (NHL, CLL, Castleman), with intractable stomatitis, polymorphous skin lesions and bronchiolitis obliterans. It needs a malignancy workup.

    Next: What you’ll see
    3

    Step 3 of 9 · 1 min

    What you’ll see

    • Oral erosions first in ~50–70%: painful, persistent buccal/palatal/gingival erosions, often present for months before the skin is involved and treated as "aphthous" or "candida".
    • Flaccid blisters rupture quickly, so the patient usually presents with erosions and crusts (Case 1: "no blisters seen"). Sites are scalp, face, chest, back, axillae and groin. The erosions are painful, not itchy.
    • Positive Nikolsky and Asboe-Hansen signs.
    • Severe disease brings fluid and protein loss, poor oral intake, hypovolaemia and sepsis (Case 1: clinically dry, BP 125/60 on two antihypertensives).
    • Variants: vegetans (flexural vegetating plaques); foliaceus (scaly crusted "cornflake" erosions on the seborrhoeic trunk, face and scalp; photos A07/A08); erythematosus/Senear-Usher (malar, LE-like); IgA pemphigus; paraneoplastic

    Diagnosis

    Case 1 answers, with the UK detail added:

    • Histology: suprabasal acantholysis. The basal cells stay attached to the BMZ like a row of tombstones.
    • DIF of perilesional skin: intercellular IgG ± C3 in a "chicken-wire" pattern. DIF is the most sensitive test.
    • Serology: indirect IMF on monkey oesophagus, and ELISA anti-Dsg3/Dsg1, which are used for monitoring.
    • Tzanck smear (acantholytic cells) is historical and not used in UK practice.
    • Baseline before therapy: especially TPMT before azathioprine, hepatitis B before rituximab, and bone protection.

    Pemphigus vulgaris: primary-care spot diagnosis

    • Urgent dermatology.
    • Gold standard: lesional biopsy for histopathology plus perilesional direct immunofluorescence (DIF); serum ELISA for desmoglein antibodies.
    • Do not rely on antibiotics for crusted erosions without considering immunobullous disease.
    • ENT input if hoarseness / airway concern.

    More detailfull notes · 2 min

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    1. Recognise and refer.: Erosions + mucosal disease + unwell, dehydrated or extensive skin → same-day admission (acute medicine, with on-call dermatology). Case 1's hypovolaemia puts him here; Mucosal-only, stable disease → urgent dermatology (days, not weeks) with oral medicine input
    2. Specialist treatment (BAD 2017, paraphrased; for awareness, not IP initiation): First line for new moderate–severe PV: rituximab (2 × 1 g IV, 2 weeks apart) plus oral prednisolone (typically 0.5–1 mg/kg/day, tapered quicker than steroid-alone regimens). This follows the Ritux 3 trial, and NHS England commissions rituximab for PV; Steroid-sparing adjuvants: azathioprine (after TPMT) or mycophenolate mofetil. Use them when rituximab isn't possible or for maintenance; Refractory disease: IVIG, immunoadsorption, cyclophosphamide; Mild disease: superpotent topical or intralesional steroids; for oral disease, steroid mouthwash/spray (betamethasone soluble tablets as a mouthwash, or beclometasone inhaler sprayed onto the mucosa)
    3. IP supporting roles: Steroid safety: a steroid emergency card, bone protection (e.g. alendronic acid + calcium/vitamin D when long-term prednisolone ≥7.5 mg for ≥3 months is expected, per NOGG/BNF), a PPI if GI risk factors, glucose and BP monitoring (Case 1 BP: consider pausing or reducing antihypertensives while volume-depleted), and PJP prophylaxis per the specialist (co-trimoxazole with high-dose steroid + immunosuppressant); Vaccines: inactivated influenza/COVID/pneumococcal before rituximab where possible; no live vaccines while on immunosuppression; Shared-care monitoring for azathioprine/MMF (FBC/LFT per the local protocol); Oral care: soft diet, avoid spicy or acidic food, chlorhexidine or benzydamine mouthwash, treat candida (miconazole oral gel; check interactions, especially warfarin, or use nystatin), and refer to the dentist

    Pemphigus vulgaris: primary-care spot diagnosis

    • Recognition + urgent referral — not primary-care disease-modifying prescribing.
    • Supportive: emollients, wound care, analgesia, nutrition, treat secondary infection if present.
    • Specialty care: systemic corticosteroids historically foundational; rituximab (anti-CD20) now major disease-modifying option in specialist protocols; steroid-sparing agents as directed by derm.
    • Vaccination/infection risk counselling once on immunosuppression (specialist-led).
    • Same-day acute if extensive denudation, inability to drink, or airway symptoms.

    Step 7 of 9 · 1 min

    Red flags & referral

    • Same day

      Extensive erosions, unwell, dehydrated or unable to eat: same-day admission.

    • Routine

      Oral erosions persisting more than 2–3 weeks without a cause: refer (oral medicine or dermatology).

    • Routine

      Severe stomatitis with a known tumour: paraneoplastic pemphigus.

    Next: Prescriber pearls
    8

    Step 8 of 9 · 1 min

    Prescriber pearls

    • Months of "mouth ulcers" plus new skin erosions means PV until proven otherwise. Don't keep repeating antifungals or mouthwashes.

    • Drug-induced pemphigus: list every thiol or ACE inhibitor. Swapping ramipril for a non-ACE alternative (e.g. amlodipine alone, or an ARB) is reasonable to discuss with the specialist, but don't expect it to cure idiopathic PV.

    • Rituximab patients: infection risk lasts 6–12 months, with hepatitis B reactivation and poor vaccine response. Coordinate any surgery or dental work.

    • Patient support: PEMFriends (UK pemphigus and pemphigoid patient charity).

    • Crusted “sores” failing antibiotics + mucosal clues (oral/hoarse) = red-flag immunobullous.

    • Avoid attributing everything to infection or poorly controlled eczema without exam of mouth.

    • IP: escalate early; do not trial prolonged systemic steroids alone outside shared-care.
