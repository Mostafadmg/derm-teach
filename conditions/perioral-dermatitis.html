    Perioral (periorificial) dermatitis

    Small red papules and pustules around the mouth, nose or eyes with a clear rim next to the lips, often provoked by topical or inhaled steroids and heavy creams. Stop the steroid, expect a rebound, and treat with topical metronidazole or azelaic acid, or an oral tetracycline.

    Also calledperioral dermatitisperiorificial dermatitisperiocular dermatitissteroid-induced perioral dermatitis

    Key facts

    • Perioral dermatitis is a facial eruption of tiny papules and pustules around the mouth, nose or eyes with sparing next to the lips.
    • Commonest in women aged 20–45 and children; often provoked by topical, inhaled or nasal steroids and heavy creams.
    • No comedones (unlike acne); no flushing (unlike rosacea).
    • Stop steroids and cosmetics, expect a rebound; topical metronidazole or azelaic acid; oral tetracycline for 6–8 weeks if needed.
    • Children under 12: erythromycin rather than tetracycline.

    Overview

    Perioral dermatitis (periorificial dermatitis) is a common facial eruption of small papules and pustules around the mouth, nose and sometimes eyes, with a characteristic clear zone next to the lip vermilion. It mainly affects women aged 20–45 and children.

    Epidemiology

    • Who
      • Women 20–45 most; prepubertal children (often periocular, steroid-related).

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    • Topical corticosteroids on the face are the commonest trigger; also inhaled or nasal steroids, and heavy moisturisers or foundations.
    • Fluorinated toothpaste and barrier disruption may contribute.

    Risk factors and associations

    • Steroids disrupt the barrier and the follicle; occlusive cosmetics; possibly fluorinated toothpaste and microbiome changes.

    Myths to address

    • "Hydrocortisone will settle it": it masks it briefly and then makes it worse.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    Steroids and occlusion damage the barrier around facial follicles, triggering inflammation.

    1. 1

      Barrier disruption

      Steroids and heavy creams thin the barrier and alter the follicular microbiome.

    2. 2

      Follicular inflammation

      Innate immune activation causes small papules and pustules around follicles.

    3. 3

      Steroid dependence

      Steroids suppress the rash; stopping causes rebound, so patients reapply.

    4. 4

      Resolution

      Stopping the trigger and anti-inflammatory treatment restores the barrier over weeks.

    Histopathology

    • Rarely needed: perifollicular lymphohistiocytic infiltrate; granulomatous in the granulomatous variant.

    Clinical features

    • Monomorphic 1–2 mm red papules, papulovesicles and pustules on a red, sometimes scaly background.
    • Around the mouth, nasolabial folds, chin; may involve the sides of the nose and eyelids (periocular).
    • Vermilion sparing: a narrow clear rim around the lips.
    • Burning or tightness more than itch; no comedones.

    Special groups

    • Granulomatous periorificial dermatitis
      • Yellow-brown papules in children of African descent; slower to clear; refer if persistent.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    Clinical. Ask specifically about steroid creams, inhalers, nasal sprays and cosmetics.

    Differential diagnoses

    Look-alikeClues
    RosaceaOlder; flushing; central face; no clear perioral rim
    Acne vulgarisComedones; polymorphic lesions
    Seborrhoeic dermatitisGreasy scale in nasolabial folds and brows
    Contact dermatitisItch, eczematous, linked to a product

    Management

    • Stop the facial steroid (taper systemic or high-potency use with the prescriber; never stop inhaled asthma steroids: rinse and wipe the face after use).
    • Warn of a rebound flare for 1–2 weeks after stopping.
    • ‘Zero therapy’: stop all creams and cosmetics; use a gentle cleanser only.
    • Topical metronidazole or azelaic acid.
    • Oral tetracycline for 6–8 weeks if needed (erythromycin if under 12).

    Treatment ladder

    1. 1

      Stop triggers

      Stop facial steroids and heavy creams; gentle cleanser only.

    2. 2

      Topical

      Topical metronidazole or azelaic acid.

    3. 3

      Oral

      Oral tetracycline 6–8 weeks if needed (erythromycin if under 12).

    Referral

    • Routine

      Not responding after 8–12 weeks, granulomatous or periocular disease, diagnostic doubt.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Granulomatous or periocular disease not responding, or diagnostic doubt: dermatology.

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Steroid dependence, rebound flares, distress.

    Prognosis

    • Clears over 6–12 weeks with treatment; recurs if steroids are restarted.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • Pharmacists often spot inhaled-steroid perioral dermatitis: advise rinsing and wiping the face after inhalers.

    • Hydrocortisone makes it look better briefly, then worse.

    Self-check quiz

    4 questions · optional
    1. Q1Commonest trigger of perioral dermatitis?Reveal

      Topical (or inhaled/nasal) corticosteroids on the face.

    2. Q2What is the classic sign around the lips?Reveal

      A clear rim next to the vermilion (vermilion sparing).

    3. Q3What happens after stopping the steroid?Reveal

      A rebound flare for 1–2 weeks.

    4. Q4Oral option in under-12s?Reveal

      Erythromycin (tetracyclines avoided).
