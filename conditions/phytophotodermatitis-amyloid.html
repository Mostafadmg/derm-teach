    Pigment & colour change

    Phytophotodermatitis & cutaneous amyloid

    Module 6 · Week 3

    Plant psoralens plus UVA cause streaky burns then long pigmentation; macular/lichen amyloidosis cause rippled itchy brown patches from chronic friction.

    Coversphytophotodermatitisberloquegiant hogweedlime burnmacular amyloidosislichen amyloidosis
    Think of it when

    Bizarre drip, streak or hand-print burns after gardening or lime cocktails in the sun — later deep brown marks; or rippled itchy interscapular pigment from scratching.

    What drives it

    Phototoxic furocoumarin + UVA damage (phyto); keratin-derived amyloid from chronic friction/itch (amyloidosis).

    Backbone of care

    Wash plant juice off, cool care, short topical steroid, strict sun protection; for amyloid — stop friction, potent steroid ± occlusion, refer if refractory.

    Never miss

    Child hand-print phytophotodermatitis can mimic non-accidental injury — take a careful plant/sun history.

    What it is

    Phytophotodermatitis is a non-immune phototoxic reaction when plant psoralens on the skin meet UVA, causing erythema and blisters in odd shapes, then long-lasting hyperpigmentation. Classic plants: giant hogweed, parsnip, celery, lime, bergamot, fig. Berloque dermatitis is perfume bergamot streaks on the neck.

    Macular and lichen amyloidosis are localised cutaneous amyloidoses: rippled brown macules or itchy keratotic papules from chronic friction, often on the upper back or shins.

    2

    How it’s caused

    • Psoralens + UVA → epidermal damage and later melanogenesis.
    • Exposure: strimming, hiking, holiday drinks, cooking with limes.
    • Amyloid: chronic scratching/friction deposits keratin-derived amyloid in the papillary dermis.
    3

    Clinical features

    • Phyto: burning erythema ± bullae at 24–72 h in streaks/drips/hand-prints; pigmentation weeks later lasting months.
    • Amyloid: rippled “rippled” brown macular patches (interscapular) or lichenified papules on shins; itch can be severe.
    4

    Diagnosis

    Phyto is clinical from shape + exposure history. Amyloid is clinical ± biopsy with special stains when needed.

    5

    Differentials

    Look-alikeClues

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    Phytophotodermatitis

    • Wash plant sap off promptly; cool soaks; leave small blisters intact.
    • Short topical steroid for inflammation; analgesia; strict sun protection while pigment settles.
    • Prevention: recognise hogweed, cover arms/legs when strimming, wash after gardening, avoid lime juice on skin in sun.

    Cutaneous amyloidosis

    • Stop scrubbing/loofahs; soft clothing; potent topical steroid ± occlusion or topical calcineurin inhibitor for itch.
    • Emollients; consider sedating antihistamine at night for sleep.
    • Refer if severe, diagnostic doubt, or for specialist options (retinoids, phototherapy).
    7

    Red flags & referral

    • Extensive hogweed burns or eye exposure → urgent assessment.
    • Safeguarding concern when phytophotodermatitis mimics injury patterns in children.
    • Systemic amyloid signs are a different disease — this page is localised cutaneous only.
    8

    Prescriber pearls

    • Pigmentation, not the acute burn, is often why patients attend.
    • Berloque = perfume + sun streaks.
    • Tell gardeners about giant hogweed — sap plus sun is a classic UK summer presentation.
