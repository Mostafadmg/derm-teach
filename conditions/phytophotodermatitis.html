    Phytophotodermatitis

    Plant furocoumarins (psoralens) on the skin plus UVA cause a non-immune phototoxic burn in bizarre streaks, drips or hand-prints, followed by pigmentation that lasts months.

    Also calledphytophotodermatitisphototoxic plant dermatitisberloque dermatitisgiant hogweed burnlime burnmargarita dermatitis+1
    Related pagesCutaneous amyloidosis (macular and lichen)Drug-induced photosensitivityPolymorphic light eruptionBlister beetle & Paederus dermatitis

    Key facts

    • A non-immune phototoxic reaction: plant furocoumarins (psoralens) on the skin plus UVA light.
    • Anyone can be affected with enough sap and sun; no previous sensitisation is needed.
    • Burning redness and blisters appear about 24 h after exposure and peak at 48–72 h, in streaks, drips or hand-prints.
    • Hyperpigmentation follows and can last months to years; it is often why people present.
    • Manage like a superficial burn: wash sap off, keep out of sun for 48 h, short topical steroid, strict sun protection. Think safeguarding with odd marks in children.

    Overview

    Phytophotodermatitis is a phototoxic skin reaction that happens when light-sensitising chemicals in some plants (furocoumarins, also called psoralens) get on the skin and are then exposed to ultraviolet A (UVA). It is not an allergy: the sap and light damage the skin directly, so it can happen to anyone at first exposure.

    • The burn has bizarre, geometric shapes (streaks, drips, splashes, finger- and hand-prints) that trace where the juice or sap touched.
    • It heals with brown pigmentation that can persist for months.
    • Classic UK sources: giant hogweed, wild parsnip and rue (strimming, walking), and limes (cocktails and cooking on holiday).
    • Berloque dermatitis is a variant from bergamot-containing perfume on the neck.

    Epidemiology

    • Who
      • Any age, sex or skin type; incidence is unknown because mild cases are not reported.
    • When
      • Mostly summer and sunny holidays, when people handle plants or citrus outdoors.
    • At-risk groups
      • Gardeners, farm and grounds workers, bartenders and chefs (limes), children playing outdoors, hikers.
      • Fair skin may burn more readily, while darker skin shows more marked and longer-lasting pigmentation.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    Two things are needed: a furocoumarin on the skin and enough UVA. Moisture (sweat, water) and heat increase absorption and the reaction.

    Common plant sources

    FamilyExamples
    Apiaceae (Umbelliferae)Giant hogweed, cow parsley, wild and cultivated parsnip, celery, carrot, fennel, dill, parsley
    RutaceaeLime, lemon, bergamot orange, bitter orange, grapefruit, rue
    MoraceaeFig (leaves and sap)
    OtherSome St John's wort and other garden plants
    • Activities: strimming or mowing weeds, clearing riverbanks, picking wild plants, squeezing limes or making drinks in the sun.
    • Berloque dermatitis: perfume containing bergamot oil (5-methoxypsoralen) applied to the neck, then sun.

    Myths to address

    • "It must be an allergy": no; phototoxicity needs no sensitisation and anyone can react.
    • "It was a sunburn": plain sunburn is uniform over exposed skin; the streaks and drips point to a plant or citrus.
    • "The marks are bruises or scars": they are pigment and fade slowly without scarring.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    1. 1

      Sap meets light

      Furocoumarins in plant sap or citrus juice penetrate the epidermis. UVA (320–400 nm, peak effect around 335 nm) activates them; a cloudy day still delivers enough UVA.

    2. 2

      Phototoxic damage to DNA and membranes

      Activated psoralens act in two ways: type I reactions bind directly to DNA, forming cross-links between strands; type II reactions generate reactive oxygen species that damage cell membranes. No immune memory is involved.

    3. 3

      Keratinocyte death and blistering

      Damaged keratinocytes die ("sunburn cells"), with dermal oedema and inflammation. Severe damage separates the epidermis, producing vesicles and bullae after about 24 h, peaking at 48–72 h.

    4. 4

      Pigment follows

      Psoralen–UVA stimulates melanocytes to make more melanin and melanin drops into the dermis where melanophages hold it, so brown or grey-brown pigment appears 1–2 weeks later and lasts months.

    Histopathology

    • Acute: necrotic keratinocytes, spongiosis, intraepidermal or subepidermal vesicles, dermal oedema and a mild perivascular infiltrate (like an acute sunburn).
    • Late: pigment incontinence with melanophages in the upper dermis and increased basal melanin.
    • Biopsy is rarely needed; history and shape are diagnostic.

    Clinical features

    Symptoms

    • Burning or stinging pain more than itch, starting about 24 h after sun exposure.
    • Usually no systemic symptoms, except with extensive hogweed burns.
    • Later, brown marks with no memory of the inflammatory phase: often the presenting complaint.

    Lesions and morphology

    • Streaky erythema

      Sharply outlined linear streaks of redness and oedema where sap brushed the skin.

    • Vesicles and bullae

      Tense blisters, sometimes large, resembling a partial-thickness burn.

    • Drips and hand-prints

      Drip, splash and finger-print shapes from citrus juice.

    • Hyperpigmentation

      Brown to grey-brown streaks that persist for months after the burn settles.

    Distribution

    • Exposed skin that touched the plant or juice: hands, forearms, legs and ankles (strimming), around the mouth and chin (citrus), neck (perfume).
    • Shapes follow contact, not the whole sun-exposed area (unlike drug photosensitivity).

    Variants

    • Lime ("margarita") dermatitis

      Drips and hand-prints on hands, wrists, face and chest after squeezing limes in the sun.

    • Giant hogweed burn

      Severe blistering on arms and legs after contact with sap on riverbanks and verges; can need burns care.

    • Bullous phytophotodermatitis

      Large blisters, usually on the lower legs and ankles.

    • Pigmentation-only presentation

      Streaky brown marks without remembered redness.

    • Berloque dermatitis

      Pendant-shaped streaks on the neck from bergamot perfume plus sun.

    Special groups

    • Children
      • Hand-print or splash patterns can look like bruising or non-accidental injury; take a careful plant and sun history and follow safeguarding procedures if the story does not fit.
    • Skin of colour
      • Redness may be subtle; the hyperpigmentation is often marked and lasts longer.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    Diagnosis is clinical, from the bizarre shape plus an exposure history.

    History

    • Plants handled, strimming or gardening, walks through long vegetation, citrus drinks or cooking, perfumes.
    • Sun exposure in the previous 1–2 days; timing of symptoms.
    • Drugs (to exclude drug photosensitivity); previous similar episodes.

    Examination

    • Shape and site of lesions; blister size and extent; eye involvement; signs of infection.

    Investigations

    • Usually none.
    • Swab if secondary infection is suspected.
    • Patch or photopatch testing only if allergic contact dermatitis is a possibility.
    • Biopsy rarely.

    Differential diagnoses

    PhotoConditionDistinguishing features
    		Allergic contact dermatitis (plants)Very itchy eczema; needs sensitisation; does not need sun; spreads beyond the contact.
    		Drug photosensitivityUniform redness over all sun-exposed skin with a drug history (e.g. doxycycline, thiazides).
    		SunburnUniform over exposed sites; no streaks.
    		Blister beetle dermatitisLinear blisters after crushing a beetle; no sun needed.
    		Polymorphic light eruptionItchy papules on sun-exposed skin hours after sun; recurrent each spring.

    Management

    Principles

    • Treat the acute phase like a superficial burn; then protect the pigmented skin from the sun.
    • Explain the cause so the patient can avoid it; most cases settle without scarring.

    Immediate care after contact (e.g. hogweed sap)

    • Wash off sap promptly with soap and water, cover the skin and keep it out of sunlight for at least 48 h.
    • If sap gets in the eyes: rinse and seek urgent eye assessment.

    Conservative and self-care

    • Cool compresses, emollients and simple analgesia.
    • Leave blisters intact or use non-adherent dressings; keep clean (topical antiseptic only if needed).
    • OTC hydrocortisone 1% for mild small areas (not on broken skin).

    Medical treatment

    Potent topical corticosteroid

    Short course
    Dose
    Short course of 5–7 days to inflamed intact skin on the limbs or trunk; moderate potency on the face.
    Cautions
    Not on broken or infected skin.
    Counselling
    Apply thinly; stop when redness settles.

    Oral antihistamine

    Symptom relief
    Use
    If itchy.

    Secondary infection

    Antibiotic per NICE guidance
    Use
    Infected blisters: treat per NICE impetigo / secondary bacterial infection guidance.
    Counselling
    Link: impetigo.

    Pigmentation phase

    • Reassure: it fades slowly over months.
    • Strict sun protection (even limited UV can darken it again): clothing, broad-spectrum high-SPF sunscreen.
    • Avoid bleaching agents unless advised by a specialist.

    Prevention

    • Learn to recognise giant hogweed; wear long sleeves, trousers and gloves when strimming or clearing plants.
    • Wash skin after gardening; avoid squeezing limes or applying citrus or perfume to skin before sun.

    Treatment ladder

    1. 1

      Everyone

      Wash off sap, 48 h out of sun, cool compresses, analgesia, emollient.

    2. 2

      Inflamed intact skin

      Potent topical steroid 5–7 days (moderate on face); antihistamine if itchy.

    3. 3

      Pigmentation

      Reassurance and strict sun protection for months.

    4. 4

      Burns-level injury

      Large or blistering burns, face, genitals or hands: same-day urgent care or burns unit.

    Referral

    • Same day

      Large or extensive blistering, burns on the face, genitals or hands, severe pain or infection: same-day GP, urgent care or burns unit.

    • Same day

      Sap in the eye: urgent eye assessment.

    • Urgent

      Safeguarding concern in a child: follow local procedures.

    • Routine

      Diagnostic doubt or persistent distressing pigmentation.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Same day

      Extensive hogweed burns, eye exposure, or blistering of the face, hands or genitals.

    • Urgent

      Unexplained marks in a child where the history does not fit: safeguarding.

    • Routine

      Signs of infection: spreading redness, pus, fever.

    Complications and prognosis

    • Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation for months, sometimes years.
    • Secondary bacterial infection of blisters.
    • Rarely scarring after severe bullous burns.
    • Misdiagnosis as abuse or burns causing distress.

    Prognosis

    • The acute reaction settles in 1–2 weeks; pigment fades gradually over months with sun protection.
    • Recurs only with re-exposure.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • Pigmentation, not the acute burn, is often why patients attend.

    • Berloque = perfume + sun streaks.

    • Tell gardeners about giant hogweed: sap plus sun is a classic UK summer presentation.

    • Ask about holidays, cocktails and cooking with limes.

    • Check for photosensitising drugs before blaming the plant.

    • Other light-related rashes: polymorphic light eruption, drug-induced photosensitivity and chronic actinic dermatitis.
