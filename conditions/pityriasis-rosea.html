    Pityriasis rosea

    Module 5 · Week 5 · Module 4 · Week 1

    A self-limiting rash of young adults: a single herald patch, then oval scaly patches along skin lines on the trunk (‘Christmas tree’), fading over 6–12 weeks. Reassure, but test for syphilis when the picture is atypical.

    CoversPRherald patchChristmas tree rashcollarette scaleHHV-6HHV-7guttate psoriasissecondary syphilis
    Think of it when

    A single larger oval patch, then 1–2 weeks later many smaller pink oval patches with a collarette of scale on the trunk.

    What drives it

    Probably reactivation of HHV-6/HHV-7; not contagious in practice.

    Backbone of care

    Explain and reassure; emollients, mild topical steroid or antihistamine for itch; review if it lasts beyond 12 weeks.

    Never miss

    Secondary syphilis (palms and soles, mucosal lesions, lymph nodes), guttate psoriasis, tinea and drug eruptions; PR in early pregnancy (miscarriage risk).

    What it is

    Diagnosis confirmed from the course case: the case shows a single initial lesion (Picture 1) followed ~2 weeks later by a generalised eruption (Picture 2) after a URTI. All student answers (incl. a student's post, 16 June) and the tutor discussion converged on pityriasis rosea; guttate psoriasis was listed as a differential, not the answer. The tutor then focused on secondary syphilis as the must-not-miss differential.

    A common, self-limiting papulosquamous eruption of adolescents and young adults (10–35 years), beginning with a single herald patch followed days to ~2 weeks later by a secondary eruption of smaller oval scaly patches on the trunk and proximal limbs. Lasts about 6–12 weeks (most 6–8). Recurrence is uncommon (~2–3%).

    2

    How it’s caused

    Cause not proven; strongest evidence links it to reactivation of human herpesvirus 6 and 7 (HHV-6/HHV-7) — supported by preceding URTI/prodrome, seasonal clustering, and low recurrence. PR-like (pityriasiform) drug eruptions occur with e.g. ACE inhibitors, NSAIDs, isotretinoin, gold, some biologics/vaccines — these lack a herald patch and persist while the drug continues.

    3

    Clinical features

    • Possible prodrome: malaise, headache, sore throat, low-grade fever (URTI — Case 3).
    • Herald patch: single, 2–5 cm, oval, salmon-pink (grey-brown/violaceous in darker skin) plaque with fine collarette scale inside the edge; often trunk, upper arm, neck, thigh.
    • Secondary eruption (after ~1–2 weeks): many smaller oval macules/plaques with collarette scale, long axes along skin cleavage lines → "Christmas-tree" pattern on the back, "V" on the chest; usually spares face, scalp, palms and soles.
    • Itch: none to moderate (~25% significant).
    • Variants: inverse (flexural — axillae/groin, commoner in children/skin of colour), papular, vesicular, purpuric, urticarial, giant, unilateral, oral lesions.
    • Darker skin: more papular/facial lesions; prolonged post-inflammatory hyper/hypopigmentation.
    4

    Diagnosis

    Clinical. Tests only to exclude mimics:

    • Syphilis serology (treponemal EIA ± RPR) if atypical, palms/soles involved, no herald patch, sexual risk, or lasting >12 weeks.
    • Skin scrapings for mycology if the herald patch alone looks like tinea (scale at advancing edge).
    • Drug history (pityriasiform drug eruption).
    • Biopsy rarely (atypical/persistent).
    • Pregnancy test/status relevant — see 3.6.
    5

    Differentials

    Look-alikeDistinguishing clues
    Guttate psoriasisMany small (0.5–1 cm) drop-like papules with thicker silvery scale over the whole lesion, no herald patch, 1–3 weeks after streptococcal sore throat/tonsillitis; may have family history, nail pitting, scalp involvement; can persist/evolve into plaque psoriasis
    Secondary syphilisPalms and soles copper-red papules, mucous patches, condylomata lata, patchy alopecia, generalised lymphadenopathy, malaise; papules tender to blunt pressure (Buschke–Ollendorff sign — tutor pearl); sexual history; positive serology
    Tinea corporis (vs herald patch)Annular, active scaly edge with central clearing, scale at advancing edge, often asymmetric; KOH/scraping positive
    Nummular (discoid) eczemaCoin-shaped, very itchy, crusted/oozing, limbs > trunk
    Pityriasiform drug eruptionNew drug, larger lesions, no herald patch, persists, eosinophils on biopsy
    Pityriasis versicolorFine scale, hypo/hyperpigmented, upper trunk, Malassezia on scraping, chronic
    Pityriasis lichenoides chronicaSmall red-brown papules with mica-like scale, recurrent crops over months
    Seborrhoeic dermatitis (trunk)Presternal/interscapular greasy scale, scalp/face involvement

    Guttate psoriasis vs pityriasis rosea

    What it is

    Guttate psoriasis is an acute eruption of small (<1 cm) drop-like scaly papules/plaques, typically in children, teens, and young adults — often 1–3 weeks after streptococcal pharyngitis. Module 4 Spot Diagnosis consensus: widespread scaly eruption on trunk/limbs in a 14-year-old with no prior skin disease → guttate psoriasis. Main clinical DDx: pityriasis rosea.

    How it is caused / pathophysiology

    • Same psoriatic immune axis; strong link to Group A streptococcus and HLA-C*06:02-enriched early-onset disease.
    • May be first presentation of psoriasis or remain a one-off self-limited episode.
    • PR (DDx): putative viral trigger; herald patch then secondary oval plaques on Langer lines.

    Clinical features & typical sites

    Guttate psoriasisPityriasis rosea
    LesionsUniform raindrop scaly papules/plaquesHerald patch then smaller oval plaques with collarette
    DistributionTrunk + limbs; often spares palms/solesTrunk/proximal limbs; Christmas-tree back
    ScalePsoriaticFine peripheral collarette
    Strep HxCommonAbsent
    CourseWeeks–months; may clear or evolve to plaque psoriasisUsually clears 6–12 weeks

    How to diagnose

    • Clinical. Ask about sore throat; consider throat swab / ASO if indicated.
    • Look for herald patch and Christmas-tree pattern to favour PR.
    • Biopsy rarely needed.
    • Consider syphilis serology if PR-like but atypical (palms/soles, systemic, persistent).

    Key differentials

    Look-alikeDistinguishing clues
    Pityriasis roseaHerald + cleavage-line ovals
    Secondary syphilisPalms/soles, lymphadenopathy, serology
    Tinea versicolor / corporisMorphology/KOH
    Viral exanthemLess scale; febrile context
    Small-plaque parapsoriasisPersistent; adult; refer if unsure

    Management for pharmacist/IP (UK)

    Guttate:

    • Emollients; mild–potent TCS matched to extent/site; vitamin D analogues sometimes.
    • Treat proven strep infection per local antimicrobial guidance (treating strep does not always clear guttate quickly).
    • Counsel possible recurrence or later plaque psoriasis.

    PR (if that is the diagnosis):

    • Reassure; emollient ± mild TCS for itch; UV advice individualised; refer if severe/atypical.

    Prescriber pearls & counselling

    • Teen + abrupt drop-like scaly rash + recent sore throat ≈ guttate until proven otherwise.
    • Avoid oral steroids.
    • Safety-net return if eruption becomes confluent, pustular, or erythrodermic.

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    Based on NICE CKS Pityriasis rosea and PCDS/BAD leaflet themes:

    Self-care / reassurance

    • Explain benign, non-contagious, self-limiting (6–12 weeks); no isolation needed; scars do not occur (pigment change may persist months in darker skin).
    • Emollients and soap substitutes; avoid hot showers, perfumed products, tight clothes; moderate sun exposure may speed resolution in some.

    Symptomatic Rx

    • Itch: emollient ± mild–moderate topical corticosteroid (e.g. clobetasone butyrate 0.05%) short course to itchy areas; sedating antihistamine at night (e.g. chlorphenamine; counsel drowsiness) or non-sedating by day.
    • Evidence for high-dose aciclovir early in extensive/symptomatic disease is limited — specialist decision.

    Guttate psoriasis (if that is the diagnosis instead)

    • Throat swab only if sore throat ongoing (treat streptococcal infection per NG84); emollients; topical vitamin D analogue ± topical steroid, coal-tar; refer dermatology for NB-UVB phototherapy if widespread/distressing (lesions too numerous for topicals). Often clears in 3–4 months; ~⅓ later develop chronic plaque psoriasis.

    Red flags / refer

    • Pregnancy: PR in pregnancy (esp. first 15 weeks) has been associated with miscarriage/premature delivery — inform/refer to midwife/obstetrician (CKS).
    • Atypical features, palms/soles/mucosal involvement or sexual risk → syphilis serology / refer to sexual health (GUM).
    • Persisting >12 weeks, diagnostic doubt, severe symptoms → GP/dermatology.
    • Suspected drug cause → review medication with prescriber.
    7

    Red flags & referral

    • Palm or sole lesions, mouth ulcers, lymphadenopathy or sexual risk: syphilis serology and sexual-health referral.
    • Pityriasis rosea in the first 15 weeks of pregnancy: inform the midwife or obstetric team.
    • Lasting more than 3 months: reconsider the diagnosis.
    8

    Prescriber pearls

    • "One big patch first, then many small ovals along the ribs" = PR; "shower of small drops after strep throat" = guttate psoriasis.
    • The herald patch is often mis-treated as ringworm — antifungal failure is a clue.
    • Every generalised papulosquamous rash: ask about sexual history and look at palms and soles.
    • Do not prescribe oral steroids for PR (may prolong/worsen).
    • Provide BAD/PCDS patient leaflet; review if not resolving by 3 months.
