    Infections — fungal

    Pityriasis versicolor

    Malassezia yeast overgrowth causing pale, pink or brown finely scaly patches on the trunk. Treat with a ketoconazole wash and warn that the colour takes months to return.

    Coverstinea versicolormalasseziapityrosporumketoconazole shampoopale patches
    Think of it when

    Well-defined pale, pink or fawn macules with fine, bran-like scale on the upper trunk and shoulders of a young adult, often noticed after a holiday.

    What drives it

    Overgrowth of Malassezia, a normal skin yeast, in warm, sweaty, sebum-rich skin. It interferes with melanin production.

    Backbone of care

    Ketoconazole 2% shampoo used as a body wash for 5 days, or an azole cream for small areas.

    Never miss

    Not dangerous. Make sure it is not vitiligo (no scale, depigmented) or pityriasis alba in children.

    What it is

    Pityriasis versicolor is a common, superficial yeast infection that causes patchy colour change with fine scale, usually on the trunk. It is caused by Malassezia, a yeast that is part of normal skin flora.

    Because the organism is a normal skin resident, the condition is not contagious in the usual sense. It is an overgrowth rather than something caught.

    2

    How it’s caused

    • Lipophilic Malassezia overgrows in sebum-rich skin (chest, back, shoulders, neck).
    • It produces substances that interfere with melanin, giving hypopigmented patches, or causes mild inflammation, giving pink or brown hyperpigmented patches.
    • Promoters: heat, humidity, sweating, oily skin and young adulthood. Mild immunosuppression can contribute.
    3

    Clinical features

    • Well-demarcated hypopigmented, hyperpigmented or pink-fawn macules that coalesce into patches.
    • Fine, bran-like scale, which becomes more obvious when the skin is gently scratched or stretched (the ‘evoked scale’ sign).
    • Sites: upper trunk, shoulders, neck and upper arms.
    • Usually asymptomatic or mildly itchy. Often noticed after tanning, because affected skin does not tan.
    4

    Diagnosis

    • Usually clinical.
    • Skin scrapings or tape-stripping for microscopy show the classic ‘spaghetti and meatballs’ hyphae and spores.
    • Wood’s lamp may show a yellow-green fluorescence, but this is variable.
    5

    Differentials

    Look-alikeDistinguishing clues
    VitiligoComplete depigmentation (chalk-white), no scale, often on the hands, face and around orifices
    Pityriasis albaChildren; ill-defined pale patches on the face, associated with atopy
    Post-inflammatory hypopigmentationHistory of a previous rash at the same sites; no scale
    Seborrhoeic dermatitisGreasy scale; scalp, face and central chest
    Pityriasis roseaHerald patch, then a ‘Christmas-tree’ pattern; self-limiting over 6–8 weeks
    6

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    First line (CKS)

    • Extensive: ketoconazole 2% shampoo used as a body wash once daily for up to 5 days. Lather onto the affected skin, leave for 3–5 minutes, then rinse. Licensed for this use from age 12.
    • Small areas: an azole (clotrimazole, econazole, ketoconazole) or terbinafine cream twice daily for 2–3 weeks. Clotrimazole is preferred in pregnancy.

    If topicals fail or disease is very widespread

    Oral itraconazole or fluconazole in adults. Check interactions and pregnancy status. Seek specialist advice for pregnancy or children.

    Preventing recurrence

    Recurrence is common. Options are ketoconazole shampoo every 1–4 weeks, or a 3-day course before a sunny holiday.

    7

    Red flags & referral

    • Diagnostic doubt, especially no scale or complete depigmentation (think vitiligo).
    • Very extensive, recurrent or unusual disease: consider immunosuppression.
    • Pregnancy or children needing oral therapy: seek specialist advice.
    8

    Prescriber pearls

    • Manage expectations: the colour takes weeks to months to return after the yeast is cleared. This is not treatment failure.
    • Reassure that it is not caused by poor hygiene and is not usually catching.
    • Even sun exposure after treatment helps the colour even out. Ongoing scale suggests the infection is still active.
    • A prophylaxis plan before holidays prevents a lot of repeat consultations.
