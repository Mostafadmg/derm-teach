    Infections — fungal

    Pityriasis versicolor

    Malassezia yeast overgrowth causing pale, pink or brown finely scaly patches on the trunk. Treat with a ketoconazole wash and warn that the colour takes months to return.

    Also calledtinea versicolormalasseziapityrosporumketoconazole shampoopale patches
    Related pagesHypopigmentation & skin of colourTinea: corporis, cruris & pedisPityriasis roseaFolliculitis

    Key facts

    • Superficial infection with the lipophilic yeast Malassezia causing scaly hypo- or hyperpigmented patches on the upper trunk.
    • Most common in young adults, hot humid climates and people who sweat heavily; recurs each summer.
    • Fine scale shows when the skin is stretched or scraped; Wood's light may give yellow fluorescence.
    • First line (CKS): ketoconazole 2% shampoo as a short-contact wash; topical azole for small areas; oral itraconazole if extensive or resistant.
    • Colour takes months to normalise after the yeast is cleared; recurrence is common (prophylaxis may help).

    Overview

    Pityriasis versicolor is a common, superficial yeast infection that causes patchy colour change with fine scale, usually on the trunk. It is caused by Malassezia, a yeast that is part of normal skin flora.

    Because the organism is a normal skin resident, the condition is not contagious in the usual sense. It is an overgrowth rather than something caught.

    Epidemiology

    • Who
      • Young adults, slightly more men; up to 40% in tropical climates, 1–4% in temperate climates.
      • Malassezia is normal flora in 90–100% of adults.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    • Lipophilic Malassezia overgrows in sebum-rich skin (chest, back, shoulders, neck).
    • It produces substances that interfere with melanin, giving hypopigmented patches, or causes mild inflammation, giving pink or brown hyperpigmented patches.
    • Promoters: heat, humidity, sweating, oily skin and young adulthood. Mild immunosuppression can contribute.

    Risk factors and associations

    • Organisms: M. globosa, M. sympodialis, M. furfur.
    • Triggers: heat, humidity, sweating, oily skin, occlusive clothing, immunosuppression, pregnancy, genetic predisposition.

    Myths to address

    • "The white patches are vitiligo": PV patches are scaly and fade after treatment; vitiligo is smooth and depigmented.
    • "It's contagious": it comes from your own skin yeast.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    Normal skin yeast switches to a hyphal form and alters pigment in the stratum corneum.

    1. 1

      Yeast to hyphae

      Heat, humidity and sebum let Malassezia convert from yeast to the invasive mycelial form in the stratum corneum.

    2. 2

      Hypopigmentation

      The yeast produces azelaic acid and other dicarboxylic acids that inhibit tyrosinase and melanocytes, so patches look paler, especially after a tan.

    3. 3

      Hyperpigmentation

      In other patients, thicker keratin and inflammation make patches pink or brown.

    4. 4

      Slow recovery

      After the yeast is killed, pigment returns only slowly over months.

    Histopathology

    • Short hyphae and round yeasts ('spaghetti and meatballs') in the stratum corneum on KOH microscopy of scrapings.
    • Biopsy is not needed.

    Clinical features

    • Well-demarcated hypopigmented, hyperpigmented or pink-fawn macules that coalesce into patches.
    • Fine, bran-like scale, which becomes more obvious when the skin is gently scratched or stretched (the ‘evoked scale’ sign).
    • Sites: upper trunk, shoulders, neck and upper arms.
    • Usually asymptomatic or mildly itchy. Often noticed after tanning, because affected skin does not tan.

    Colour variants

    • Hypopigmented

      Coalescing pale scaly macules. Site: Upper trunk.

    • On tanned skin

      Small pale scaly macules; noticed after sun. Site: Shoulders.

    • Hyperpigmented / pink

      Fawn-pink scaly macules coalescing. Site: Chest, back.

    Special groups

    • Skin of colour
      • Hypopigmentation is especially noticeable and distressing; reassure that colour returns slowly.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    • Usually clinical.
    • Skin scrapings or tape-stripping for microscopy show the classic ‘spaghetti and meatballs’ hyphae and spores.
    • Wood’s lamp may show a yellow-green fluorescence, but this is variable.

    Clinical

    • Usually clinical.
    • Stretch the skin to evoke scale.

    Microscopy

    • Scrapings or tape strip: ‘spaghetti and meatballs’ hyphae and spores.

    Wood’s lamp

    • Yellow-green fluorescence: variable.

    If no scale

    • Complete depigmentation, no scale → think vitiligo.

    Management

    First line (CKS)

    • Extensive: ketoconazole 2% shampoo used as a body wash once daily for up to 5 days. Lather onto the affected skin, leave for 3–5 minutes, then rinse. Licensed for this use from age 12.
    • Small areas: an azole (clotrimazole, econazole, ketoconazole) or terbinafine cream twice daily for 2–3 weeks. Clotrimazole is preferred in pregnancy.

    If topicals fail or disease is very widespread

    Oral itraconazole or fluconazole in adults. Check interactions and pregnancy status. Seek specialist advice for pregnancy or children.

    Preventing recurrence

    Recurrence is common. Options are ketoconazole shampoo every 1–4 weeks, or a 3-day course before a sunny holiday.

    Caution

    Prescribing limits and pitfalls

    • Expectations: Colour takes weeks to months to return: not treatment failure.
    • Ongoing scale: Suggests the infection is still active.

    Referral

    • Routine

      Diagnostic doubt: especially no scale or complete depigmentation (vitiligo).

    • Routine

      Very extensive, recurrent or unusual: consider immunosuppression.

    • Routine

      Pregnancy or children needing oral therapy: specialist advice.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Diagnostic doubt, especially no scale or complete depigmentation (think vitiligo).
    • Very extensive, recurrent or unusual disease: consider immunosuppression.
    • Pregnancy or children needing oral therapy: seek specialist advice.

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Persistent pigment change for months.
    • Recurrence in up to 60% within a year and 80% within 2 years without prophylaxis.

    Prognosis

    • Responds well to antifungals; recurrence is very common, so maintenance or seasonal prophylaxis may be offered.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • Manage expectations: the colour takes weeks to months to return after the yeast is cleared. This is not treatment failure.

    • Reassure that it is not caused by poor hygiene and is not usually catching.

    • Even sun exposure after treatment helps the colour even out. Ongoing scale suggests the infection is still active.

    • A prophylaxis plan before holidays prevents a lot of repeat consultations.
