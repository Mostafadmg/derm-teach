    Polymorphic light eruption

    The commonest photodermatosis: itchy papules or vesicles on sun-exposed skin hours after the first strong sun of spring or a holiday. Sun protection, short potent steroid bursts and, for severe cases, specialist ‘hardening’ phototherapy.

    Overview

    Module 5 · Week 6PMLEPLEsun allergy

    • Spot it

      An itchy rash on the V of the neck, forearms and backs of hands hours to a day after early-season sun, settling within days without further exposure.

    • Why

      A delayed immune reaction to a UV-induced skin antigen, with a failure of normal UV immunosuppression; it improves through the summer (‘hardening’).

    • Treat

      Broad-spectrum high-UVA sunscreen, clothing and gradual exposure; potent topical steroid for flares; refer for phototesting or hardening if severe.

    • Never miss

      Lupus (persistent rash, systemic symptoms, positive ANA) and drug photosensitivity.

    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    What it is

    The commonest idiopathic (primary) photodermatosis: a recurrent, itchy, non-scarring eruption on sun-exposed skin appearing hours to a couple of days after the first significant sun exposure of spring/summer or of a sunny holiday. Also called polymorphous light eruption or "prickly heat"/"sun allergy" by the public (not true prickly heat — that is miliaria). Affects roughly 10–20% of people in northern Europe; more common in women, onset usually in the teens to 30s; all skin types.

    Why it happens

    Key points for this step are in More detail below.

    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    How it’s caused

    A delayed-type (cell-mediated) immune reaction to a UV-induced skin antigen. Normally UV exposure suppresses skin immunity; in PMLE that UV-induced immunosuppression fails, so the newly photo-induced antigen provokes inflammation. UVA is the main trigger in most patients (UVB alone in a minority, both in some) — which is why a high-SPF but weak-UVA sunscreen fails (Case 1). Skin "hardens" with repeated exposure, so it is typically worse early in the season and improves through summer. Family history in a proportion.

    Next: What you’ll see
    3

    Step 3 of 9 · 1 min

    What you’ll see

    • Itch or burning often starting within hours (Case 1: 30 minutes); rash within hours to 1–3 days.
    • Morphology varies between patients but is usually consistent in one individual: small papules (commonest), papulovesicles, plaques, targetoid/EM-like lesions, rarely purpuric.
    • Sites: V of neck, upper chest, outer arms/forearms, dorsal hands, legs; face often relatively spared (habitually exposed, hardened).
    • Resolves over days–2 weeks without scarring if further sun is avoided; can spread and persist with continued exposure (Case 1 extension to trunk).
    • Variant: juvenile spring eruption (papulovesicles on helices of ears in boys).

    Confirm the diagnosis

    Key points for this step are in More detail below.

    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    Diagnosis

    Clinical: timing (first strong sun), photodistribution, recurrent seasonal pattern, normal health. In severe, atypical or treatment-resistant cases specialists consider: ANA, anti-Ro/La antibodies (exclude lupus — SCLE can be triggered by sun and by drugs), porphyrin screen (exclude erythropoietic protoporphyria/other porphyrias), skin biopsy (dermal oedema, perivascular lymphocytes — non-specific but helps exclude lupus), and phototesting/provocation testing in a photobiology unit. Always take a full drug history to exclude drug-induced photosensitivity.

    Step 5 of 9 · 1 min

    Lookalikes

    • Sunburn

      Uniform painful erythema; no papules; dose-related; within hours, peaks 24 h

    • Drug-induced photosensitivity

      New photosensitiser (doxycycline, thiazide, NSAID…); "exaggerated sunburn" or eczema; ask about drugs

      Open lesson

    • Cutaneous lupus (SCLE/DLE)

      Persistent annular/psoriasiform or scarring plaques; ANA/Ro positive; lasts weeks–months

    • Solar urticaria

      Wheals within minutes of exposure, fade within hours

    • Actinic prurigo

      Childhood onset, excoriated papules/nodules incl. covered sites, cheilitis, persists into winter

    • Erythema multiforme

      Targets on palms/soles; HSV/drug trigger; not photodistributed

      Open lesson

    • Miliaria (true prickly heat)

      Heat/sweat, covered/occluded sites, tiny vesicles

      Open lesson

    • Airborne contact dermatitis / CAD

      Eczematous, chronic; shaded sites involved (airborne) or older man, lichenified (CAD)

    Step 6 of 9 · 1 min

    Treat

    Treatment ladder

    1. 1

      FoundationSelf-care / OTC

      • Avoid further sun until settled; cool showers/compresses, emollient.
      • Oral non-sedating antihistamine for itch (helps itch, not the rash) — hydroxyzine in Case 1 had limited effect.
      • OTC hydrocortisone 1% is usually too weak for established PMLE on limbs/trunk.
    2. 2

      Step 1First-line Rx

      • Short course (5–7 days, up to ~2 weeks) of a potent topical corticosteroid (e.g. betamethasone valerate 0.1%, mometasone 0.1%) to the trunk/limbs; avoid potent on face — use moderate for ≤5–7 days if needed.
      • Severe extensive flares: a short course of oral prednisolone may be used (GP/specialist decision; e.g. a few days at the start of a holiday is a recognised specialist strategy for predictable attacks).
    3. 3

      SpecialistRed flags / refer

      • Diagnostic doubt (rule out lupus/porphyria), scarring, persistent >2–3 weeks without sun, systemic features, failure of potent topical steroid, severe recurrent PMLE affecting quality of life (phototherapy referral).

    Doses as in the source notes. Always confirm in the current BNF and local formulary.

    More detailfull notes · 2 min

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    Self-care / OTC

    • Avoid further sun until settled; cool showers/compresses, emollient.
    • Oral non-sedating antihistamine for itch (helps itch, not the rash) — hydroxyzine in Case 1 had limited effect.
    • OTC hydrocortisone 1% is usually too weak for established PMLE on limbs/trunk.

    First-line Rx

    • Short course (5–7 days, up to ~2 weeks) of a potent topical corticosteroid (e.g. betamethasone valerate 0.1%, mometasone 0.1%) to the trunk/limbs; avoid potent on face — use moderate for ≤5–7 days if needed.
    • Severe extensive flares: a short course of oral prednisolone may be used (GP/specialist decision; e.g. a few days at the start of a holiday is a recognised specialist strategy for predictable attacks).

    Prevention (the key intervention)

    • Graded, gradual sun exposure in spring ("natural hardening").
    • Broad-spectrum SPF 30–50+ with high UVA protection (UK 4–5 star UVA or EU "UVA" circle logo), applied generously 15–30 min before going out and re-applied every 2 h and after swimming.
    • Clothing, hat, shade, avoid 11:00–15:00; UVA passes through glass (car side windows, conservatory).
    • Severe recurrent: referral for prophylactic phototherapy ("hardening") with narrowband UVB or PUVA in spring; hydroxychloroquine or immunosuppressants are rarely used (specialist).

    Red flags / refer

    Diagnostic doubt (rule out lupus/porphyria), scarring, persistent >2–3 weeks without sun, systemic features, failure of potent topical steroid, severe recurrent PMLE affecting quality of life (phototherapy referral).

    Sun-protection counselling script

    SPF is not the only measure of sunscreen quality — choose by UVA protection too.

    8

    Step 8 of 9 · 1 min

    Prescriber pearls

    • "It's not an allergy to the sun cream — it's a reaction to UVA the cream didn't block." Check the UVA rating, not just SPF.

    • Expect recurrence each spring/holiday but often milder later in summer; tends to improve over years.

    • Vitamin D: if strict sun avoidance, advise 10 micrograms (400 units) daily supplement (UK public health advice).

    • Always run a quick drug check — a new doxycycline or thiazide changes the diagnosis.
