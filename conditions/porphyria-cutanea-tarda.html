    Porphyria cutanea tarda (& pseudoporphyria)

    Fragile skin, blisters, erosions, scars and milia on the backs of the hands, with facial hair growth and dark urine. Look for alcohol, hepatitis C, haemochromatosis, oestrogens and HIV; pseudoporphyria from drugs looks the same.

    Also calledPCTporphyriablisters back of handsskin fragility sunhypertrichosisdark urine+4
    Related pagesMiliaSkin fragility & dermatoporosisDrug-induced photosensitivityEpidermolysis bullosa (EB)Bullous pemphigoid (incl. MMP & pemphigoid gestationis)

    Key facts

    • Porphyria cutanea tarda (PCT) is the commonest porphyria: blisters, erosions, skin fragility, scars and milia on the backs of the hands.
    • Also facial hypertrichosis and hyperpigmentation; urine may be dark.
    • Triggers: alcohol, oestrogens, hepatitis C, HIV, iron overload (haemochromatosis), smoking.
    • Confirm: plasma and urine porphyrins; check ferritin, LFT, hepatitis C, HFE genotype.
    • Specialist: venesection (iron reduction) or low-dose hydroxychloroquine; remove triggers; visible-light protection.

    Overview

    The commonest porphyria worldwide: a blistering photosensitivity with skin fragility caused by reduced activity of the hepatic haem-pathway enzyme uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase (UROD). Usually presents in middle-aged adults (more often men). Sporadic/acquired (type 1, ~75–80%) or familial (type 2, inherited heterozygous UROD mutation) — the latter still needs acquired triggers to become clinically active. A non-acute porphyria: no neurovisceral attacks.

    Epidemiology

    • Who
      • Adults over 40; about 1 in 10,000–25,000; familial form in about a fifth.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    When hepatic UROD activity falls below ~20–25% of normal, uroporphyrin and heptacarboxyl porphyrins accumulate in the liver, spill into plasma and urine, and deposit in skin. Porphyrins absorb strongly in the Soret band (~400–410 nm, visible violet light), generating reactive oxygen species → damage at the dermo-epidermal junction → fragility and subepidermal blisters. Triggers / associations (mostly hepatic iron and oxidative stress):

    • Alcohol (Case 2: ~40 units/week) and smoking (Case 2: 10/day)
    • Iron overload — including HFE haemochromatosis mutations
    • Hepatitis C (also hepatitis B), HIV
    • Oestrogens (COC, HRT, prostate cancer therapy)
    • Chronic kidney disease/haemodialysis; occasionally hepatocellular carcinoma ("hepatic porphyrinoma"); polyhalogenated aromatic hydrocarbons (historical)

    Liver is the internal organ most affected: steatosis, siderosis, fibrosis/cirrhosis and raised risk of hepatocellular carcinoma.

    Myths to address

    • "Sunscreen protects": ordinary sunscreens do not block the visible light (around 400 nm) that excites porphyrins.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    Reduced activity of uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase in the liver lets light-sensitive porphyrins build up in the skin.

    1. 1

      Enzyme inhibition

      Iron and oxidative stress (alcohol, hepatitis C, oestrogen) inhibit hepatic UROD.

    2. 2

      Porphyrin accumulation

      Uroporphyrins accumulate in the liver, blood and skin.

    3. 3

      Phototoxicity

      Visible light (about 400–410 nm) excites porphyrins, generating reactive oxygen that damages the dermo-epidermal junction.

    4. 4

      Fragility and blisters

      Minor trauma causes subepidermal blisters that heal with scars and milia.

    Histopathology

    • Cell-poor subepidermal blister with 'festooning' of dermal papillae; caterpillar bodies.

    Clinical features

    • Tense vesicles and bullae (some haemorrhagic) on dorsal hands and fingers, forearms, face, and scalp/ears in bald men — often after minor knocks.
    • Skin fragility: erosions and crusts from trivial trauma; slow healing over weeks.
    • Milia, atrophic and white/pink scars on dorsal hands.
    • Hypertrichosis of temples/cheeks, mottled hyper/hypopigmentation, sclerodermoid thickening (late).
    • Dark, red-brown or "tea"/port-wine urine; fluoresces pink-red under Wood's lamp.
    • Worse in summer (Case 2) — but patients often don't link it to sun, because the trigger is visible light through windows and damage is delayed.

    Special groups

    • Pseudoporphyria
      • Same skin signs with normal porphyrins: naproxen, tetracyclines, furosemide, sunbeds, dialysis.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    • Most relevant first test: urine (and/or plasma) porphyrins — raised uroporphyrin and heptacarboxylic porphyrin; faecal isocoproporphyrin raised. Plasma fluorescence emission peak ~619–620 nm distinguishes PCT from variegate porphyria (~626 nm) — this matters because variegate porphyria can cause acute attacks. ALA and porphobilinogen normal.
    • Associated-cause work-up: LFTs, ferritin + transferrin saturation, HFE genotyping, hepatitis C and B, HIV serology, glucose/HbA1c, renal function, consider liver ultrasound (± HCC surveillance if cirrhotic), ANA if diagnostic overlap.
    • Skin biopsy (subepidermal blister with "festooning" of dermal papillae; immunofluorescence) when needed to exclude other bullous disease.
    • Sent via specialist porphyria laboratory; UK National Acute Porphyria Service / specialist dermatology.

    Pseudoporphyria and EBA

    Note

    Full PCT coverage

    (pathophysiology, urine porphyrin testing, venesection, low-dose hydroxychloroquine, visible-light sunscreen) is in the photodermatoses notes. Only the case-specific points and the mimics are covered here.

    What it is

    PCT is the commonest porphyria. It comes from reduced hepatic uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase (UROD) activity, which is acquired (type 1, ~80%) or familial (type 2). Uroporphyrins build up and cause blistering photosensitivity on sun-exposed skin.

    How it is caused / pathophysiology

    Porphyrins absorb visible light (Soret band ~400–410 nm), which damages the dermal–epidermal junction, so the skin becomes fragile and blisters below the epidermis. Triggers that the history in Case 3 points to:

    • alcohol (dependence)
    • hepatitis C and HIV (homelessness and possible injecting drug use, which may also explain the forearm scars)
    • iron overload / HFE haemochromatosis
    • oestrogens
    • smoking

    Clinical features & typical sites

    • fragile skin, tense and haemorrhagic blisters and erosions on the backs of the hands after minor trauma (Case 3; photo D01, and the photodermatoses notes B01–B06)
    • healing with scarring, milia and dyspigmentation
    • facial hypertrichosis (upper cheeks and temples)
    • photo-distributed hyperpigmentation or "tanned" face
    • morphoea-like sclerodermoid plaques later on
    • dark or red-brown urine

    How to diagnose

    Urine porphyrins (a random urine sample protected from light; it may glow pink under Wood's lamp), plasma porphyrins with a plasma fluorescence emission scan (peak ~619 nm) to separate PCT from variegate porphyria, and stool porphyrins. Add LFT, ferritin/transferrin saturation, HFE genotype, HCV, HBV and HIV serology, glucose and liver ultrasound (HCC risk). Skin biopsy with DIF (perivascular IgG/C3) is not usually needed. Specialist support: UK Porphyria Medicines Information Service / National Acute Porphyria Service, Cardiff and King's.

    Key differentials

    Differentials for Case 3:

    • Pseudoporphyria: looks the same but porphyrins are normal. Drug causes are naproxen and other NSAIDs, tetracyclines, nalidixic acid, furosemide, retinoids, and sunbeds. Also seen in dialysis. Stop the drug (Module 5 exam Q2).
    • Variegate porphyria / hereditary coproporphyria: the skin looks the same, but there is a risk of acute neurovisceral attacks with porphyrinogenic drugs. This is why the plasma scan matters before choosing drugs.
    • EBA: trauma-induced acral blisters, scarring and milia; porphyrins normal; linear IgG on the dermal floor of salt-split skin.
    • BP, bullous impetigo, friction or trauma blisters (rough sleeping), bullous drug eruption, bullosis diabeticorum, burns (cigarettes in intoxication), and erythropoietic protoporphyria (painful, burning, no blisters, children).

    Management for pharmacist/IP (UK)

    • Refer to dermatology or hepatology (routine to urgent). Treat the drivers: alcohol cessation (local alcohol services, thiamine), HCV direct-acting antivirals (which often cure PCT), and stop oestrogens. Specialists then use venesection (to ferritin ~20 µg/L) or low-dose hydroxychloroquine 100–200 mg twice weekly (not daily, which causes hepatotoxicity), with eye screening per the RCOphth schedule. Visible-light protection: reflectant tinted sunscreen such as zinc/titanium with iron oxides; standard UV sunscreen is not enough.
    • Social context for Case 3: homelessness makes venesection and follow-up hard. Arrange a GP registration route, link with the hepatology outreach or HCV peer-support team, and give gloves and hand protection.

    Prescriber pearls & counselling

    • Blisters on the backs of the hands plus hypertrichosis or dark urine: check porphyrins and do a medication review (NSAIDs, so think pseudoporphyria).
    • The porphyria drug-safety database (Welsh Medicines Information / NAPS) matters for acute porphyrias, not for PCT. Until the plasma scan excludes variegate porphyria, prescribe cautiously.

    Management

    Refer — PCT diagnosis and treatment are specialist (dermatology ± hepatology/porphyria service). The pharmacist/IP role:

    • Recognise the pattern (blisters + fragility + scars/milia on dorsal hands; alcohol/smoking/oestrogen/hep C) and refer with suspicion stated; topical steroids don't help (Case 2).
    • Remove triggers: stop alcohol and smoking (offer NHS stop-smoking support and alcohol brief intervention), review oestrogens (COC/HRT — discuss alternatives with prescriber), stop iron supplements and iron-containing multivitamins.
    • Specialist treatments (know, don't initiate): venesection (reduce ferritin), low-dose hydroxychloroquine (e.g. 100 mg–200 mg twice weekly, not standard daily antimalarial doses, which can cause hepatotoxicity), treatment of hepatitis C with direct-acting antivirals often induces remission. Chloroquine sometimes used at low dose.
    • Visible-light photoprotection: conventional chemical UV filters don't cover 400–410 nm — recommend reflectant sunscreens containing titanium dioxide/zinc oxide, ideally tinted (iron-oxide), e.g. Dundee cream (via specialists) or tinted mineral SPF 50; gloves, long sleeves, hats; window film for car/home.
    • Wound care for erosions (non-adherent dressings, watch for infection); protect hands from trauma (gloves for DIY/gardening).

    Referral

    • Urgent

      Signs of chronic liver disease or a known liver mass: urgent review.

    • Don’t

      Ordinary sunscreens do not block visible light: use opaque or tinted (iron oxide) protection.

    • Don’t

      Do not use standard-dose hydroxychloroquine: only low-dose under specialist advice (risk of hepatotoxicity).

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Signs of chronic liver disease or a known liver mass: urgent review.
    • Ordinary sunscreens do not block visible light: use opaque or tinted (iron oxide) protection.
    • Do not use standard-dose hydroxychloroquine: only low-dose under specialist advice (risk of hepatotoxicity).

    Red flags / refer

    All suspected PCT → routine-soon dermatology with urine porphyrins requested in primary care if local pathway allows. Urgent if abdominal pain, neuropathy, psychiatric features or hyponatraemia (possible acute porphyria — check drug safety via the Welsh Medicines Advice/UK Porphyria Medicines Information Service database). Signs of decompensated liver disease → urgent.

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Scarring, cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma (in liver disease).

    Prognosis

    • Remits with iron depletion or hydroxychloroquine; relapse if triggers return.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • Blisters on the back of the hands in a drinker = check porphyrins; "worse in summer" but light through glass counts.

    • Pseudoporphyria is the pharmacist's catch: look for naproxen, doxycycline, furosemide or sunbed use — normal porphyrins, resolves (slowly, months) after withdrawal.

    • Before supplying drugs to anyone with a porphyria diagnosis, check porphyria drug safety lists (BNF "Acute porphyrias" section and UK Porphyria Medicines Information Service) — strictly relevant to acute porphyrias, but confirm the type.

    • Ask about urine colour.

    • Long-term: alcohol abstinence reduces relapse; screen for HCC if cirrhosis.

    Self-check quiz

    5 questions · optional
    1. Q1When should you think of Porphyria cutanea tarda (& pseudoporphyria)?Reveal

      Blisters and erosions on the backs of the hands after minor knocks, healing with scars and milia; facial hypertrichosis; reddish-brown urine.

    2. Q2What drives it?Reveal

      Reduced hepatic uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase activity (iron, alcohol, hepatitis C, oestrogen) causes porphyrin build-up that reacts with visible light.

    3. Q3What is the backbone of care?Reveal

      Refer for porphyrin testing; stop alcohol and oestrogens; screen liver, iron, hepatitis B/C and HIV; specialist venesection or low-dose hydroxychloroquine; visible-light protection.

    4. Q4What must you never miss?Reveal

      Underlying liver disease or hepatocellular carcinoma; haemochromatosis; pseudoporphyria from naproxen or other drugs.

    5. Q5Name a red flag that needs escalation.Reveal

      Signs of chronic liver disease or a known liver mass: urgent review.
