    Port-wine stain (capillary malformation)

    A flat red or purple capillary malformation present at birth that grows with the child and never involutes. Facial V1 stains need assessment for Sturge–Weber syndrome; pulsed dye laser is specialist.

    Also calledport-wine staincapillary malformationnaevus flammeusSturge-WeberKlippel-Trénaunayvascular birthmark
    Related pagesInfantile haemangioma (strawberry mark)Pigmented & epidermal birthmarksNeonatal pustular rashesIntense pulsed light (IPL)

    Key facts

    • Port-wine stain is a capillary malformation, present at birth, that grows with the child and never involutes.
    • Caused by a somatic GNAQ mutation; about 0.3% of newborns.
    • Darkens and thickens with age (cobblestoning, overgrowth).
    • Facial V1 distribution: assess for Sturge–Weber (glaucoma, seizures); limb stain with overgrowth: consider Klippel–Trénaunay.
    • Pulsed dye laser (specialist) lightens it, best started early; camouflage for all.

    Overview

    A port-wine stain (PWS) is a capillary malformation: present at birth as a flat red or purple patch, it grows in proportion with the child, may darken and thicken with age, and never involutes. It affects about 0.3% of newborns.

    Epidemiology

    • Who
      • Equal sexes; any site, face commonest.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    Dilated, malformed dermal capillaries, usually from a somatic mosaic GNAQ mutation; reduced nerve supply to the vessels lets them dilate progressively.

    Myths to address

    • "It's a stork mark that will fade": salmon patches fade; port-wine stains do not.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    A port-wine stain is a patch of malformed, progressively widening capillaries.

    1. 1

      Mosaic mutation

      A GNAQ mutation in a developing cell gives a patch of abnormal vessels.

    2. 2

      Ectatic capillaries

      Dermal capillaries are dilated and increase in calibre with age.

    3. 3

      Reduced neural control

      Poor sympathetic innervation of the vessels allows progressive dilation.

    4. 4

      Thickening

      Over decades vessels enlarge further, causing darker colour, blebs and soft-tissue overgrowth.

    Histopathology

    • Dilated capillaries and venules in the papillary and reticular dermis without endothelial proliferation.

    Clinical features

    • Flat, well-demarcated pink, red or purple patch, often unilateral and following a dermatome-like segment.
    • With age: darker colour, thickening and cobblestone nodules (vascular blebs), soft-tissue overgrowth.
    • Blanches partly on pressure; does not grow out of proportion.

    Special groups

    • Sturge–Weber syndrome
      • V1 facial PWS with leptomeningeal angiomatosis and glaucoma: seizures, hemiparesis, learning difficulties.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    Clinical. MRI brain and eye review for facial V1 stains (Sturge–Weber); limb imaging and leg-length check for limb stains with overgrowth.

    Differential diagnoses

    Look-alikeDistinguishing clues
    Salmon patch (naevus simplex)Pale pink, midline (nape, glabella, eyelids); fades in the first years
    Infantile haemangioma (see Infantile haemangioma)Absent or faint at birth, then rapid growth and involution
    Venous malformationBlue, compressible, swells when dependent

    Management

    • Reassure for small, uncomplicated stains; camouflage cosmetics.
    • Pulsed dye laser is specialist, and earlier childhood referral is often preferred.
    • Facial PWS in the V1 distribution: consider Sturge–Weber (neurology and ophthalmology for glaucoma and seizures).
    • Limb PWS with overgrowth: consider Klippel–Trénaunay and related syndromes.

    Treatment ladder

    1. 1

      Reassure

      Explain permanence; camouflage.

    2. 2

      Laser

      Pulsed dye laser via specialist; earlier childhood treatment often preferred.

    Referral

    • Urgent

      Facial V1 stain: ophthalmology (glaucoma) and neurology (Sturge–Weber).

    • Routine

      Limb stain with overgrowth or asymmetry.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Facial segmental port-wine stain with eye or neurological symptoms (seizures, glaucoma).
    • Limb asymmetry or overgrowth.

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Glaucoma, seizures (Sturge–Weber); limb overgrowth; bleeding from blebs; psychosocial impact.

    Prognosis

    • Permanent; laser lightens but rarely clears.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • A malformation is not a tumour: be clear with parents whether it is a PWS or a haemangioma.

    • PWS does not fade; salmon patches do.

    Self-check quiz

    4 questions · optional
    1. Q1Does a port-wine stain fade?Reveal

      No; it grows with the child and may darken and thicken.

    2. Q2Which mutation is usually responsible?Reveal

      Somatic mosaic GNAQ.

    3. Q3Which syndrome must be considered with a V1 facial stain?Reveal

      Sturge–Weber syndrome.

    4. Q4How is a salmon patch different?Reveal

      Pale, midline and fades in early childhood.
