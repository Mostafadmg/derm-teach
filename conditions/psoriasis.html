    Psoriasis

    Sharply defined, scaly plaques caused by IL-23/IL-17 inflammation. Manage with topical steroid and vitamin D, steroid breaks, and screening for joints and comorbidities.

    Also calledplaque psoriasisguttate psoriasisscalp psoriasisnail psoriasisflexural psoriasisinverse psoriasis+1

    Key facts

    • Chronic, relapsing, immune-mediated inflammatory disease of skin, nails and joints.
    • Affects about 2–3% of the UK population; onset peaks at 15–25 and 50–60 years.
    • Driven by the IL-23 → Th17 → IL-17 axis: epidermis turns over in 3–5 days instead of about 28.
    • Classic sign: well-demarcated salmon-pink plaques with silvery scale on elbows, knees, scalp and lower back.
    • UK care follows NICE CG153: emollient + potent steroid + vitamin D analogue, with planned steroid breaks; screen yearly for psoriatic arthritis (PEST).

    Overview

    Psoriasis is a chronic, non-contagious, immune-mediated inflammatory disease that relapses and remits. It mainly causes sharply defined, red, scaly plaques, but it is a multisystem disease affecting the nails and joints and linked with cardiometabolic disease and depression.

    • Chronic plaque psoriasis is the most common type (about 80–90%).
    • Other types: guttate, flexural (inverse), scalp, palmoplantar, nail; rare emergencies are erythrodermic and generalised pustular psoriasis.
    • Up to 30% of people with psoriasis develop psoriatic arthritis.
    • Diagnosis is clinical; management is long term and stepped, from topicals to phototherapy, systemic drugs and biologics.

    Epidemiology

    • Prevalence
      • About 1–3% of the UK population (2–4% in many Western populations); up to about 4% worldwide.
      • Less common in tropical regions and in people of African or East Asian descent.
    • Age
      • Can start at any age, including childhood (prevalence 0.5–2% in children).
      • Two onset peaks: 15–25 years (early onset, often familial, HLA-C*06:02) and 50–60 years.
    • Sex
      • Males and females are affected about equally.
    • Family history
      • About one third of patients have an affected relative.
    • Severity
      • Roughly 60% of patients have mild disease, 30% moderate and 10% severe.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    Psoriasis is multifactorial: a genetic predisposition plus environmental triggers that switch on an immune response in the skin.

    Genetic factors

    • HLA-C*06:02 (HLA-Cw6) is strongly linked with early-onset and guttate psoriasis (not with nail or late-onset disease).
    • Other susceptibility genes affect skin barrier (e.g. LCE3B/LCE3C deletion) and the IL-23/IL-17 pathway.

    Triggers and aggravating factors

    • Infection
      • Streptococcal sore throat (guttate psoriasis); HIV can cause severe or atypical disease.
    • Skin injury: the Koebner phenomenon
      • New plaques at sites of cuts, scratches, surgery, burns, sunburn or friction.
    • Drugs
      • Beta-blockers, lithium, antimalarials (hydroxychloroquine, chloroquine), interferons, sometimes NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors and terbinafine.
      • Abrupt withdrawal of systemic (or potent topical) corticosteroids: rebound and pustular flares.
    • Lifestyle
      • Smoking, excess alcohol, obesity, stress.
    • Cold, dry weather; sun usually helps, but about 10% of patients are worse with sun.

    Prescriber tip

    Drug-triggered flare after an MI

    With post-MI polypharmacy the beta-blocker is the prime suspect, but do not stop cardiology-critical drugs without discussing with the GP or cardiologist.

    Associated conditions

    • Psoriatic arthritis and spondyloarthropathy.
    • Cardiovascular disease and metabolic syndrome (obesity, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, type 2 diabetes); psoriasis is an independent cardiovascular risk factor, especially when severe.
    • Inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn's, ulcerative colitis), uveitis, coeliac disease, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease.
    • Depression and anxiety; higher alcohol use.
    • NICE notes a higher VTE risk in severe psoriasis.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    Psoriasis is a self-amplifying loop between immune cells and keratinocytes.

    1. 1

      A trigger activates the innate immune system

      Injury, infection or drugs stress keratinocytes. Plasmacytoid dendritic cells, keratinocytes and macrophages release interferon-α, TNF-α and other signals that activate myeloid dendritic cells in the dermis.

    2. 2

      Dendritic cells release IL-23 and IL-12

      Activated dendritic cells produce IL-23, which drives the survival and expansion of Th17 (and Th22) cells, and IL-12, which drives Th1 cells. The IL-23/Th17 pathway is the dominant one.

    3. 3

      IL-17 and IL-22 drive keratinocytes

      Th17 cells (and γδ T cells, ILC3s, CD8 cells, neutrophils and mast cells) release IL-17A, IL-17F and IL-22. Keratinocytes proliferate and release chemokines (e.g. CCL20) and antimicrobial peptides that recruit more immune cells: a feed-forward loop. TNF-α amplifies every step.

    4. 4

      The epidermis thickens and capillaries dilate

      Keratinocytes cycle in 3–5 days instead of about 28. The epidermis thickens (acanthosis) with long, regular rete ridges; papillary capillaries become dilated and tortuous, which explains the Auspitz sign (pinpoint bleeding when scale is removed).

    5. 5

      Immature cells make silvery scale

      Cells reach the surface still holding their nuclei (parakeratosis) and fail to release the lipids that bind corneocytes, so the stratum corneum flakes off as silvery scale. Neutrophils collect in small pockets (Munro microabscesses); IL-36 overactivity tips the balance towards pustular psoriasis.

    How treatments map onto the mechanism

    • Topical steroids: broad anti-inflammatory action on the whole loop.
    • Vitamin D analogues (calcipotriol): slow keratinocyte proliferation and normalise differentiation.
    • Coal tar, dithranol, salicylic acid: antiproliferative and keratolytic.
    • Phototherapy (NB-UVB): induces T-cell apoptosis in the skin.
    • Biologics: TNF-α inhibitors, IL-17 inhibitors, IL-23 (p19) and IL-12/23 (p40) inhibitors block the central cytokines; TYK2 and PDE4 inhibitors act on intracellular signalling.

    Histopathology

    • Regular acanthosis with elongated, club-shaped rete ridges.
    • Parakeratosis (retained nuclei in the stratum corneum) with loss of the granular layer.
    • Munro microabscesses (neutrophils in the stratum corneum) and spongiform pustules of Kogoj.
    • Thinned suprapapillary plates and dilated, tortuous papillary capillaries (Auspitz sign).
    • Perivascular lymphocytic infiltrate. Biopsy is only needed if the diagnosis is uncertain.

    Clinical features

    Symptoms

    • Itch is usually mild to moderate but can be severe; scratching causes lichenification.
    • Painful fissures, especially on palms, soles and in flexures.
    • Scaling and shedding; joint pain, stiffness or swelling suggests psoriatic arthritis.
    • Major psychosocial burden: embarrassment, low mood, impact on work and relationships.

    Lesions and morphology

    • Well-demarcated plaque

      Sharply defined, raised, salmon-pink plaque, usually symmetrical. The sharp edge helps separate it from eczema.

    • Silvery scale

      Thick, white, micaceous scale; removing it causes pinpoint bleeding (Auspitz sign).

    • Koebner phenomenon

      New lesions appear in lines along scratches or scars (also seen in lichen planus and vitiligo).

    • Nail changes

      Pitting, onycholysis, oil-drop discolouration, subungual hyperkeratosis, crumbling.

    • Post-inflammatory change

      Plaques may leave pale or dark marks that fade over months, especially in skin of colour.

    Distribution

    • Extensor surfaces: elbows and knees.
    • Scalp, often extending just beyond the hairline and behind the ears.
    • Lower back, natal cleft and umbilicus.
    • Nails (in up to half of patients, more with arthritis).
    • Flexures, genitals, palms and soles in specific types.

    Variants and subtypes

    • Chronic plaque

      Persistent plaques > 3 cm on extensors, scalp and lower back; from mild to very extensive.

    • Guttate

      Many small raindrop plaques on trunk and limbs, often 2–3 weeks after a strep throat; may clear in months or evolve into plaque disease.

    • Scalp

      Thick adherent scale; often the first or only site; extends beyond the hairline.

    • Flexural (inverse)

      Smooth, shiny, well-defined red patches with little or no scale in axillae, groin, natal cleft and under the breasts; fissure in the crease; may be colonised by Candida.

    • Palmoplantar

      Well-defined scaly plaques or diffuse hyperkeratosis with painful fissures; disproportionately disabling.

    • Nail psoriasis

      Pitting, onycholysis and oil-drop sign; a marker of higher psoriatic arthritis risk.

    • Sebopsoriasis

      Overlap with seborrhoeic dermatitis on the scalp, face, ears and chest.

    • Unstable / pustular

      Rapid extension, new plaques or pustules, often after steroid withdrawal or infection.

    • Erythrodermic

      Confluent redness and scaling over > 90% of the skin; temperature dysregulation, fluid loss, heart failure. Same-day assessment.

    • Generalised pustular (von Zumbusch)

      Sheets of sterile pustules on red skin with fever and malaise. Same-day assessment.

    Special groups

    • Skin of colour
      • Plaques are often thicker with more prominent grey or silvery scale and look violaceous, grey or dark brown rather than pink; erythema is harder to judge.
      • Post-inflammatory hyper- or hypopigmentation is marked and can last longer than the plaques.
      • Involvement is often more extensive; palmoplantar, pustular and erythrodermic forms may be more frequent.
    • Children
      • Guttate and scalp/flexural/napkin disease are common; NICE recommends specialist referral at presentation.
    • Pregnancy
      • Psoriasis often improves but may flare after delivery; acitretin and methotrexate are teratogenic (specialist planning).
    • HIV
      • Can be extensive and recalcitrant; consider HIV testing in sudden severe psoriasis.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    Diagnosis is clinical, based on morphology, distribution, nails and joints.

    History

    • Onset, course, previous treatments and response; triggers (sore throat, drugs, stress, steroid withdrawal).
    • Joint symptoms: pain, swelling, morning stiffness, heel pain, dactylitis.
    • Family history; smoking, alcohol, weight; mood; impact on work and relationships.

    Examination

    • Morphology and body surface area (BSA): the patient's palm with fingers is about 1% BSA.
    • High-impact sites: face, scalp, hands, feet, nails, genitals.
    • Nails, scalp, natal cleft and umbilicus for supporting clues; joints for swelling or dactylitis.

    Severity and impact scoring

    ToolWhat it measures
    BSAExtent: the patient's palm ≈ 1%. > 10% suggests moderate to severe disease.
    PASIPsoriasis Area and Severity Index (0–72): redness, thickness, scale × area; used for systemic and biologic decisions.
    PGAStatic physician's or patient's global assessment (clear to severe); NICE suggests patient and clinician assessment.
    DLQIDermatology Life Quality Index (0–30); CDLQI for children.
    PESTPsoriasis Epidemiology Screening Tool: annual screen for psoriatic arthritis; score ≥ 3 → rheumatology.

    Investigations

    • Usually none.
    • Skin biopsy if the diagnosis is uncertain (e.g. to exclude mycosis fungoides or lupus).
    • Throat swab (or ASO titre) if guttate psoriasis follows a sore throat.
    • Skin scrapings and nail clippings for mycology when tinea or onychomycosis is possible.
    • Cardiovascular risk assessment (NICE: at presentation, and at least every 5 years if severe): BP, BMI, lipids, HbA1c.
    • Baseline bloods before systemic therapy are arranged by the specialist team.

    Management

    Principles

    • Follow NICE CG153 and the CKS summary. Psoriasis is chronic: treatment is intermittent and most patients relapse after stopping.
    • Agree goals; consider severity, sites, impact, patient preference and comorbidities.
    • Explain how much to apply and when (fingertip units), planned steroid breaks, and how long until response (about 4 weeks for topicals).
    • Review 4 weeks after starting a new topical (2 weeks in children).
    • Screen yearly for psoriatic arthritis and assess cardiovascular risk, mood and lifestyle.

    Severity

    • Mild: limited plaques managed with topicals.
    • Moderate to severe (e.g. BSA > 10%, PASI ≥ 10, DLQI > 10, high-impact sites, failure of topicals): consider referral for phototherapy or systemic therapy.

    Conservative and self-care

    • Emollients regularly and generously: reduce scale and itch and help active topicals work.
    • Stop smoking; moderate alcohol; support weight loss (it can reduce severity).
    • Avoid skin trauma and picking; manage stress.
    • Seek prompt review for unexplained joint pain or swelling.
    • Prescribing detail for emollients: emollients.

    Medical treatment: topical (adults)

    Potent topical corticosteroid + vitamin D analogue

    First line, trunk and limbs
    Dose
    Potent steroid once daily plus vitamin D analogue once daily, applied at different times of day, for up to 4 weeks.
    Use
    First-line for chronic plaque psoriasis on trunk and limbs.
    Cautions
    Potent steroid no longer than 8 weeks at any one site; leave a 4-week break between courses.
    Counselling
    Apply to plaques only; separate the two products in time (e.g. morning and evening).

    Vitamin D analogue (e.g. calcipotriol)

    Second step
    Dose
    Twice daily for 8–12 weeks if not controlled after 4 weeks of the combination above.
    Cautions
    Irritation, especially on the face and flexures; avoid excessive amounts (hypercalcaemia).
    Counselling
    Wash hands after use; can be used between steroid courses.

    Calcipotriol / betamethasone dipropionate

    Fixed combination (gel, foam, ointment)
    Dose
    Once daily for up to 4 weeks.
    Use
    Third step (or alternative to a potent steroid twice daily or coal tar) when the vitamin D analogue fails; helps adherence.
    Cautions
    Contains a potent steroid: same duration limits and breaks. Brands vary by formulary.
    Counselling
    Foam and gel suit the scalp and hairy areas; shake foam well.

    Very potent topical corticosteroid

    Specialist advice
    Cautions
    Usually on specialist advice; no longer than 4 weeks at a site; avoid face and flexures.
    Counselling
    Explain atrophy and rebound risk.

    Coal tar

    Antiproliferative, anti-scaling
    Use
    Alternative step on trunk, limbs and scalp (shampoo).
    Cautions
    Messy, odour, staining; coal tar shampoo alone is not enough for severe scalp disease.
    Counselling
    Use in the evening; may stain clothes and bedding.

    Dithranol (short-contact)

    Antiproliferative
    Use
    Hospital or specialist settings for large plaques.
    Cautions
    Burns and stains skin and fabrics; avoid face and flexures.

    Scalp

    • Start with a potent steroid in a suitable vehicle (lotion, gel or shampoo) for up to 4 weeks.
    • Next: calcipotriol/betamethasone combination or a vitamin D analogue.
    • Use a scale softener (e.g. salicylic acid or oil-based preparations) first for thick scale.

    Face, flexures and genitals

    • Mild or moderate potency steroid once or twice daily for a maximum of 2 weeks; flexures are at high risk of atrophy (about 1–2 weeks a month).
    • Do not use potent or very potent steroids here.
    • If response is poor or continuous steroid is needed: topical calcineurin inhibitor (tacrolimus or pimecrolimus) twice daily for up to 4 weeks, started by a clinician with psoriasis expertise (off-label).
    • Vitamin D analogues may help but can irritate; tar, dithranol and salicylic acid are usually too irritant in folds.

    Palms, soles and nails

    • Emollients and keratolytics (e.g. salicylic acid, urea); potent or very potent steroid ± vitamin D analogue or combination, with occlusion overnight.
    • Nail disease responds poorly to topicals; refer if severe or with joint symptoms.

    Medical treatment: systemic and phototherapy (specialist)

    Phototherapy (narrowband UVB; PUVA)

    Hospital-basedSpecialist only
    Use
    Plaque or guttate psoriasis not controlled with topicals; hand–foot PUVA for palmoplantar disease.
    Cautions
    Cumulative skin cancer risk, especially PUVA.

    Methotrexate

    Conventional systemic (often first choice)Specialist only
    Use
    Moderate to severe psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis.
    Cautions
    Once weekly dosing; folic acid; teratogenic; interactions (e.g. trimethoprim, NSAIDs).
    Monitoring
    FBC, LFTs, renal function; liver fibrosis assessment (specialist).

    Ciclosporin

    Conventional systemicSpecialist only
    Use
    Rapid control, or when planning conception.
    Cautions
    Nephrotoxicity, hypertension, many interactions.
    Monitoring
    BP and renal function.

    Acitretin

    Oral retinoidSpecialist only
    Use
    Especially pustular and palmoplantar psoriasis.
    Cautions
    Teratogenic for 3 years after stopping: pregnancy prevention required; lipids and LFTs.

    Apremilast, dimethyl fumarate, deucravacitinib

    Small molecules (PDE4, fumarate, TYK2)Specialist only
    Use
    Under NICE technology appraisals.

    Biologics

    TNF-α, IL-17, IL-23 and IL-12/23 inhibitorsSpecialist only
    Use
    Severe psoriasis that has failed or cannot use conventional systemic therapy and phototherapy (NICE TAs).
    Cautions
    Infection risk; screen for TB and hepatitis; no live vaccines.
    Counselling
    Patients on biologics: see biologics and systemics.

    Caution

    Avoid oral steroids

    Systemic corticosteroids are best avoided in psoriasis: withdrawal can trigger rebound, erythrodermic or pustular flares.

    Treatment ladder

    1. 1

      Everyone

      Emollients, lifestyle support, explanation and arthritis screening.

    2. 2

      Step 1 (4 weeks)

      Potent steroid OD + vitamin D analogue OD (different times of day) to trunk and limbs; potent steroid for scalp; mild/moderate steroid for face and flexures.

    3. 3

      Step 2 (8–12 weeks)

      Vitamin D analogue BD if not controlled.

    4. 4

      Step 3

      Potent steroid BD (up to 4 weeks) or coal tar, or calcipotriol/betamethasone OD (up to 4 weeks). Plan 4-week steroid breaks.

    5. 5

      Specialist

      Phototherapy, methotrexate, ciclosporin, acitretin, then small molecules and biologics.

    Psychological impact

    • Psoriasis is linked with depression, anxiety and suicidal ideation; quality-of-life impact can equal other major chronic diseases.
    • Ask about mood, alcohol and work at reviews; use the DLQI.
    • Signpost support (e.g. the Psoriasis Association) and refer for psychological support when needed.

    Referral (NICE CG153 / CKS)

    • Same day

      Erythrodermic or generalised pustular psoriasis.

    • Urgent

      Rapidly worsening or unstable psoriasis.

    • Routine

      Suspected psoriatic arthritis: rheumatology (urgent if active synovitis).

    • Routine

      Diagnostic uncertainty; extensive disease or failure of topicals; major impact on quality of life; severe nail disease; all children.

    • Routine

      Eligible for phototherapy or systemic therapy.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Same day

      Widespread redness over 90% of the skin (erythroderma), especially with shivering, feeling unwell or fluid loss.

    • Same day

      Sheets of pustules with fever (generalised pustular psoriasis), often after steroid withdrawal.

    • Urgent

      Hot, swollen joints or dactylitis (active psoriatic arthritis).

    • Routine

      Atypical plaques not responding to treatment (exclude mycosis fungoides or lupus).

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Psoriatic arthritis (10–30%): joint damage if untreated.
    • Cardiometabolic disease: ischaemic heart disease, stroke, hypertension, diabetes, chronic kidney disease (severe disease).
    • Erythroderma: fluid and electrolyte loss, hypothermia, infection, high-output heart failure.
    • Treatment-related: steroid atrophy, striae and rebound; phototherapy skin cancer risk; systemic drug toxicity; infection on biologics.
    • Secondary infection in flexures (Candida, bacteria); lichenification from scratching.
    • Psychological: depression, alcohol misuse, social isolation.

    Prognosis

    • A lifelong relapsing and remitting condition; about 17–55% of patients have remissions of varying length.
    • Guttate psoriasis often clears within months but may later evolve into plaque disease.
    • Severe psoriasis is associated with reduced life expectancy (about 3–4 years), largely through cardiovascular disease.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • Explain that psoriasis is chronic and treatment is intermittent; most patients relapse after stopping.

    • Demonstrate application; cosmetic acceptability drives adherence, so gels and foams often beat ointments for daytime use.

    • Spell out steroid breaks (4 weeks between potent courses) to prevent atrophy and rebound.

    • Calcipotriol/betamethasone products vary by formulary: check local brands.

    • Avoid oral steroids: withdrawal can trigger rebound or pustular flares.

    • Check the drug history for beta-blockers, lithium and antimalarials, but do not stop cardiology-critical drugs alone.

    • Counsel on vaccination and infection risk if the patient later moves to immunosuppression.

    • NICE notes a higher VTE risk in severe psoriasis.

    • Related: vitamin D analogues, tar and phototherapy, biologics and systemics, topical corticosteroids.

    Self-check quiz

    8 questions · optional
    1. Q1Which cytokine axis is central to psoriasis?Reveal

      IL-23 → Th17 → IL-17 (with TNF-α amplifying).

    2. Q2How fast do keratinocytes turn over in a plaque?Reveal

      About 3–5 days instead of about 28.

    3. Q3What is the Auspitz sign?Reveal

      Pinpoint bleeding when scale is removed, from dilated papillary capillaries.

    4. Q4First-line NICE topical regimen for trunk and limbs?Reveal

      Potent steroid once daily plus vitamin D analogue once daily, at different times, for up to 4 weeks.

    5. Q5Maximum potent steroid duration at one site?Reveal

      8 weeks (very potent 4 weeks), with a 4-week break between courses.

    6. Q6Which steroid potency for face and flexures?Reveal

      Mild or moderate only, for up to 2 weeks.

    7. Q7Which tool screens for psoriatic arthritis, and how often?Reveal

      PEST questionnaire, every year.

    8. Q8Two psoriasis emergencies?Reveal

      Erythrodermic and generalised pustular psoriasis: same-day assessment.
