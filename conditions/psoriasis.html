    Inflammatory skin

    Psoriasis

    Sharply defined, scaly plaques caused by IL-23/IL-17 inflammation. Manage with topical steroid and vitamin D, steroid breaks, and screening for joints and comorbidities.

    Adult core · deepened with Modules 3 & 4 · 32 images

    Also coversplaque psoriasisguttate psoriasisscalp psoriasisnail psoriasisflexural psoriasisinverse psoriasispsoriatic arthritis
    Think of it when

    Well-demarcated salmon-pink plaques with silvery scale on elbows, knees, scalp or lower back; nail pitting.

    What drives it

    IL-23/Th17/IL-17 inflammation driving very fast keratinocyte turnover (3–5 days instead of about a month).

    Backbone of care

    Emollient plus potent steroid and vitamin D analogue, with planned steroid breaks.

    Never miss

    Erythroderma or generalised pustular psoriasis (same day), and joint symptoms suggesting psoriatic arthritis.

    01

    What it is

    Psoriasis is a chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory skin disease that relapses and remits. It affects roughly 1–3% of the UK population. Onset peaks in young adulthood and again in mid-life, and about a third of patients have a family history.

    It can involve the nails and joints (psoriatic arthritis). It is associated with metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease, inflammatory bowel disease and depression.

    Note

    Main types

    Chronic plaque (most common, about 80–90%), guttate, flexural (inverse), scalp, palmoplantar and nail. Erythrodermic and generalised pustular psoriasis are rare emergencies.

    Palmoplantar psoriasis (Module 3 Week 6)

    • psoriasis localised to palms/soles, alone or with plaques elsewhere; disproportionately disabling (manual tasks, walking).
    02

    How it’s caused

    • Genetic predisposition (e.g. HLA-C*06:02, linked with early-onset and guttate disease) plus immune activation.
    • Key cytokine axis: IL-23 → Th17 → IL-17, plus TNF-α, causing keratinocyte hyperproliferation and inflammation.
    • Very fast epidermal turnover produces thick, scaly plaques.

    Triggers to ask about

    • Streptococcal throat infection (guttate psoriasis)
    • Trauma, scratches and surgery: the Koebner phenomenon
    • Drugs: lithium, beta-blockers, antimalarials, and abrupt withdrawal of systemic steroids
    • Smoking, alcohol, obesity and stress
    • HIV, which can cause severe or atypical disease

    Drug-triggered psoriasis (Module 4 Week 1)

    • Same IL-23/Th17-driven keratinocyte hyperproliferation as idiopathic psoriasis.
    • Drugs strongly linked: β-blockers (atenolol and others), lithium, antimalarials (e.g. hydroxychloroquine/chloroquine), interferons, rapid taper of systemic corticosteroids (rebound/pustular risk), sometimes NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors, and others with weaker evidence.
    • Post-MI polypharmacy: β-blocker is the prime suspect; do not stop cardiology-critical drugs without liaison.
    • Other triggers still apply: infection, trauma (Koebner), stress, smoking, alcohol, obesity.

    Palmoplantar psoriasis (Module 3 Week 6)

    • trauma/friction (Koebner — manual work), infection, stress, lithium, β-blockers, smoking, alcohol, obesity.
    03

    Clinical features

    TypeWhat you seeWhere
    Chronic plaqueSharply demarcated red plaques with silvery-white scale; pinpoint bleeding when scale is removed (Auspitz sign)Elbows, knees, lower back, scalp
    Flexural (inverse)Shiny, well-defined, moist, little or no scale; may be colonised by CandidaAxillae, groin, natal cleft, under the breasts
    GuttateMany small ‘raindrop’ plaques, often 2–3 weeks after a strep throatTrunk and limbs
    ScalpThick adherent scale, often extending beyond the hairlineScalp, hairline, behind the ears
    NailPitting, onycholysis, oil-drop discolouration, subungual hyperkeratosisFingernails and toenails

    Itch is usually mild to moderate but can be severe. Fissures on the palms and soles are painful. Nail disease is a clue to a higher risk of psoriatic arthritis.

    Prescriber tip

    Skin of colour

    Scale may be thicker and erythema harder to see, often looking violaceous or grey. Post-inflammatory pigment change is marked and can last longer than the plaques.

    Guttate psoriasis: spot diagnosis (Module 4 Week 1)

    Guttate psoriasisPityriasis rosea
    LesionsUniform raindrop scaly papules/plaquesHerald patch then smaller oval plaques with collarette
    DistributionTrunk + limbs; often spares palms/solesTrunk/proximal limbs; Christmas-tree back
    ScalePsoriaticFine peripheral collarette
    Strep HxCommonAbsent
    CourseWeeks–months; may clear or evolve to plaque psoriasisUsually clears 6–12 weeks

    Flexural (inverse) psoriasis (Module 3 Week 5)

    • Well-demarcated, deep red/shiny, smooth patches; scale often absent or minimal.
    • Fissure in crease depth common.
    • Sites: axillae, inframammary, groin, natal cleft, umbilicus, genitals, anus.
    • Check ears, scalp, elbows/knees, nails for supporting clues.
    • Complications: secondary yeast/bacteria, lichenification from itch (esp. perianal), sexual dysfunction/embarrassment, steroid atrophy from overuse.

    Palmoplantar psoriasis (Module 3 Week 6)

    • (1) well-defined red scaly plaques; (2) diffuse thick hyperkeratosis without much redness (acquired keratoderma). Painful fissures; sharp border; symmetrical. More frequent nail psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis — screen (PEST questionnaire).
    04

    Diagnosis

    • Clinical: morphology, distribution, nails and joints.
    • Assess: body surface area (BSA), high-impact sites (face, hands, genitals, scalp) and quality of life.
    • Screen for psoriatic arthritis every year with the PEST questionnaire, especially in the first 10 years.
    • Tests are rarely needed. Biopsy if uncertain. Take a throat swab if guttate psoriasis follows a recent sore throat.
    • Comorbidities: assess cardiovascular risk (NICE: at presentation, and at least every 5 years if severe), lifestyle and mood.

    Differentials

    Look-alikeDistinguishing clues
    Atopic eczemaIll-defined, flexural, intensely itchy, lichenified
    Seborrhoeic dermatitisGreasier scale on the central face; can overlap (‘sebopsoriasis’)
    Tinea corporisAsymmetrical with an advancing edge; mycology positive
    Lichen planusViolaceous, flat-topped papules with Wickham striae; wrists and mucosa
    Discoid eczemaCoin-shaped, very itchy, often on the legs
    Candidal intertrigo (vs flexural psoriasis)Satellite papules and pustules, macerated; flexural psoriasis is sharply defined with no satellites
    Cutaneous lymphomaPersistent, atypical plaques; refer

    Look-alikeDistinguishing clues

    CandidaSatellites; KOH/culture positive; rapid onset

    ErythrasmaBrownish, quiet itch, Wood lamp coral-red

    See also intertrigo and the flexural differential, pityriasis rosea and palmoplantar pustulosis.

    Palmoplantar psoriasis (Module 3 Week 6)

    • hyperkeratotic hand eczema (hardest — biopsy or patch testing sometimes needed; they can coexist), tinea (scrape), KE, PPP, keratoderma.
    06

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    Pharmacist / IP

    Aligned with NICE CG153 and the CKS summary.

    Self-care

    • Use emollients regularly. They reduce scale and itch and help active topicals work.
    • Lifestyle: weight loss if overweight, stopping smoking, moderating alcohol, and support with stress.
    • Seek review promptly for unexplained joint pain or swelling.

    First-line topicals: trunk and limbs (adults)

    1. Potent steroid once daily plus vitamin D analogue once daily, applied at different times of day, for up to 4 weeks.
    2. If not controlled: vitamin D analogue twice daily for 8–12 weeks.
    3. Then either a potent steroid twice daily (short course) or coal tar, or fixed-combination calcipotriol/betamethasone once daily for up to 4 weeks, which helps adherence.
    4. Plan a 4-week break between courses of potent or very potent steroid, using a non-steroid topical in between.
    5. Do not exceed the maximum durations: potent steroid ≤ 8 weeks per site; very potent ≤ 4 weeks, usually on specialist advice.

    Scalp

    Start with a potent steroid in a suitable formulation (lotion, gel or shampoo). Next steps are calcipotriol/betamethasone combination or a vitamin D analogue. Use scale softeners (e.g. salicylic acid) for thick scale. Coal tar shampoo alone is not enough for severe scalp disease.

    Face, flexures and genitals

    Use a mild to moderate steroid short term (up to about 2 weeks). Do not use potent or very potent steroids here. Experienced clinicians may use calcineurin inhibitors to spare steroids.

    Children

    NICE recommends specialist referral at presentation. Primary care options are limited by age and licensing.

    Specialist systemic therapy (not IP-initiated)

    Methotrexate (often the first conventional option), ciclosporin (for rapid control or when planning conception) and acitretin, followed by biologics or TYK2 inhibitors under NICE technology appraisals.

    Drug-triggered flare: what the IP does

    Align with NICE CG153 / CKS psoriasis (see core notes for full stepped topicals):

    • Emollients + potent TCS ± vitamin D analogue / fixed calcipotriol–betamethasone per NICE steps.
    • Do not unilaterally stop β-blocker or antiplatelet/statin — discuss with GP/cardiology; switch of antihypertensive class may be considered if psoriasis severe and temporally linked.
    • Refer if moderate–severe, topical failure, diagnostic doubt, or need for phototherapy/systemics.
    • Urgent: erythrodermic or generalised pustular psoriasis.

    Face, flexures and genitals (NICE CG153), in more depth

    Paraphrased NICE recommendations for face, flexures, genitals:

    1. Offer short-term mild or moderate potency topical corticosteroid once or twice daily for maximum 2 weeks.
    2. Flexures are high-risk for atrophy — limit corticosteroids to about 1–2 weeks per month; explain risks.
    3. Do not use potent or very potent corticosteroids on face, flexures, or genitals.
    4. If response unsatisfactory, or continuous steroid needed to hold control → offer a topical calcineurin inhibitor (tacrolimus/pimecrolimus) BD up to 4 weeks, initiated by a clinician with psoriasis expertise (off-label vs eczema licence historically — follow current BNF/local policy).
    5. Emollients always; treat secondary infection.
    6. Vitamin D analogues (e.g. calcipotriol) can help some flexural disease but may irritate — use carefully.
    7. Tar/dithranol/salicylic often too irritant in folds unless diluted/short-contact.
    8. Systemic/biologic therapy if extensive or QoL-severe — specialist.

    Palmoplantar psoriasis (Module 3 Week 6)

    • emollients and keratolytics (salicylic acid 2–5%, urea); potent/very potent TCS ± vitamin D analogue (calcipotriol) or combination products; occlusion overnight; lifestyle (smoking, alcohol, weight). Refer (CKS/NICE CG153): hand–foot PUVA, acitretin, methotrexate, ciclosporin, apremilast and biologics per NICE psoriasis TAs.
    07

    Red flags & referral

    Escalate
    • Erythroderma (widespread redness over 90% of the skin) or generalised pustular psoriasis: same-day dermatology or hospital assessment.
    • Suspected psoriatic arthritis (swollen or painful joints, dactylitis, enthesitis): refer to rheumatology.
    • BSA over 10%, moderate or worse severity, failure of topicals, or a major impact on quality of life: refer for phototherapy or systemics.
    • Severe nail disease, children, and diagnostic uncertainty: refer.
    08

    Prescriber pearls

    • Explain that psoriasis is chronic and treatment is intermittent. Most patients relapse after stopping.
    • Demonstrate how to apply treatment. Cosmetic acceptability drives adherence, so gels and foams often beat ointments for daytime use.
    • Spell out steroid breaks to prevent atrophy and rebound.
    • Calcipotriol/betamethasone combinations (Dovobet®-type gel, foam or ointment) vary by formulary, so check local brands.
    • Avoid oral steroids. Withdrawal can trigger rebound or pustular flares.
    • Counsel on vaccination and infection risk if the patient later moves to immunosuppression (specialist-led).
    • NICE notes a higher VTE risk in severe psoriasis, so keep it in mind.
    • Prescribing detail for vitamin D analogues, coal tar, dithranol and phototherapy: vitamin D analogues, tar and phototherapy; for patients on methotrexate or a biologic, see biologics and systemics.
