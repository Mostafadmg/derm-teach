    Pyoderma gangrenosum

    A rare, very painful, rapidly enlarging neutrophilic ulcer with a purple undermined edge. It is not an infection. Avoid debridement and refer urgently to dermatology.

    Also calledPGneutrophilic dermatosispathergyviolaceous undermined ulcerIBD skin ulcer
    Related pagesVenous leg ulcerArterial (ischaemic) leg ulcerApproach to cutaneous vasculitisErythema nodosum (incl. drug-induced)Biologics, JAK inhibitors & conventional systemics

    Key facts

    • Pyoderma gangrenosum (PG) is a rare neutrophilic dermatosis: a rapidly enlarging, very painful ulcer with a violaceous, undermined edge.
    • Not an infection and not gangrene; often starts as a pustule after minor trauma (pathergy).
    • About half have an associated disease: IBD, inflammatory arthritis, haematological disorders (MGUS, myeloma, leukaemia).
    • Diagnosis of exclusion: biopsy the edge, swab, exclude vasculitis, infection and malignancy.
    • Avoid debridement and surgery; urgent dermatology for potent topical/systemic steroids or ciclosporin, biologics.

    Overview

    Pyoderma gangrenosum (PG) is a rare neutrophilic, autoinflammatory ulcerating skin disease. Despite the name, it is not an infection. It causes a rapidly enlarging, very painful ulcer with a violaceous, undermined border, and shows pathergy: it worsens after trauma or surgery.

    Epidemiology

    • Who
      • Rare (about 1 in 100,000 per year); adults 20–50; slightly more women.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    Neutrophil dysfunction and autoinflammation. About half are idiopathic. Associations include:

    • Inflammatory bowel disease (ulcerative colitis more than Crohn’s)
    • Rheumatoid arthritis
    • Haematological malignancy and monoclonal gammopathy
    • Behçet disease and PAPA syndrome
    • Drugs, e.g. cocaine contaminated with levamisole

    Myths to address

    • "Debride it like any ulcer": surgery worsens PG through pathergy.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    PG is driven by overactive neutrophils in otherwise sterile tissue.

    1. 1

      Immune dysregulation

      Innate immune overactivity (IL-1, IL-8, IL-17, TNF-α) recruits neutrophils.

    2. 2

      Pathergy

      Minor trauma triggers an exaggerated neutrophilic response at the site.

    3. 3

      Destructive ulcer

      Neutrophils destroy dermis and fat, undermining the edge.

    4. 4

      Cribriform healing

      Ulcers heal with thin, sieve-like (cribriform) scars.

    Mechanism, pathergy and associations in more depth

    • Neutrophil dysregulation and autoinflammation: overexpression of IL-1β, IL-8, IL-17, IL-23 and TNF-α drives neutrophils into the dermis, which then destroy tissue. Innate-immune gene variants (e.g. PSTPIP1 in PAPA syndrome) show the mechanism.
    • Pathergy: minor trauma (a graze, a needle, surgery, a stoma, debridement) triggers or worsens lesions in ~25–30%. The case: a car-door graze → pustule within days → painful ulcer.
    • Associations to screen for:
    GroupExamples
    GutCrohn's disease, ulcerative colitis (PG activity often, but not always, parallels bowel activity); peristomal PG after IBD surgery
    JointsRheumatoid arthritis, seronegative spondyloarthritis, psoriatic arthritis
    BloodMyeloid dyscrasias: AML, myelodysplasia, myeloproliferative neoplasms; monoclonal gammopathy (especially IgA MGUS), myeloma
    OtherHidradenitis suppurativa (PASH/PAPASH syndromes), Behçet's, granulomatosis with polyangiitis, autoimmune hepatitis, solid tumours (rare)
    DrugsLevamisole-adulterated cocaine, isotretinoin, propylthiouracil, sunitinib and other kinase inhibitors, rituximab, G-CSF
    • Azathioprine (the patient's Crohn's treatment) does not protect against PG; immunosuppression also raises the chance that an ulcer is infected or atypical infection, which must be excluded.

    Histopathology

    • Dense neutrophilic infiltrate without vasculitis or organisms; findings are supportive, not specific (Delphi/PARACELSUS criteria).

    Clinical features

    • Starts as a pustule, papule or haemorrhagic blister, often after a minor injury, then becomes a deep ulcer within days.
    • Classic purple, overhanging (undermined) edge with a necrotic or purulent base.
    • Severe pain, out of proportion to the size.
    • Legs most often; also around stomas in IBD, and at surgical sites (pathergy).
    • Heals with cribriform (sieve-like) scars.

    Variants and clinical clues (Module 1 Week 5)

    • Classic ulcerative PG: starts as a tender papule, pustule or haemorrhagic bulla, often at a site of minor injury, which breaks down within days into an ulcer with: a violaceous (purple-blue), undermined, ragged edge and surrounding erythema; a necrotic, purulent or haemorrhagic base; pain out of proportion to the size (often ≥ 7/10); rapid centrifugal growth (centimetres in days to weeks)
    • Sites: pretibial skin (commonest); also trunk, head and neck, peristomal skin, post-surgical wounds (breast surgery, caesarean section), genitals; rarely mucosa and internal organs (sterile neutrophilic abscesses in lung, spleen).
    • Variants: Bullous — superficial haemorrhagic blisters on the arms or face; strong link with myeloid leukaemia/MDS (check the blood film); Pustular — sterile pustules in active IBD; Vegetative (superficial granulomatous) — slow, less painful, rarely associated with systemic disease; Peristomal — around an ileostomy/colostomy; often misread as an irritant or allergic stoma reaction; Postoperative — wound "dehiscence" or "infection" that fails to respond to antibiotics and worsens with debridement
    • Healing leaves atrophic cribriform (sieve-like) scars — a useful clue to past PG.
    • Systemic: low-grade fever, malaise, arthralgia and raised CRP/ESR are common and do not by themselves mean infection. The case: T 37.5 °C, HR 95, high CRP and ESR.

    Special groups

    • Peristomal PG
      • Around stomas in IBD; involve the stoma team.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    • Clinical, after excluding infection, vasculitis and malignancy. Biopsy of the edge supports the diagnosis (neutrophilic infiltrate; Delphi/Maverakis criteria).
    • Swabs only to look for secondary infection.
    • Seek dermatology urgently.

    Diagnostic criteria (Su, Delphi, PARACELSUS) and the work-up

    There is no single diagnostic test. Diagnosis combines the clinical picture, exclusion of mimics, histology and the treatment response.

    • Su criteria (2004) — the course answer: both major criteria (rapidly progressive painful necrolytic ulcer with an irregular violaceous undermined border; other causes excluded) plus ≥ 2 minor (pathergy or cribriform scarring; associated systemic disease; compatible histology; rapid response to systemic steroids).
    • Delphi consensus criteria (Maverakis et al., JAMA Dermatology 2018) — now widely used in UK specialist practice: 1 major criterion — biopsy of the ulcer edge showing a neutrophilic infiltrate — plus ≥ 4 of 8 minor criteria: exclusion of infection (including histology stains and tissue cultures); pathergy; personal history of IBD or inflammatory arthritis; papule, pustule or vesicle that ulcerates within 4 days; peripheral erythema, undermining border and tenderness at the ulcer site; multiple ulcers, at least one on the anterior lower leg; cribriform or "wrinkled paper" scars at healed sites; ulcer shrinking within 1 month of starting immunosuppression
    • PARACELSUS score (2019) — a weighted score from German wound clinics (rapid progression, assessment of differentials, violaceous border as the main items); a total of ≥ 10 points suggests PG. Useful as a teaching checklist.
    • Biopsy (specialist): a deep incisional or punch biopsy from the inflamed edge (including subcutis) for H&E plus tissue culture (bacteria, mycobacteria, fungi) and special stains. Histology shows a sterile dermal neutrophilic infiltrate — supportive but non-specific. Its main job is to exclude the mimics. Pathergy makes biopsy a small risk; dermatology usually does it with steroid cover in mind.
    • Primary-care work-up while referring: Swab the ulcer (bacterial culture) — a positive swab often reflects colonisation, not the cause; FBC and blood film (myeloid disorders; azathioprine-induced cytopenias), U&E, LFT, CRP/ESR; serum protein electrophoresis and serum free light chains (paraprotein); ANCA, ANA, rheumatoid factor/anti-CCP, antiphospholipid antibodies, cryoglobulins (vasculitis and vasculopathy mimics), hepatitis B/C and HIV serology as indicated; coagulation screen; HbA1c; ABPI (vascular contribution and before any compression); faecal calprotectin and bowel history if IBD is not yet known; photograph and measure the ulcer (length × width, edge marked) to document speed of spread

    Management

    Refer

    Recognise and refer: do not debride

    Do not debride or excise active PG, because pathergy makes it worse. Do not assume antibiotics will cure it. Refer urgently to dermatology.

    Specialist treatment (awareness, Module 5)

    • Oral corticosteroids first line for rapidly progressive disease. Ciclosporin is an alternative first-line option.
    • Later lines: dapsone, tetracyclines, and biologics (e.g. infliximab, particularly with IBD).
    • Potent topical or intralesional steroid, or topical tacrolimus, for small or early lesions.
    • Gentle, non-adherent wound care, good analgesia, and treatment of the associated disease (e.g. IBD).

    Steroid safety: the IP role

    • Monitor blood glucose and blood pressure.
    • Bone health: the Module 5 notes flag prednisolone above 7.5 mg daily for 6 months or more as an osteoporosis risk needing bone assessment (DEXA). UK guidance (NOGG) advises assessing bone health in anyone expected to take oral steroids for 3 months or more, so follow local policy and consider bone protection early.
    • Counsel on steroid cards, infection risk and not stopping abruptly.

    UK management in more depth: STOP GAP, IBD and medicines safety

    There is no NICE guideline or BAD guideline specific to PG; UK practice follows the UK STOP GAP trials, BAD and PCDS clinical guidance, the BSG IBD guideline (2019) and specialist expert consensus. PG is specialist-managed; the IP role is recognition, avoiding harm, symptom control and medicines safety.

    1. Recognise and refer.: Urgent dermatology referral (same week; same day if rapidly spreading, very painful, multiple ulcers, peristomal, or systemically unwell). Tell the IBD/rheumatology team already looking after the patient; Admit / same-day medical review if signs of sepsis (NEWS2), necrotising infection cannot be excluded, or there is severe pain or rapid spread
    2. Do no harm.: Do not debride, incise or excise active PG, and avoid sharp debridement by wound clinics — pathergy enlarges the ulcer; Avoid repeated courses of antibiotics for a "non-healing infection" without a diagnosis — escalate instead. Antibiotics are appropriate only for proven secondary infection (e.g. cellulitis spreading from the ulcer); Compression only after an ABPI and specialist advice (painful, and trauma can provoke pathergy)
    3. Wound care and pain.: Moist, non-adherent dressings (e.g. silicone or paraffin gauze, hydrogel or foam); antimicrobial dressings (silver, iodine) if heavily colonised; protect the surrounding skin. Involve the tissue viability / district nursing team; Analgesia is a priority: paracetamol ± weak or strong opioids (PG pain is often severe); avoid NSAIDs in IBD where possible (flare risk); review at dressing changes. Peristomal PG: stoma nurse for appliance fit
    4. Topical and local treatment (small or early lesions; specialist advice) — UK STOP GAP topical cohort (Thomas et al., J Am Acad Dermatol 2016; a prospective cohort, not an RCT): very potent topical corticosteroid (clobetasol propionate 0.05% ointment) to the ulcer edge/base, or; topical tacrolimus (0.03% or 0.1% ointment — off-label); overall about 44% healed on topical treatment alone by 6 months (median 145 days); smaller ulcers healed faster; intralesional triamcinolone into the active edge (specialist)
    5. Systemic treatment (specialist-initiated) — UK STOP GAP RCT (Ormerod et al., BMJ 2015): Prednisolone 0.75 mg/kg/day (maximum 75 mg) or ciclosporin 4 mg/kg/day (maximum 400 mg) gave similar healing (about half healed by 6 months); serious adverse effects (especially infections) were somewhat commoner with prednisolone. Choice depends on comorbidity (diabetes, osteoporosis, hypertension, renal function, infection risk); With IBD: anti-TNF therapy — infliximab (the only biologic with RCT evidence in PG; BSG 2019 and ECCO) or adalimumab — is often preferred, treating gut and skin together. Ustekinumab, JAK inhibitors and IL-1/IL-23 blockers are emerging options (off-label); Others/steroid-sparing: dapsone (G6PD first), doxycycline or minocycline (mild disease, anti-neutrophil effect), mycophenolate, methotrexate, IVIG, tacrolimus (oral); Treat the underlying disease (IBD, arthritis, haematological disorder) and stop a culprit drug (e.g. cocaine; review isotretinoin)
    6. Pharmacist medicines-safety checklist: Steroids: blood glucose and BP, mood/sleep; bone protection (FRAX; calcium/vitamin D ± bisphosphonate for those on ≥ 3 months of prednisolone at higher risk — NICE CKS Corticosteroids – oral; Royal Osteoporosis Society 2022); consider gastroprotection; Steroid Emergency Card and sick-day rules when on prednisolone ≥ 5 mg/day for ≥ 4 weeks (NatPSA 2020); never stop abruptly; Ciclosporin: baseline and regular BP, creatinine/eGFR (stop or reduce if creatinine rises > 25–30% above baseline), potassium, magnesium, LFT, lipids; major CYP3A4/P-gp interactions (clarithromycin, azole antifungals, diltiazem, grapefruit, St John's wort, rifampicin, carbamazepine); avoid NSAIDs/nephrotoxins; sun protection; Azathioprine (already prescribed): TPMT checked at baseline; shared-care FBC/LFT monitoring; allopurinol/febuxostat interaction (dangerous myelosuppression); any new infection or cytopenia changes the picture; Before biologics: TB screen (IGRA ± CXR), hepatitis B/C, HIV, VZV status; inactivated vaccines (influenza, pneumococcal, COVID-19, Shingrix where eligible); avoid live vaccines; Counsel about pathergy: protect the legs, avoid injections into affected areas, and plan elective surgery (including stoma surgery) with dermatology input and the disease quiet

    IP scope

    Recognition and urgent referral; ulcer measurement and photography; swabs and the baseline bloods above; wound-care and dressing advice with the nursing team; analgesia; stopping or questioning antibiotic cycling; medicines reconciliation, interaction checks and shared-care monitoring of steroids, ciclosporin, azathioprine and biologics; steroid card and bone protection; vaccination advice. Diagnosis (biopsy) and systemic or biologic treatment are specialist.

    Worked case: 38-year-old woman with Crohn's on azathioprine

    • QA = 4, pyoderma gangrenosum. A minor graze (pathergy) → pustule within days → rapidly enlarging, very painful ulcer in a woman with Crohn's disease, low-grade fever and raised CRP/ESR: 1. Atypical mycobacterial infection: evolves over weeks to months; it should still be excluded (tissue culture) because she is on azathioprine; 2. S. aureus infection: would not usually produce a violaceous undermined ulcer this painful and fast, and would respond to antibiotics; swab anyway; 3. Martorell ulcer: an ischaemic ulcer of long-standing hypertension ± diabetes, usually older patients, lateral/posterior leg; her BP is 125/80; 5. SCC: would not reach this size in 4 weeks, is rarely this painful and lacks an inflammatory violaceous border
    • QB = 2, diagnosis based on clinical and laboratory criteria. There is no confirmatory test; PG is a diagnosis of exclusion using the Su criteria (the course answer); current UK specialist practice uses the Delphi consensus criteria: 4/5. Skin biopsy for histology (and tissue culture): an essential part of the work-up — mainly to exclude infection, vasculitis and malignancy — but histology is non-specific, so biopsy alone does not confirm PG (and a biopsy of the edge is the major Delphi criterion; see the course-vs-UK table); 1. ABPI: assesses arterial disease and safety of compression; no diagnostic role in PG; 3. Nerve conduction studies/EMG: for neuropathic ulcers
    • QC = 1, myeloid blood dyscrasias (AML, myelodysplasia, myeloproliferative disorders; also IgA monoclonal gammopathy/myeloma). Other well-described associations are rheumatoid and other inflammatory arthritis and IBD. Eosinophilic asthma, penicillin antibiotics, pregnancy and ischaemic heart disease are not recognised associations.
    • UK additions for this patient: exclude infection first (swab, FBC — azathioprine can cause neutropenia — CRP, tissue culture via biopsy); urgent dermatology referral and tell her IBD team; no debridement; analgesia (avoid NSAIDs in Crohn's); non-adherent dressings; check her Crohn's activity. Expect prednisolone or ciclosporin (STOP GAP), given active IBD, an anti-TNF (infliximab/adalimumab); steroid safety, interaction and vaccination checks.

    Referral

    • Routine

      A painful ulcer with a purple undermined edge that expands over days.

    • Routine

      An ulcer getting worse after debridement or surgery (pathergy).

    • Routine

      Peristomal ulcer in IBD.

    • Routine

      Systemic upset, or rapid spread despite antibiotics.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • A painful ulcer with a purple undermined edge that expands over days.
    • An ulcer getting worse after debridement or surgery (pathergy).
    • Peristomal ulcer in IBD.
    • Systemic upset, or rapid spread despite antibiotics.

    Red flags / refer

    Rapidly enlarging, extremely painful ulcer with a violaceous undermined edge (urgent dermatology); fever with spreading cellulitis, crepitus, pain out of proportion or systemic toxicity (exclude necrotising fasciitis — same-day/999); an ulcer worsening after debridement or surgery (pathergy); bullous or haemorrhagic PG or abnormal blood film (urgent haematology — myeloid malignancy); peristomal ulcer in IBD; new PG with bowel symptoms (undiagnosed IBD — urgent GI referral); a patient on immunosuppressants with a new ulcer (infection first).

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Severe pain, infection, scarring; recurrence; complications of immunosuppression.

    Prognosis

    • Healing takes months; relapses common.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • Pathergy counselling: protect the skin, and plan procedures only when the disease is quiet, with specialist input.

    • In the community, escalate early. Do not trial antibiotic after antibiotic.

    • Ask about bowel symptoms and joint pain. PG can be the first sign of IBD.

    • "Graze → pustule → fast, agonising, purple-edged ulcer in someone with IBD = PG until proved otherwise — and never debride it."

    • PG is a diagnosis of exclusion: infection first (especially on azathioprine or a biologic), then vasculitis, vascular disease and cancer.

    • Pain out of proportion and an ulcer that gets bigger after debridement are the two most useful bedside clues.

    • Cribriform scars on the legs at a later consultation = past PG: ask about the bowel, joints and blood disorders.

    • Steroids and ciclosporin heal PG equally (STOP GAP): the choice is a medicines-safety decision — a natural place for pharmacist input.

    • Pyoderma gangrenosum is one of the skin signs of systemic disease (IBD, arthritis, blood disorders): see also skin signs of systemic disease.

    5 questions · optional
    1. Q1When should you think of Pyoderma gangrenosum?Reveal

      A pustule or small wound that rapidly becomes an exquisitely painful ulcer with a violaceous, overhanging edge, often in a patient with IBD or rheumatoid arthritis.

    2. Q2What drives it?Reveal

      Neutrophilic autoinflammation, not infection. Trauma makes it worse (pathergy).

    3. Q3What is the backbone of care?Reveal

      Recognise it and refer urgently. Specialist treatment with oral steroids or ciclosporin. No wide debridement.

    4. Q4What must you never miss?Reveal

      Misdiagnosis as an infected ulcer, with repeated antibiotics and surgical debridement that make it worse.

    5. Q5Name a red flag that needs escalation.Reveal

      A painful ulcer with a purple undermined edge that expands over days.
