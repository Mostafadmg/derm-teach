    Pyogenic granuloma

    A fast-growing, bright-red, friable lump that bleeds easily, often on a finger, lip or gum after minor injury or in pregnancy. Treat by curettage and cautery with histology, because amelanotic melanoma can look the same.

    Overview

    Module 2 · Week 1lobular capillary haemangiomableeding red lumpgranuloma gravidarum

    • Spot it

      A bright-red, shiny, easily bleeding lump that grew over a few weeks on a finger, lip, gum or face.

    • Why

      A reactive proliferation of small blood vessels, triggered by trauma, pregnancy or drugs (e.g. isotretinoin, some cancer drugs).

    • Treat

      Pressure for bleeding; refer for curettage and cautery and always send for histology; pregnancy lesions may resolve after birth.

    • Never miss

      Amelanotic nodular melanoma and SCC mimicking a pyogenic granuloma; recurrence if the base is not cauterised.

    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    What it is

    A common, benign, rapidly growing vascular tumour of skin and mucosa. The name is a misnomer: it is neither pyogenic nor a granuloma.

    Why it happens

    • systemic retinoids (isotretinoin, acitretin)
    • EGFR inhibitors (cetuximab, erlotinib, gefitinib; around the nail folds)
    • BRAF/MEK inhibitors
    • some antiretrovirals (indinavir)
    • ciclosporin
    • capecitabine and taxanes
    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    How it’s caused

    Reactive proliferation of lobules of capillaries, often after minor trauma. Hormonal (pregnancy, the "granuloma gravidarum" on the gums) and drug triggers, which a pharmacist should know:

    • systemic retinoids (isotretinoin, acitretin)
    • EGFR inhibitors (cetuximab, erlotinib, gefitinib; around the nail folds)
    • BRAF/MEK inhibitors
    • some antiretrovirals (indinavir)
    • ciclosporin
    • capecitabine and taxanes
    • the combined oral contraceptive

    What you’ll see

    • A bright red, glistening, friable papule or polyp, 5–10 mm, developing over weeks (the 4-week series is in ). It often has a collarette of scale.
    • Bleeds profusely with minor trauma. Fingers, lips, gums, face, eyelid, and nail folds.

    Differentials

    Amelanotic nodular melanoma (the critical one; consider in any adult with a "PG" on a non-traumatised site, recurrence, or atypical vessels), SCC/keratoacanthoma, BCC, Kaposi sarcoma, bacillary angiomatosis (HIV), cherry angioma (older, stable), infantile haemangioma (infants), metastasis (kidney, thyroid), and periungual granulation tissue from an ingrown nail.

    Treat

    Doses as in the source notes. Always confirm in the current BNF and local formulary.

    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    • Bleeding first aid: firm pressure for 10–15 minutes; a non-adherent dressing.
    • Refer for removal with histology. Curettage and cautery (shave/curette plus base cautery) is the usual UK primary-care or dermatology approach. It is discussed at knowledge level in the skin surgery notes.
    • Silver nitrate or cryotherapy alone gives no specimen and recurs more. Only use them where the diagnosis is not in doubt (e.g. a small pregnancy gum lesion, managed by a dentist).
    • Drug-induced PG: discuss with the prescribing specialist before stopping a cancer drug. Topical timolol or potent steroid are sometimes used by specialists for small periungual lesions.
    • Pregnancy PGs may regress after delivery; manage conservatively unless bleeding.

    Why curettage and cautery

    • A friable, bleeding vascular lesion that would bleed during shave alone. C&C removes it and seals the base, and the curettings go for histology (amelanotic melanoma, SCC and Kaposi are the "must-not-miss" mimics; the skin lumps notes see Procedures at knowledge level).
    • Alternatives: shave + cautery; excision for recurrent lesions (with closure, as course teaching described); silver nitrate or cryotherapy for very small lesions where the diagnosis is certain (no specimen).
    • Posterior neck: superficial procedures are safe. A deeper excision would need awareness of the spinal accessory nerve (see Consent, complications and anatomical danger zones).

    8

    Step 8 of 9 · 1 min

    Prescriber pearls

    • Recurrence (up to 15%) and satellite lesions happen. Return if it recurs, and do not assume it is benign.

    • On isotretinoin? PG-like granulation tissue at the nail folds is a known adverse effect. Report it (Yellow Card) and liaise with the prescriber.
