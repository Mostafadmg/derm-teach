    Ulcers & red leg

    Red leg: differentials & referral

    Module 5 · Week 2

    A structured approach to the red leg: is it really cellulitis? Bilateral, chronic, itchy redness is usually venous. Plus a one-table guide to when to refer urgently.

    Coversred legbilateral cellulitispseudocellulitiscellulitis mimicsstasis dermatitislipodermatosclerosisleg ulcer referralnecrotising fasciitis
    Think of it when

    Any patient presenting with ‘cellulitis’, especially if both legs are involved, it is chronic or itchy, or there is no fever.

    What drives it

    Most bilateral red legs are venous (stasis dermatitis, lipodermatosclerosis) or oedema, not infection.

    Backbone of care

    Ask: one leg or two, acute or chronic, fever or not, itch or pain? Treat the actual cause.

    Never miss

    Necrotising fasciitis, sepsis, DVT, critical ischaemia and diabetic foot infection.

    What it is

    ‘Red leg’ is a presentation, not a diagnosis. Up to a third of patients admitted with ‘cellulitis’ turn out to have a mimic (pseudocellulitis). This page brings together the Module 5 red-leg teaching and the referral triggers for leg ulcers.

    2

    How it’s caused

    CluePoints towards
    Unilateral, acute, fever or malaise, spreadingCellulitis or erysipelas
    Bilateral, chronic, itchy, scalyVenous (stasis) eczema
    Painful, woody, red gaiter skin, often bilateralAcute lipodermatosclerosis
    Calf swelling and tenderness, less skin rednessDVT
    Joint-centred, exquisitely tenderGout or septic arthritis
    Long history of swellingLymphoedema (with or without a flare)
    Pain out of proportion, rapid spread, toxicNecrotising fasciitis
    3

    Clinical features

    Compare the photos below. Venous eczema is scaly and itchy on both legs. Lipodermatosclerosis is indurated and tapering. True cellulitis is hot, tender, one-sided and expanding.

    4

    Diagnosis

    • Ask: one leg or two? Acute or chronic? Fever or rigors? Itch or pain? Is there a portal of entry?
    • Mark the border and record observations (NEWS2).
    • Wells score with D-dimer or Doppler if DVT is possible.
    • Check pulses or ABPI if there is an ulcer, or before compression.
    5

    Differentials

    MimicDistinguishing clues
    Venous eczema / stasis dermatitisBilateral or chronic, itchy, scale, haemosiderin, no fever
    Acute lipodermatosclerosisPainful, woody, red skin above the ankle, often misdiagnosed as cellulitis
    DVTCalf pain and swelling, less intense redness
    Lymphoedema flareChronic swelling history
    Gout / septic arthritisJoint-centred
    Contact dermatitis (e.g. to dressings)Itchy, vesicular, matches the area covered
    Necrotising fasciitisSevere pain out of proportion, rapid progression, systemic toxicity. ED
    Pyoderma gangrenosum / vasculitisUlcer edge features, purpura, systemic disease
    6

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    Referral summary (Module 5)

    SituationAction
    Suspected necrotising infection or sepsisImmediate ED (999)
    Facial or orbital cellulitis, or rapidly spreadingUrgent, same day
    Critical limb ischaemia, rest pain or gangreneUrgent vascular
    Diabetic foot ulcerUrgent diabetic foot pathway (NG19)
    Suspected PG (painful violaceous undermined ulcer)Urgent dermatology; avoid debridement
    Leg ulcer not improving, or atypical edgeTissue viability plus dermatology or vascular
    Red leg with no fever, bilateral, chronic and itchyRethink: venous eczema. Emollient, topical steroid and compression, no antibiotic
    Recurrent cellulitisTreat portals of entry; discuss prophylaxis with a clinician

    Cellulitis treatment itself: see Cellulitis & erysipelas.

    7

    Red flags & referral

    • Pain out of proportion, purple bullae, crepitus, or a rapidly spreading area: necrotising fasciitis.
    • Sepsis signs: low blood pressure, tachycardia, confusion, mottling.
    • A cold, pulseless or ischaemic limb.
    • A diabetic foot that is hot, red or ulcerated.
    8

    Prescriber pearls

    • Bilateral ‘cellulitis’ is rarely infection.
    • Venous eczema responds to emollient plus a topical steroid and compression (after ABPI). Antibiotics do nothing for it.
    • Recurrent ‘cellulitis’ in the same leg may be lipodermatosclerosis. Look at the whole leg history.
