    Retinoids (topical & oral)

    Topical retinoids for acne and photoageing, and the oral retinoids (isotretinoin, acitretin, alitretinoin): mechanism, uses, adverse effects, MHRA pregnancy-prevention rules and what the primary-care prescriber monitors.

    Module 1 · Week 2 · 5 images

    Also coversadapalenetretinoinisotretinoinacitretinalitretinoinpregnancy prevention programmeteratogenic
    Think of it when

    Acne needing a topical retinoid, or a patient on (or asking about) isotretinoin, acitretin or alitretinoin.

    What drives it

    Retinoic-acid receptor binding normalises follicular keratinisation, reduces sebum (isotretinoin) and slows keratinocyte turnover.

    Backbone of care

    Topicals: start alternate nights, small amount, sunscreen. Oral retinoids are specialist-initiated with the MHRA Pregnancy Prevention Programme and mood monitoring.

    Never miss

    Pregnancy exposure (acitretin: avoid pregnancy for 3 years after stopping); mood change and suicidal thoughts; raised lipids and LFTs; tetracycline interaction (raised intracranial pressure).

    What it is

    Natural and synthetic compounds related to vitamin A (retinol) that act on retinoid receptors.

    03

    Clinical features

    How it works

    • Retinoids bind the nuclear retinoic acid receptors (RAR-α, -β, -γ) and retinoid X receptors (RXR). Receptor dimers bind retinoic-acid response elements and regulate genes for keratinocyte proliferation and differentiation. RAR-γ predominates in the epidermis, which is why adapalene and trifarotene are skin-selective.
    • Topical retinoids in acne: They normalise follicular keratinisation: comedolytic, and anti-comedogenic because they prevent the microcomedone; They are anti-inflammatory (down-regulate TLR-2 and AP-1) and help other agents penetrate; In photoageing they stimulate collagen and even out pigment
    • Oral isotretinoin is the only drug that acts on all four acne mechanisms: profound sebaceous-gland shrinkage and sebum suppression (largely through intracellular isomerisation and sebocyte apoptosis, not simply direct receptor binding); normalised keratinisation; fewer C. acnes (a knock-on effect of sebum loss); anti-inflammatory effects
    • Acitretin is anti-proliferative and normalises differentiation in psoriasis. It is not immunosuppressive, so it is useful when infection or malignancy risk rules out immunosuppressants.

    Clinical uses & where it fits

    • Topical: acne (first-line, NICE NG198); photoageing (tretinoin — cosmetic, not NHS); some ichthyoses; off-label melasma combinations.
    • Isotretinoin: severe acne (nodular/conglobate), acne at risk of permanent scarring, or moderate acne that has failed adequate standard therapy including an oral antibiotic. Specialist-initiated only (consultant-led team).
    • Acitretin, alitretinoin, bexarotene: specialist-initiated (hospital-only prescribing).

    How to choose / assess

    • Topical retinoids suit comedonal and mixed acne. Choose adapalene for tolerability, or adapalene/BPO for inflammatory lesions. Avoid in pregnancy or when planning pregnancy.
    • Before an oral retinoid, assess whether the patient could become pregnant, mental health, lipids/liver, and whether a tetracycline is being taken.

    06

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    Pharmacist / IP

    Pregnancy and topical retinoids

    • Systemic absorption from topical tretinoin/adapalene is very low. Observational data have not shown a clear rise in malformations, though isolated case reports exist.
    • Since the EMA 2018 retinoid review (reflected in MHRA materials and SmPCs), the EU/UK position is that topical retinoids are contraindicated in pregnancy and in women planning pregnancy. No formal PPP is needed for topicals, but ask about pregnancy intention and contraception, and record the advice.
    • Inadvertent exposure to a topical: stop the product, reassure that the risk appears very low, and seek advice from UKTIS (UK Teratology Information Service, 0344 892 0909).
    • Exposure to an oral retinoid: urgent specialist/obstetric referral and a Yellow Card report.

    Oral retinoid Pregnancy Prevention Programme (PPP) — MHRA (2019 materials, still current)

    • Applies to isotretinoin, acitretin and alitretinoin in anyone who could become pregnant.
    • Contraception: at least one highly effective, user-independent method (LARC — implant, IUD/IUS — or sterilisation) or two complementary user-dependent methods (e.g. combined pill + condom), started at least 1 month before treatment.
    • Pregnancy testing: a medically supervised test before starting, ideally monthly during treatment, and 1 month after stopping (acitretin: periodically for 3 years).
    • Supply: prescriptions within the PPP are limited to 30 days' supply and must be dispensed within 7 days of the prescription date.
    • Paperwork: an annual acknowledgement of risk form, a patient reminder card and a pharmacist checklist at dispensing.

    Isotretinoin — MHRA rules (Oct 2023, updated Jan 2026)

    • 31 Oct 2023 (CHM Isotretinoin Expert Working Group): Counselling on possible psychiatric and sexual side effects, including reports of sexual dysfunction persisting after stopping; Assessment of mental health and sexual function before starting and at every follow-up (patient-reported measures, e.g. PHQ-9/GAD-7); An in-person review about 1 month after starting; A new Acknowledgement of Risk Form, Patient Reminder Card and Pharmacist Checklist; The Lead Prescriber must have expertise in systemic retinoids (consultant-led); For under-18s, agreement of two independent prescribers
    • October 2025 clarifications: follow-ups after the first may be remote; supervised pregnancy testing may be done remotely with oversight; by the third appointment the sexual-function questions can be brief.
    • Update, 22 Jan 2026: The two-prescriber requirement for under-18s was removed; It was replaced by an updated two-page Acknowledgement of Risk Form: the prescriber confirms there is no other appropriate effective treatment, and the patient confirms they know they can ask for a second opinion; A BAD-run national clinical audit starts in 2026, and patients should watch a BAD patient information video before starting; All other measures remain
    • What this means for a primary-care IP: You do not initiate isotretinoin; you refer (NICE NG198 criteria); Pre-counsel: MHRA asks the referrer to give initial information on the risks, including mental health and sexual function; Check and record contraception and screen mood; Avoid interacting prescriptions: no tetracyclines (IIH) and no high-dose vitamin A supplements; Report suspected ADRs via Yellow Card; If a patient on isotretinoin presents with low mood or suicidal thoughts → stop the drug, arrange urgent mental-health review, and inform the dermatologist
    • Monitoring (BNF/BAD): LFTs and fasting lipids before starting, after ~1 month, then 3-monthly (or per the specialist protocol); pregnancy tests as above. Typical dose 0.5–1 mg/kg/day to a cumulative 120–150 mg/kg; lower-dose regimens are common.
    • Counselling list: Dry lips, skin and eyes (lip balm, emollient, lubricant eye drops; contact lenses may become uncomfortable), nosebleeds; Photosensitivity and sunburn; Skin fragility: no waxing, laser or dermabrasion during treatment and for 6 months after; An early acne flare (photo m6-w2 I15); Myalgia (cut back strenuous exercise); Headache or visual disturbance (IIH — urgent); reduced night vision (driving); Hair thinning: telogen effluvium that usually recovers after treatment, though persistent cases are reported; Impetigo/S. aureus colonisation; Mood and sexual changes; No blood donation during treatment or for 1 month after

    Acitretin and alitretinoin (hospital-only)

    • Acitretin: PPP with contraception continuing for 3 years after stopping; No alcohol during treatment and for 2 months after, because alcohol converts acitretin to etretinate, which has a very long half-life; Avoid tetracyclines and methotrexate (hepatotoxicity); monitor LFTs and lipids; Nail fragility and hair loss
    • Alitretinoin (NICE TA177): severe chronic hand eczema unresponsive to potent TCS; PPP; check lipids and TFTs; headache.

    Red flags / refer

    • Pregnancy on an oral retinoid → urgent: stop the drug, obstetric/fetal medicine referral, UKTIS, Yellow Card.
    • Mood change, suicidal ideation or psychosis on isotretinoin → stop, urgent mental-health assessment.
    • Severe headache with visual symptoms (IIH), severe abdominal pain (pancreatitis with very high triglycerides), jaundice.
    • Acne fulminans (see Acne therapeutics case and the combined oral contraceptive) → dermatology the same day or within 24 h.

    IP scope

    Topical retinoids: initiate within competence (NICE NG198). Oral retinoids: specialist-only initiation and supply (hospital/homecare). Your role is referral, counselling, interaction and contraception checks, and safety-netting.

    07

    Red flags & referral

    Escalate
    • Any possibility of pregnancy on an oral retinoid: stop and seek urgent specialist advice.
    • New low mood, anxiety or suicidal thoughts on isotretinoin: stop and refer the same day.
    • Headache, visual disturbance or vomiting on a retinoid plus a tetracycline: possible raised intracranial pressure.
    08

    Prescriber pearls

    • Topical: a pea-sized amount for the whole face, at night, on dry skin. Start on alternate nights, use a moisturiser and daily SPF. Expect "purging" for 2–4 weeks and benefit by 8–12 weeks. Avoid if pregnant or trying to conceive.
    • Ask every woman on a topical retinoid "Could you be pregnant, or are you planning to be?" — and document the answer.
    • Isotretinoin memory aid: "contraception, mood, sex, sun, skin procedures, no tetracyclines".
