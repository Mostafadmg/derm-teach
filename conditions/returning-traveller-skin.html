    Tropical & travel

    The returning traveller with a skin problem

    Module 5 · Week 4

    A structured approach to skin problems after travel or migration: where, when, what exposure, and is there fever? Fever after the tropics means same-day malaria exclusion.

    Coverstravel rashtropical dermatologymigrant healthfever and rashNaTHNaCtravel history
    Think of it when

    Any rash, ulcer, lump or creeping track in someone who has travelled or migrated in the past year.

    What drives it

    Exposure-specific infections (sandflies, mosquitoes, soil, water, animals) plus common UK conditions acquired abroad.

    Backbone of care

    Take a full travel and exposure history, examine the whole skin, exclude malaria the same day if febrile, and use the UK tropical-medicine advice routes.

    Never miss

    Fever (malaria, dengue, rickettsia, typhoid), mucosal leishmaniasis, and numb patches (leprosy).

    What it is

    Core approach

    • Most skin problems after travel are common things (insect-bite reactions, impetigo/cellulitis from scratched bites, sunburn, tinea, pityriasis versicolor, contact/phyto-dermatitis). Tropical-specific diagnoses are less common but matter because they are missed (PCDS).
    • Travel history checklist: countries and regions (urban vs rural/forest/beach), dates (incubation windows: dengue ~4–10 days; CLM days–weeks; leishmaniasis 2 weeks–6+ months; leprosy years; mycetoma months–years), activities (barefoot on beaches/soil, jungle trekking, farming, military service), insect bites, freshwater/animal contact, sexual contact, prior residence (migrants, VFR travellers — "visiting friends and relatives"), pre-travel vaccines/prophylaxis, and illness or treatment while abroad.
    • Fever + rash in a returning traveller = same-day medical assessment. Malaria must be excluded urgently in anyone with fever after travel to a malarious area (usually within the past year, especially within 3 months), even if they took prophylaxis. Consider dengue, chikungunya, rickettsial infection (look for an eschar), meningococcal disease, measles, acute HIV, typhoid.
    • Where to refer in the UK: local Infectious Diseases (ID) or tropical medicine service; national centres include the Hospital for Tropical Diseases (HTD), UCLH, London (GP e-Referral; 24-hour on-call tropical medicine registrar via UCLH switchboard; emergency walk-in for unwell returnees within 6 months, weekday clinic hours) and the Liverpool School of Tropical Medicine/Royal Liverpool tropical service. Travel-health resources: NaTHNaC / TravelHealthPro country pages; UKHSA Rare and Imported Pathogens Laboratory (RIPL) for arbovirus testing.
    • Notification (England): leprosy is a statutorily notifiable disease for clinicians; dengue virus is a notifiable organism reported by the laboratory (clinicians do not need to notify suspected dengue).
    2

    How it’s caused

    • Common UK conditions acquired abroad: insect-bite reactions, impetigo and cellulitis from scratched bites, sunburn and phototoxic reactions, tinea, pityriasis versicolor, contact and phytophotodermatitis.
    • Exposure-specific infections: sandflies (leishmaniasis), mosquitoes (dengue, chikungunya, Zika), beaches and soil (larva migrans, tungiasis, mycetoma), ticks (rickettsial eschar, Lyme disease), fresh water (schistosome cercarial dermatitis), sexual contact (syphilis, HIV).

    Diagnosis

    • Ask where (including rural, beach or forest), when (incubation windows), what they did, bites, water and animal contact, sexual contact, and treatment taken abroad.
    • Examine the whole skin, lymph nodes and mucosa.
    • Fever = same-day malaria film or rapid test, plus FBC and specialist advice.
    • Use the national tropical-medicine services for advice on biopsy, culture or PCR; take photographs to share.
    5

    Differentials

    Most rashes after travel are the same conditions seen at home. Keep scabies, impetigo, tinea, drug eruptions, urticaria and contact dermatitis on the list before deciding it is a tropical disease.

    6

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    • Treat common conditions as usual, with review dates.
    • Refer exotic or uncertain cases early to infectious diseases or tropical medicine.
    • Do not give NSAIDs or aspirin to a febrile traveller until dengue has been considered.
    • Remember notifiable diseases and give prevention advice for the next trip (insect repellent with DEET 50%, covering up, footwear on beaches).
    7

    Red flags & referral

    • Fever after travel to a malaria area: same-day malaria testing, whatever the rash.
    • Dengue warning signs (abdominal pain, vomiting, bleeding, lethargy): emergency.
    • Nasal or oral ulceration after leishmaniasis exposure: specialist referral.
    8

    Prescriber pearls

    Cross-cutting prescriber pearls

    1. "Where have you been, and when?" Take the travel and migration history for every unexplained or non-healing skin lesion. Incubation runs from days (dengue, CLM) to months (leishmaniasis) to years (leprosy, mycetoma).
    2. Fever after travel = same-day assessment. Exclude malaria first. In suspected dengue, give paracetamol and no NSAIDs or aspirin.
    3. Painless is a warning sign, not a comfort. Painless ulcers suggest leishmaniasis, leprosy (neuropathic) or Buruli ulcer. Painless swelling with sinuses suggests mycetoma.
    4. Test sensation in any persistent hypopigmented or red patch in someone from a leprosy-endemic country. Palpate the nerves.
    5. Do not put steroid creams on undiagnosed tropical ulcers, and do not give systemic steroids to someone who may have Strongyloides (larva currens) or TB without specialist input.
    6. Know your UK specialist routes: HTD (UCLH) e-Referral, the 24-hour on-call tropical registrar via UCLH switchboard, the HTD Leishmaniasis MDT, the national leprosy Consultant Advisors (UKHSA memorandum), and local ID. Leprosy is clinician-notifiable in England.
    7. Teratogens in tropical dermatology: miltefosine, thalidomide (ENL), albendazole/ivermectin (avoid in pregnancy), and live Qdenga (contraindicated in pregnancy). Always check pregnancy and contraception status. Rifampicin defeats hormonal contraception.
    8. Pre-travel prevention is the pharmacist's strongest lever: footwear (CLM, mycetoma); day-time Aedes bite avoidance and DEET 50% (dengue); dusk/dawn sandfly avoidance and fine-mesh nets (leishmaniasis); Qdenga only for previously infected travellers
