    Rosacea (incl. Demodex & steroid rosacea)

    Chronic central facial redness, flushing and papulopustules without comedones. Treat by phenotype, control triggers, look for eye involvement, and never put a potent steroid on it.

    Also calledrosaceaflushingerythematotelangiectaticpapulopustular rosacearhinophymaocular rosacea+5
    Related pagesAcne mimics: rosacea, steroid eruptions and folliculitisAcne vulgarisTopical corticosteroids: potency, quantities & safetyDiscoid lupus erythematosus

    Key facts

    • Chronic inflammatory facial disease with flushing, persistent central redness, papules and pustules and telangiectasia; no comedones.
    • Affects about 5% of adults, usually after 30; men and women about equally (phymas mainly men).
    • Driven by neurovascular hyper-reactivity, innate immune activation (cathelicidin/LL-37) and Demodex.
    • UK: trigger avoidance and sun protection; ivermectin 1%, azelaic acid or metronidazole for papules; brimonidine for redness; oral doxycycline for moderate–severe.
    • Ocular rosacea with pain or reduced vision needs urgent eye review.

    Overview

    Note

    Core rosacea is in the acne notes.

    This section goes further.

    ROSCO 2017/2019 and the NRS 2017 criteria replaced the four rigid "subtypes" with phenotypes, because patients often overlap (e.g. ETR + PPR + ocular).

    • Diagnostic (one is enough): persistent centrofacial erythema that can flare periodically, or phymatous change.
    • Major (two are needed if no diagnostic feature): flushing/transient erythema, inflammatory papules/pustules, telangiectasia, ocular manifestations.
    • Minor/secondary: burning, stinging, oedema, dryness.

    The older “four subtypes” teaching maps onto these phenotypes. Teach phenotypes, and treat each feature separately.

    Demodex and demodicosis

    Demodex folliculorum (in the follicle infundibulum) and D. brevis (in sebaceous glands) are normal commensal mites. Demodicosis is a skin disease caused by too many of them. It can be primary (rosacea-like, with fine scale) or secondary (to immunosuppression, TCS/TCI use, HIV or chemotherapy).

    Steroid-induced rosacea

    Note

    Core is in the acne notes (steroid and drug-induced acneiform eruptions; photo K08). Withdrawal practicalities are added here.

    A rosacea-like eruption (erythema, telangiectasia, papulopustules, burning) caused by prolonged facial topical (or inhaled/nasal) corticosteroid use. It overlaps with periorificial dermatitis and with "topical steroid withdrawal" (red skin syndrome; MHRA Drug Safety Update, Sept 2021).

    Epidemiology

    • Prevalence
      • About 5% of adults worldwide; under-recognised in skin of colour.
    • Age and sex
      • Usually presents after 30 and becomes commoner with age; roughly equal sex distribution; rhinophyma is mainly in men.
    • Skin type
      • Most common in fair skin (Celtic and Northern European descent).
    • Associations
      • Depression, anxiety, migraine, cardiovascular disease, inflammatory bowel disease and H. pylori have been linked.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    • Heat, hot drinks and spicy food (capsaicin): activate TRPV1 on sensory nerves and vessels, so neuropeptides are released (substance P, CGRP) and vessels dilate. Pepper and chilli are classic triggers.
    • Alcohol, especially red wine: acetaldehyde, histamine and polyphenols cause vasodilation. Red wine is a classic trigger.
    • UV: induces MMPs, ROS and cathelicidin activation through kallikrein-5, and damages dermal matrix so telangiectasia shows. This is the case's "worse with sun".
    • Cold, wind, exercise, stress, menopause: vascular reactivity through neurogenic routes.
    • Topical steroids and calcineurin inhibitors: local immunosuppression lets Demodex overgrow, steroids cause vasodilation, and there is rebound on withdrawal.
    • Drugs that cause flushing: nicotinic acid, CCBs (nifedipine, amlodipine), nitrates, sildenafil, opioids, vancomycin, tamoxifen and aromatase inhibitors.
    • Systemic associations: H. pylori (conflicting evidence; eradication is not routine), IBD/SIBO, migraine, depression and anxiety, and possibly cardiovascular risk. These are associations, not reasons for routine tests.

    Demodex and demodicosis

    • Rosacea skin carries roughly 6–15× more mites (the density is higher).
    • Mites and their bacterium (Bacillus oleronius) activate TLR2, which drives kallikrein-5 → cathelicidin → inflammation.
    • When dying mites burst, they release antigens.
    • The response to ivermectin and permethrin is indirect evidence that the mites play a part.
    • Whether they cause rosacea or simply thrive in it is unresolved. Teach "contributor and amplifier".

    Steroid-induced rosacea

    Steroids cause vasoconstriction and then rebound vasodilation. They also cause skin atrophy, which makes telangiectasia visible. Local immunosuppression lets Demodex and bacteria overgrow, and the patient gets steroid addiction (stopping causes a flare, so they restart). Typical sources: OTC hydrocortisone, potent TCS borrowed or prescribed for "eczema", and imported skin-lightening creams (clobetasol, illegal in the UK).

    Risk factors and associations

    • Genetic susceptibility (HLA class II polymorphisms; family history).
    • Triggers: UV light, heat and cold, exercise, hot drinks, spicy food, alcohol, stress, cosmetics, topical steroids.
    • Microbes: increased Demodex folliculorum density; Bacillus oleronius; S. epidermidis.

    Myths to address

    • "Rosacea is adult acne": no comedones; acne treatments such as benzoyl peroxide often irritate.
    • "Red nose means alcoholism": rhinophyma occurs in non-drinkers; alcohol is just one trigger.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    Rosacea combines an over-reactive innate immune system with unstable facial blood vessels and nerves.

    1. 1

      Triggers activate the skin

      UV, heat, spicy food, alcohol and Demodex activate TLR2 receptors on keratinocytes.

    2. 2

      Cathelicidin overdrive

      Raised kallikrein-5 cleaves cathelicidin into LL-37, which drives inflammation, vasodilatation and new vessel growth.

    3. 3

      Neurovascular reactivity

      Sensory nerves (TRPV1 channels) release neuropeptides such as CGRP, causing flushing, burning and stinging; repeated flushing leaves persistent erythema and telangiectasia.

    4. 4

      Papules and pustules

      Inflammatory cells gather around follicles, producing papules and sterile pustules (no comedones).

    5. 5

      Phyma and fibrosis

      Chronic inflammation and lymphatic leakage cause sebaceous hyperplasia and fibrosis, thickening the nose (rhinophyma).

    Histopathology

    • Dilated dermal vessels, solar elastosis and a perivascular and perifollicular lymphohistiocytic infiltrate.
    • Papulopustular: perifollicular inflammation, sometimes granulomas; increased Demodex.
    • Phymatous: sebaceous hyperplasia, fibrosis and dilated follicles. Biopsy is rarely needed.

    Grading tools (for documentation and audit): ROSCO global severity, the Investigator's Global Assessment (IGA 0–4, used in trials and for ivermectin/brimonidine licensing), and RosaQoL for quality of life. Record phenotype + severity + QoL impact at every review.

    Skin of colour: erythema is often subtle or brownish. Look for papules, telangiectasia under dermoscopy, warmth, and a history of stinging and flushing.

    Demodex and demodicosis

    Pointers to demodicosis rather than plain rosacea:

    • fine whitish follicular scale ("frosted", "nutmeg-grater" or "sandpaper" texture)
    • itch or burning rather than flushing
    • asymmetric, sometimes unilateral
    • small superficial papulopustules
    • a history of facial TCS/TCI or immunosuppression
    • Demodex blepharitis: cylindrical "collarettes" at the lash bases

    Steroid-induced rosacea

    • erythema (often a "fire-red" colour), telangiectasia, papulopustules, burning or stinging, skin atrophy and shine
    • a flare within days of stopping the steroid
    • periocular or perioral involvement is often present

    Phenotypes to recognise

    • Erythema & telangiectasia

      Persistent central redness, visible vessels. Site: Cheeks, nose.

    • Papulopustular

      Dome-shaped papules and pustules, no comedones. Site: Cheeks, chin.

    • Phymatous

      Thickened skin, contour change; mostly men. Site: Nose.

    • Ocular

      Gritty eyes, lid-margin vessels, blepharitis. Site: Eyelids.

    • Demodicosis

      Fine ‘frosted’ follicular scale, itch, asymmetric. Site: Cheeks.

    • Steroid rosacea

      Fiery red, burning; flares within days of stopping. Site: Face.

    Special groups

    • Skin of colour
      • Redness may be hard to see; warmth, papules, burning and dryness are clues; granulomatous forms may be more common.
    • Children
      • Rare; ocular symptoms may come first; consider perioral dermatitis.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    Diagnosis is clinical. Investigate flushing only if there are atypical features:

    • diarrhoea, wheeze or palpitations: carcinoid (24 h urine 5-HIAA), mastocytosis (tryptase)
    • paroxysmal headaches, sweating, hypertension: phaeochromocytoma (plasma metanephrines)
    • photosensitive, scaly, systemic symptoms: SLE or dermatomyositis (ANA, CK)
    • sweaty, perimenopausal: menopause
    • medication-related

    Demodex and demodicosis

    • Dermoscopy: "Demodex tails" (creamy gelatinous threads poking out of follicles) and follicular plugs.
    • Standardised skin-surface biopsy (SSSB) with cyanoacrylate glue: >5 mites/cm² is abnormal. This is a specialist test.
    • Microscopy of scrapings or plucked eyelashes (optometry or ophthalmology).

    Steroid-induced rosacea

    Clinical, from the history. Ask specifically about every cream, its strength and how long it has been used, including over-the-counter and online products. Scrape for tinea if the eruption is scaly or annular (tinea incognito).

    Rosacea (often coexists), perioral or periorificial dermatitis, seborrhoeic dermatitis, folliculitis (bacterial, Malassezia, gram-negative), contact dermatitis, steroid rosacea, tinea faciei (do a scraping if scaly and asymmetric).

    Rosacea, demodicosis, periorificial dermatitis, ACD (to the steroid molecule or vehicle), tinea incognito, lupus.

    Demodex and demodicosis

    • Ivermectin 1% cream once daily, which treats both rosacea and Demodex. This is the practical first choice.
    • Permethrin 5% cream (off-label for this use; specialist-advised regimens exist), or metronidazole topical (weak anti-mite activity).
    • Stop facial TCS/TCI.
    • Refractory or immunosuppressed patients: oral ivermectin (specialist, single doses repeated).
    • Demodex blepharitis: lid hygiene and tea tree oil lid products (OTC); refer to optometry. Lotilaner eye drops are US-approved but not UK-licensed as of 2026 (check).

    Steroid-induced rosacea

    1. Stop the facial steroid. For potent steroids used for months, specialists may taper (e.g. step down to 1% hydrocortisone, then stop over 1–2 weeks) to soften the rebound. Do not swap to another steroid.
    2. Warn about the rebound flare. Expect it over 1–3 weeks and for symptoms to persist for weeks to months. This is the main reason patients restart.
    3. Cover the withdrawal with an oral tetracycline (lymecycline 408 mg daily or doxycycline 40 mg MR/100 mg for 6–12 weeks) ± topical metronidazole, azelaic acid or ivermectin. Topical tacrolimus or pimecrolimus is an off-label specialist option. It reduces rebound but can itself cause rosacea-like eruptions.
    4. Cool compresses, a bland emollient and "zero therapy" for cosmetics. Use mineral sunscreen.
    5. MHRA Yellow Card and documentation if TSW is suspected. Refer if severe or there is diagnostic doubt.

    Caution

    Prescribing limits and pitfalls

    • Potent facial steroid: Never without a specialist plan. OTC hydrocortisone licence excludes the face.
    • Clonidine for flushing: 50 micrograms twice daily (off-label), not 50 mg.
    • Relapsing: Maintenance topical therapy is often needed.

    Referral

    • Same day

      Eye pain, photophobia or reduced vision: possible keratitis → ophthalmology.

    • Urgent

      Flushing with diarrhoea, wheeze, palpitations or hypotension: carcinoid or mastocytosis.

    • Urgent

      Photosensitive malar rash with systemic symptoms: exclude lupus.

    • Routine

      Rash after potent topical steroids: stop or taper with support; refer if severe.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Eye pain, photophobia or reduced vision: possible keratitis, so same-day ophthalmology.
    • Flushing with diarrhoea, wheeze, palpitations or hypotension: investigate for carcinoid syndrome or mastocytosis.
    • A photosensitive malar rash with systemic symptoms: exclude lupus.
    • Facial rash after potent topical steroids: steroid rosacea, so stop or taper the steroid with support.

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Ocular rosacea: blepharitis, conjunctivitis, keratitis, rarely corneal ulceration.
    • Rhinophyma and other phymas.
    • Psychological distress, low self-esteem and social avoidance.

    Prognosis

    • Chronic and relapsing; treatment controls rather than cures.
    • Persistent erythema and telangiectasia respond best to vascular lasers or IPL (not on the NHS routinely).

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • Trigger diary for 2–4 weeks. Avoid only your triggers, not every listed one.

    • Daily SPF 30–50 mineral or tinted sunscreen. Use green-tinted camouflage for redness. Avoid alcohol-based toners, menthol, witch hazel, exfoliants and fragrance.

    • No topical steroids on the face. Check OTC hydrocortisone and imported "lightening" creams.

    • Set expectations: papules respond, erythema and flushing respond poorly to the antimicrobial-type drugs. That is why brimonidine and laser exist.

    • Before brimonidine, check for MAOIs, TCAs and antihypertensives, and avoid it in Raynaud's or orthostatic hypotension.

    • "Rosacea that fails metronidazole or azelaic acid, especially if it's scaly or itchy" → think Demodex, switch to ivermectin, and ask about steroid creams.

    • Mites are on everyone. Reassure the patient that this is not a hygiene problem and not contagious in any practical sense.

    • Never prescribe a potent or very potent TCS for the face without a specialist plan. When counselling on OTC hydrocortisone, the licence excludes the face (with limited exceptions).

    • Ask about skin-lightening products in a non-judgemental way. Mercury and clobetasol products are illegal for sale in the UK and are a trading-standards issue.

    Self-check quiz

    5 questions · optional
    1. Q1Which lesion is absent in rosacea but the hallmark of acne?Reveal

      Comedones.

    2. Q2First-line topical for papulopustular rosacea (CKS)?Reveal

      Ivermectin 1% cream once daily, up to 4 months.

    3. Q3What is the key warning with brimonidine gel?Reveal

      Rebound or paradoxical erythema in about 10–20%: test a small area first. It is for persistent redness, not flushing.

    4. Q4Licensed oral option for moderate–severe rosacea?Reveal

      Doxycycline 40 mg MR once daily for 16 weeks, then review.

    5. Q5Rosacea with eye pain and photophobia?Reveal

      Possible keratitis: same-day ophthalmology.
