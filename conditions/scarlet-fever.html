    Infections — bacterial

    Scarlet fever & strawberry tongue

    Module 5 · Week 5

    A group A streptococcal infection, mostly in children: sore throat, fever, then a fine ‘sandpaper’ rash, flushed cheeks with a pale ring round the mouth and a strawberry tongue. Treat with 10 days of penicillin V; it is notifiable.

    Coversscarlatinagroup A streptococcusGASstrawberry tonguesandpaper rashPastia linesphenoxymethylpenicilliniGASnotifiable
    Think of it when

    A child with fever, sore throat and a fine rough red rash starting on the neck and chest, accentuated in skin folds, with a strawberry tongue.

    What drives it

    Erythrogenic (pyrogenic) toxins from Streptococcus pyogenes.

    Backbone of care

    Phenoxymethylpenicillin for 10 days (clarithromycin if allergic), exclusion from school until 24 h of antibiotics, notify UKHSA, safety-net for invasive GAS.

    Never miss

    Invasive group A streptococcal disease, and Kawasaki disease or toxic shock as differentials.

    What it is

    A toxin-mediated illness caused by group A β-haemolytic Streptococcus (Streptococcus pyogenes, GAS), usually after streptococcal pharyngitis/tonsillitis (occasionally a skin/wound source). Mainly children 2–10 years; peaks in winter/spring. Notable UK surges (e.g. 2014–2018, winter 2022–23 with rise in invasive GAS). Strawberry tongue = inflamed, enlarged fungiform papillae on the dorsum of the tongue.

    2

    How it’s caused

    Spread by respiratory droplets and direct contact (incubation 2–5 days). GAS strains producing streptococcal pyrogenic exotoxins (SpeA/B/C — superantigens) cause the diffuse rash and enanthem; immunity is toxin-specific, so second episodes are possible. Complications arise by local spread (suppurative), immune mechanisms (non-suppurative), or invasion (iGAS).

    3

    Clinical features

    • Abrupt fever, sore throat (exudative tonsillitis, tender anterior cervical nodes), headache, nausea/vomiting, abdominal pain.
    • Rash after 12–48 h: fine, pinhead, "sandpaper" erythema starting on neck/chest, spreading to trunk/limbs; blanches; Pastia lines (linear petechial accentuation in skin folds — antecubital, axillae); flushed cheeks with circumoral pallor. On darker skin the rash may be hard to see but feels rough.
    • Tongue: day 1–2 "white strawberry tongue" (white coat with red papillae poking through) → day 3–5 "red strawberry/raspberry tongue" (coat sheds). Palatal petechiae (Forchheimer spots).
    • Rash fades in ~1 week, followed by desquamation of fingertips, toes, groin.
    • Causes of strawberry tongue (tutor point): scarlet fever; Kawasaki disease; toxic shock syndrome (staph or strep); also reported with vitamin B12/folate deficiency (glossitis), yellow fever, allergy/drug reactions and some other infections.
    4

    Diagnosis

    Clinical in primary care (UKHSA). Throat swab for culture (or rapid antigen test where available) if uncertain or in outbreaks/closed settings. Not routinely needed before treatment. If unwell: FBC, CRP, blood cultures, ASO titre (retrospective).

    5

    Differentials

    Look-alikeDistinguishing clues
    Kawasaki diseaseFever ≥5 days, bilateral non-purulent conjunctivitis, red cracked lips, strawberry tongue, swollen red hands/feet then peeling, polymorphous rash, cervical node; <5 years — urgent paediatric referral (coronary aneurysm risk)
    Toxic shock syndromeHypotension, multi-organ involvement, diffuse "sunburn" erythroderma, tampon/wound source — 999/ED
    Staphylococcal scalded skin syndromeTender erythema, flaccid blisters, Nikolsky positive, perioral crusting, no tonsillitis
    Viral exanthems (measles, rubella, enterovirus, parvovirus "slapped cheek")Measles: coryza, cough, conjunctivitis, Koplik spots; slapped cheek: lacy limb rash; no sandpaper texture
    Drug eruption / allergic rashRecent drug; itchy morbilliform/urticarial
    Infectious mononucleosis (± amoxicillin rash)Teenager, marked lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, rash after amoxicillin
    Miliaria / heat rashTiny papules in covered areas, afebrile

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    Based on UKHSA Guidelines for the public health management of scarlet fever and NICE CKS Scarlet fever:

    First-line antibiotics (to speed recovery, reduce transmission and complications)

    • Phenoxymethylpenicillin for 10 days (BNF age-banded doses; e.g. adults/children ≥12 years 500 mg four times daily, or 1 g twice daily per local guidance).
    • Penicillin allergy: clarithromycin (5 days) or azithromycin (per BNF/local guidance); amoxicillin (10 days) is an alternative where liquid palatability/supply is an issue (follow UKHSA/local advice during shortages).
    • Complete the full course.

    Public health

    • Notifiable disease in England & Wales — the diagnosing registered medical practitioner notifies UKHSA (pharmacist prescribers should ensure this happens via the GP/local process).
    • Exclusion: children and adults stay off school/nursery/work until 24 hours after starting antibiotics; untreated cases are infectious for ~2–3 weeks.
    • Outbreaks (≥2 cases in a setting within 10 days) → inform local Health Protection Team; extra caution if chickenpox co-circulating (iGAS risk).
    • Hygiene: hand washing, cover coughs, don't share cutlery/towels.

    Supportive / self-care

    • Paracetamol/ibuprofen for fever and throat pain (avoid ibuprofen if dehydrated or chickenpox co-infection per local advice), fluids, soft foods, emollient for itch/peeling.
    • NHS Pharmacy First (England) acute sore throat pathway uses FeverPAIN for pharyngitis; check the current pathway on how suspected scarlet fever is handled (typically outside PGD scope → prescriber/GP).

    Red flags / refer — safety-net for invasive GAS

    • Same-day / 999: persistent high fever despite antipyretics, drowsiness, confusion, severe muscle pain, rapidly spreading painful red skin (necrotising fasciitis), difficulty breathing, dehydration, signs of sepsis/shock (NICE NG51).
    • Quinsy (trismus, uvula deviation), otitis media, sinusitis, cervical abscess, pneumonia.
    • Late: acute rheumatic fever (2–4 weeks; arthritis, carditis), post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis (1–3 weeks; dark urine, oedema, hypertension), PANDAS (rare), and the link to guttate psoriasis.
    • Features of Kawasaki (fever ≥5 days, conjunctivitis) or TSS (hypotension) → urgent.
    7

    Red flags & referral

    • Rapid deterioration, severe pain, a spreading skin infection, breathing difficulty or signs of sepsis: invasive GAS, emergency.
    • Fever for 5 days or more with red eyes, red lips, swollen hands and feet: consider Kawasaki disease.
    • Chickenpox plus scarlet fever in the same household: higher invasive GAS risk, so lower threshold for review.
    8

    Prescriber pearls

    • "Sandpaper rash + sore throat + strawberry tongue + circumoral pallor" = scarlet fever until proven otherwise; on darker skin rely on texture and the tongue.
    • Strawberry tongue is not specific — a child with ≥5 days of fever and red eyes needs Kawasaki excluded.
    • Phenoxymethylpenicillin is best taken on an empty stomach; four-times-daily dosing needs adherence support (use bd regimen where appropriate).
    • Tell parents peeling of fingers/toes 1–2 weeks later is expected.
    • Safety-net clearly and in writing for iGAS symptoms; advise urine check/return if dark urine or swelling after 1–3 weeks.
