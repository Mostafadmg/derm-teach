    Seborrhoeic keratosis

    The commonest benign ‘stuck-on’ warty lesion of later life. Learn the dermoscopy clues (milia-like cysts, comedo openings, fissures), when an SK needs a second look, and the sign of eruptive SKs.

    Also calledseborrhoeic wartbasal cell papillomastuck-on lesionLeser–Trélat signSK dermoscopymilia-like cysts
    Related pagesActinic keratoses (solar keratoses)Melanoma: recognition & referralSolar lentiginesMelanocytic naevi (moles) & the changing mole

    Key facts

    • Very common benign epidermal tumour: waxy, warty, 'stuck-on' papules and plaques, tan to black.
    • Over 90% of adults over 60 have at least one; usually start in the 30s–40s.
    • Not premalignant; caused by clonal mutations (e.g. FGFR3, PIK3CA) in keratinocytes, not sebum or viruses.
    • Dermoscopy: milia-like cysts, comedo-like openings, 'brain-like' ridges and sharp edges.
    • Reassure if typical; refer anything atypical (melanoma mimic). Sudden eruption of many SKs (Leser–Trélat) may signal internal malignancy.

    Overview

    Core SK content (definition, cause, sites, basic management) is in the older skin pages notes and is not repeated here. This section adds dermoscopy, "the SK that changes", irritated SK and Leser–Trélat.

    A benign epidermal tumour that is almost universal with age, often multiple, and the commonest "changing lesion" seen in primary care.

    Hutchinson and Albright signs

    Eponymous signs often mixed up in exams.

    Epidemiology

    • Who
      • Extremely common in older adults of all ethnic groups; uncommon under 20.
      • Numbers increase with age; a strong family tendency in some people.
    • Variant in skin of colour
      • Dermatosis papulosa nigra: many small dark papules on the face and neck, especially in people of African or Asian descent.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    Activating FGFR3/PIK3CA mutations in keratinocytes; it is a clonal benign neoplasm, not a pre-cancer. Lesions thicken, darken, catch on clothing, become irritated/inflamed and itch. That is exactly the "changing, itchy" story in Case 3, and the same story fits melanoma.

    Risk factors and associations

    • Clonal somatic mutations in FGFR3, PIK3CA, RAS, AKT1, EGFR.
    • Sun exposure may contribute (more on exposed sites); friction in body folds.
    • Eruptive SKs may follow inflammatory dermatoses or sunburn; Leser–Trélat sign with internal cancer is rare.

    Myths to address

    • "It's from greasy skin": the name is misleading; SKs are not related to sebum or sebaceous glands.
    • "They are warts and spread": they are not viral or contagious.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    An SK is a benign clonal overgrowth of epidermal keratinocytes that sits on top of the skin.

    1. 1

      Clonal mutation

      Keratinocytes acquire activating mutations (often FGFR3 or PIK3CA) that drive orderly but excessive growth.

    2. 2

      Upward growth

      The thickened epidermis grows outwards above the skin surface, so the lesion looks stuck on and can almost be lifted off.

    3. 3

      Horn cysts and pigment

      Keratin collects in small cysts (milia-like cysts on dermoscopy); melanin transferred from melanocytes darkens many lesions.

    4. 4

      Irritation

      Friction or trauma can inflame an SK, making it red, crusted or bleeding, which can mimic cancer.

    Histopathology

    • Acanthosis of basaloid keratinocytes with horn pseudocysts, hyperkeratosis and papillomatosis; the base is flat (the 'string sign').
    • Variants: acanthotic, hyperkeratotic, reticulated, clonal, irritated. Biopsy if any doubt.

    Clinical features

    • "Stuck-on", waxy, verrucous, fissured papules and plaques, tan to black, on the trunk, face and scalp.
    • Irritated SK: red rim, crust and itch. This mimics SCC or melanoma.
    • Sign of Leser–Trélat: sudden eruption of many SKs (often with acanthosis nigricans or itch). It has been linked to internal malignancy (gastric/colorectal adenocarcinoma, lymphoma), but the evidence is weak and contested, because SKs are so common. Look for systemic symptoms rather than labelling everyone. Eruptive SKs also occur with drugs (e.g. cytarabine, docetaxel, BRAF inhibitors) and after inflammatory dermatoses (erythroderma).

    Hutchinson and Albright signs

    How they arise / the signs

    SignMeaning
    Hutchinson's sign (nail)Periungual pigment spreading onto the proximal or lateral nail fold from a longitudinal melanonychia. This suggests subungual (acral) melanoma. Cross-reference the nail disorders notes (melanonychia) and the melanoma notes
    Pseudo-Hutchinson's signPigment visible through a transparent cuticle (benign naevus, ethnic pigmentation, Laugier–Hunziker, drugs such as zidovudine, minocycline, hydroxyurea, chemotherapy)
    Hutchinson's sign (herpes zoster)Vesicles on the tip/side of the nose (nasociliary branch of V1) predict ophthalmic involvement, so same-day ophthalmology
    Hutchinson's triadCongenital syphilis: notched incisors, interstitial keratitis, deafness
    Albright's sign (pseudohypoparathyroidism / Albright hereditary osteodystrophy)Short 4th/5th metacarpals ("knuckle-knuckle-dimple-dimple" when making a fist); osteoma cutis
    McCune–Albright syndromeLarge unilateral "coast of Maine" café-au-lait macules (jagged), polyostotic fibrous dysplasia, precocious puberty (GNAS mosaicism). NF1 CALMs are smooth, like the "coast of California"

    Types to recognise

    • Classic stuck-on

      Waxy warty papule. Site: Trunk.

    • Many SKs

      Older back. Site: Back.

    • Irritated SK

      Red, crusted. Site: Trunk.

    • Flat tan SK

      Like a solar lentigo. Site: Face.

    • Milia-like cysts

      Dermoscopy. Site: Dermoscopy.

    • Brain-like ridges

      Dermoscopy. Site: Dermoscopy.

    Special groups

    • Skin of colour
      • Dermatosis papulosa nigra is common and benign; treat only for cosmetic reasons, with care to avoid pigment change.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    Key features:

    • Milia-like cysts (white/yellow "stars" or round bodies)
    • Comedo-like openings (keratin-filled crypts)
    • Fissures and ridges ("brain-like"/cerebriform, "fat fingers")
    • Sharp, "moth-eaten" border
    • Hairpin vessels with a white halo, in irritated or thick lesions
    • Caveat: some SKs show multiple colours or blue-black areas, and "collision" lesions (SK beside a melanoma or BCC) exist. Melanoma can mimic SK, especially in men's backs, like Case 3 sailor.

    Differential diagnoses

    PhotoConditionDistinguishing features
    		MelanomaEvolving asymmetry, atypical network, blue-white veil; no cysts or openings.
    		Pigmented BCCPearly, leaf-like areas, arborising vessels.

    Clinical relevance, diagnosis and differentials

    A single nail band that is new, widening (>3 mm), dark, irregular, or in an adult aged over 50, especially of the thumb or great toe, or with Hutchinson's sign needs urgent dermatology (suspected cancer pathway). Skin of colour often has multiple benign bands. A single changing band is the concern.

    Management

    • Apply the NICE NG12 weighted 7-point checklist to any changing lesion: Major features (2 points each): change in size, irregular shape, irregular colour; Minor features (1 point each): largest diameter ≥7 mm, inflammation, oozing, change in sensation (e.g. itch); Case 3: "changing" (2) + itchy (1) = 3 → suspected cancer pathway. This is unless the lesion is confidently a classic SK on dermoscopy by a trained clinician (see Where course teaching differs from UK guidance)
    • Even when the score is below 3, refer if there is any doubt. NG12 1.7.2 says dermoscopy suggesting melanoma means a suspected cancer referral.
    • Confident SK: reassure; leaflet. Itch: emollient ± a short course of a mild/moderate topical steroid for irritation.
    • Removal for cosmetic reasons is usually not NHS-funded. Cryotherapy, curettage or shave are used where symptomatic, with histology for curettage/shave if any doubt. Never destroy (cryo) a lesion that might be melanoma.
    • Topical hydrogen peroxide 40% (licensed in the US, not marketed in the UK) and other cosmetic options are not IP options.

    Hutchinson and Albright signs

    Do not label nail pigmentation as fungal or traumatic without evidence. Haematomas grow out distally (mark the nail and re-check in 4–6 weeks), whereas melanoma bands remain at the matrix. Refer on the suspected cancer pathway when Hutchinson's sign is present.

    Worked cases

    Case 1: 50-year-old Asian woman, 5 mm pigmented arm papule, slow growth over 5 years

    • Diagnosis: (pigmented) dermatofibroma.
    • Bedside sign: dimple / Fitzpatrick (pinch) sign. Dermoscopy: peripheral network with a central white patch.
    • Immunostain: Factor XIIIa positive (CD34 negative). DFSP is the reverse.
    • IP plan: reassure if classic. Refer if dermoscopy is atypical or the 7-point score is ≥3 (growth = 2 points, so one more feature tips it).

    Case 2: 68-year-old woman, tender scaly 7×8 mm pinna nodule, post-stroke, losartan/aspirin/simvastatin

    • Bedside test: gentle squeeze, which causes disproportionate tenderness. Also ask about the sleeping side.
    • Diagnosis: chondrodermatitis nodularis helicis.
    • First-line treatment: pressure relief (CNH foam protector/pillow with a hole; avoid sleeping on that side).
    • Second-line: intralesional or potent topical steroid, topical GTN (off-label), cryotherapy, cartilage excision.
    • Biopsy if: not resolving after 6–8 weeks of offloading, atypical features, or SCC/BCC suspected (NG12 1.7.4/1.7.5).
    • Do not stop aspirin for a biopsy.

    Case 3: 55-year-old sailor, itchy back lesion changing over 6 months

    • Bedside test: dermoscopy. Milia-like cysts, comedo-like openings, fissures/ridges, sharp border, and hairpin vessels mean SK.
    • Diagnosis: seborrhoeic keratosis. Management: reassure and discharge.
    • UK IP version: NG12 7-point score = change (2) + itch/sensation change (1) = 3, which means the suspected cancer pathway unless a trained clinician has confidently made the dermoscopic SK diagnosis. An IP without dermoscopy competence should refer, not discharge. Also give sun-safety advice and a full skin check.

    Practice case

    • Clear cell acanthoma: red, moist, collarette-rimmed leg papule; string-of-pearls vessels on dermoscopy.
    • Reassure when typical. Cryotherapy or curettage only if symptomatic or diagnostic doubt; usually private/cosmetic on the NHS unless catching or bleeding.
    • Ugly-duckling or atypical → refer, do not freeze.

    Treatment ladder

    1. 1

      Confident SK

      Reassure; leaflet; itch: emollient ± short mild steroid.

    2. 2

      Doubt

      Score ≥ 3, dermoscopy suggesting melanoma (NG12 1.7.2), or any doubt: refer.

    3. 3

      Removal

      Cryo / curettage only if symptomatic (catching, bleeding, inflamed); cosmetic removal usually not NHS-funded.

    Referral

    • Urgent

      An ‘SK’ that is changing, has atypical dermoscopy, or stands out from the patient’s other SKs: possible melanoma.

    • Routine

      Sudden eruption of many SKs with itch: consider internal malignancy (Leser–Trélat).

    Red flags and when to refer

    • An ‘SK’ that is changing, has atypical dermoscopy or stands out from the patient’s other SKs: refer as a possible melanoma.
    • Sudden eruption of many SKs with itch: consider internal malignancy (Leser–Trélat).

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Irritation, itch, catching on clothing and bleeding.
    • Misdiagnosis: melanoma, pigmented BCC or SCC can look like an SK.
    • Collision tumours (a cancer arising next to an SK) are rare.

    Prognosis

    • Benign and permanent; new lesions keep appearing with age.
    • Removal (cryotherapy, curettage, shave) is usually cosmetic and not funded on the NHS unless symptomatic.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • Case 3 sailor has lifelong UV exposure, so he is at higher risk for melanoma, BCC and SCC. Give sun-protection advice and do a full skin check (other lesions, scalp, ears).

    • Teach the "ugly duckling" concept and ask about change using photos (patients can photograph lesions monthly).

    • Ask about drugs that cause melanonychia (hydroxycarbamide, zidovudine, minocycline, antimalarials, cytotoxics). Multiple nails and a matching start date make it drug-related.
