    Epidermoid & pilar cysts, milia and Gardner syndrome

    Module 2 · Week 1

    Smooth, mobile lumps under the skin. Epidermoid cysts have a central punctum; pilar cysts sit on the scalp. Recognise the inflamed cyst, when to excise, and the rare syndromic clue (multiple facial cysts in Gardner syndrome).

    Coverssebaceous cystepidermal cystpilar cysttrichilemmal cystdermoid cystmiliapunctumGardner syndromeFAP
    Think of it when

    A smooth, round, mobile lump with a central pore (epidermoid), or several firm scalp lumps (pilar).

    What drives it

    Keratin-filled sacs lined by epidermis or hair-root sheath; dermoid cysts are embryonic, often at the outer eyebrow.

    Backbone of care

    Reassure; leave alone or excise the whole wall when not inflamed; inflamed cysts usually are not infected (antibiotics only for cellulitis).

    Never miss

    Recurrence after incomplete removal; midline nasal or scalp lumps in children (intracranial connection); multiple cysts with osteomas in Gardner syndrome (FAP).

    What it is

    • Epidermoid (epidermal inclusion) cyst: the commonest cutaneous cyst. Its wall resembles epidermis/infundibulum and it is filled with keratin (the "cheesy", foul-smelling contents in Case 3). "Sebaceous cyst" is a misnomer.
    • Pilar (trichilemmal) cyst: the scalp in ~90%. Firm, smooth, no punctum, often multiple and familial (autosomal dominant). It shells out easily.
    • Dermoid cyst: congenital, along embryonic fusion lines. The classic site is the lateral eyebrow (external angular dermoid); midline nasal or scalp dermoids may connect intracranially.
    • Milia: 1–2 mm superficial keratin cysts (primary in neonates/face; secondary after blistering, trauma, steroids).
    • Vellus hair cysts / steatocystoma: small multiple cysts on the chest; steatocystoma multiplex.
    2

    How it’s caused

    Most epidermoid cysts arise from occluded hair follicle infundibula. Others come from implantation of epidermis after trauma (palms/soles), acne, or HPV (plantar). Rupture releases keratin into the dermis and causes a sterile foreign-body inflammatory reaction. Most "infected cysts" are actually inflamed, not infected.

    3

    Clinical features

    • Mobile, dome-shaped dermal nodule, 0.5–5 cm, on the face, neck, trunk or scrotum, with a central punctum in many.
    • Inflamed or ruptured cysts are red, tender and fluctuant. Recurrence follows incomplete wall removal.
    • Gardner syndrome (a familial adenomatous polyposis variant, APC gene): multiple epidermoid cysts, often atypical sites (face, scalp, limbs) and appearing before puberty; osteomas of the skull or jaw; dental anomalies (supernumerary or unerupted teeth); desmoid tumours; CHRPE (retinal pigment lesions); and colorectal polyposis with near-100% colorectal cancer risk if untreated.
    4

    Diagnosis

    • Clinical. Ultrasound if unsure (helps distinguish lipoma, lymph node or sarcoma).
    • Dermoid cysts in the midline (nasal bridge, scalp), or any lesion with a sinus or hair, need imaging (MRI) before surgery because of possible intracranial extension. This was the course point.
    • Gardner: refer to genetics or colorectal services. Triggers are multiple cysts in a child or young adult, especially with osteomas, dental anomalies, or a family history of polyposis or young colorectal cancer.
    5

    Differentials

    Lipoma (soft, no punctum, lobulated); pilomatricoma (rock-hard, child, see Other lumps and syndromes); dermoid; abscess / hidradenitis (flexural, recurrent, sinus tracts); lymph node; BCC with a cystic component; metastasis; on the scalp, pilar cyst vs proliferating trichilemmal tumour (rapidly growing, ulcerating, elderly woman → biopsy).

    6

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    • Asymptomatic cysts: reassure. Cosmetic removal is usually not NHS-funded (EBI/local policy). Squeezing worsens inflammation and scarring.
    • Inflamed (not infected) cyst: warm compresses; analgesia. A fluctuant cyst may need incision and drainage, which can be done in some GP settings. Antibiotics only if there is spreading cellulitis or systemic features (choose per NICE NG141 cellulitis / local formulary). Avoid routine antibiotics for a "red cyst" (antimicrobial stewardship).
    • Elective excision once the inflammation settles (6–8 weeks) removes the entire wall to prevent recurrence. options include standard elliptical excision, or minimal-incision / punch techniques with lower scar burden but slightly higher recurrence. These are clinician-surgeon decisions (knowledge level only; see the skin surgery notes).
    • Refer: suspected dermoid near the midline or orbit (plastics/paediatric surgery, imaging first); suspected Gardner (genetics/colorectal); rapidly enlarging scalp "cyst" in an older person (biopsy); any lump with sarcoma features (see Lipoma, angiolipoma and Dercum's disease; the sarcoma lump pathway).
    7

    Red flags & referral

    • Midline lump on the nose, scalp or spine in a child: imaging before any surgery.
    • Multiple cysts in childhood with osteomas or a family history of bowel cancer: consider Gardner syndrome (FAP) and refer.
    8

    Prescriber pearls

    • Explain that the smell comes from keratin breakdown, not poor hygiene.
    • Recurrence after "popping" or drainage is expected because the wall remains.
    • Isotretinoin, ciclosporin and BRAF inhibitors can provoke milia or epidermoid cysts. Look at the drug history if cysts erupt.
