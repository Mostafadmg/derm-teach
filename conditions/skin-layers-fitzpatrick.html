    Foundations: describing skin

    Skin layers, Fitzpatrick skin types & dermoscopy

    Module 1 · Week 1

    How the layers of the skin explain each lesion type, why Fitzpatrick skin type matters for sunburn risk, pigment change and treatment, and what dermoscopy adds to naked-eye examination.

    Coversskin structureepidermisdermishistologyFitzpatrickphototypeskin of colourdermoscopydermatoscope
    Think of it when

    Linking a lesion to its level in the skin; judging sun risk, PIH risk or erythema in skin of colour; deciding whether dermoscopy helps.

    What drives it

    Epidermal processes give scale and colour change; dermal processes give induration, nodules and scarring; melanin biology sets the phototype.

    Backbone of care

    Record the Fitzpatrick type, assess severity by more than redness, and counsel photoprotection to match.

    Never miss

    Under-calling inflammation in darker skin; melanoma in any skin type (acral and nail sites in darker skin).

    What it is

    Skin layers linked to lesion type

    Skin = epidermis (stratified squamous keratinising epithelium) → basement membrane zone (dermo-epidermal junction) → dermis (papillary and reticular; collagen, elastin, vessels, nerves, appendages) → subcutis (fat). DermNet: The structure of normal skin.

    LayerKey cells/structuresFunction
    Stratum corneumFlattened anucleate corneocytes in a lipid "bricks and mortar" matrixBarrier to water loss, irritants, microbes
    Stratum granulosumKeratohyalin granules (incl. filaggrin precursor), lamellar bodies secreting lipidsBuilds barrier lipids; filaggrin → natural moisturising factor
    Stratum spinosumKeratinocytes joined by desmosomes ("prickles"); Langerhans cellsMechanical strength; immune surveillance
    Stratum basaleDividing basal keratinocytes, melanocytes (~1 per 10 basal cells), Merkel cells; anchored by hemidesmosomesRenewal (~4 weeks transit to surface); pigment
    Basement membrane zoneLamina lucida/densa, anchoring fibrils (type VII collagen), BP180/BP230 antigensGlues epidermis to dermis
    DermisFibroblasts, collagen/elastin, blood vessels, lymphatics, nerves, mast cells, hair follicles, sebaceous/eccrine/apocrine glandsStrength, elasticity, thermoregulation, sensation, immune response
    SubcutisAdipocytes, septa, larger vesselsInsulation, energy store, cushioning

    (Palms/soles have an extra stratum lucidum.)

    Fitzpatrick skin types I–VI

    The Fitzpatrick skin phototype classifies skin by its response to sun (burning vs tanning) after an initial exposure, originally (1975) to dose PUVA therapy. It is self-reported/clinically assessed and is the classification most widely used in practice and publications (tutor, Day 2).

    TypeTypical descriptionBurns / tans
    IVery fair/pale, often red hair, freckles, blue eyesAlways burns, never tans
    IIFairUsually burns, tans minimally/with difficulty
    IIIMedium/light-oliveSometimes mild burn, tans gradually/uniformly
    IVOlive/moderate brownRarely burns, tans easily
    VBrown/dark brownVery rarely burns, tans very easily
    VIDeeply pigmented dark brown–blackNever burns

    Dermoscopy: why it adds value

    Dermoscopy (dermatoscopy, epiluminescence microscopy) uses a hand-held ×10 magnifier with a light source, either polarised (no contact; cross-polarised filters cancel surface glare) or non-polarised (contact with a liquid/gel interface). It reveals pigment and vascular structures in the epidermis, dermo-epidermal junction and upper dermis that are invisible to the naked eye.

    How it’s caused

    Skin layers linked to lesion type

    LesionLevel / mechanism
    Macule/patchChange in melanin (basal melanocytes, melanosome transfer) or dermal blood flow — no thickening
    ScaleDisordered stratum corneum: hyper-/parakeratosis (fast turnover in psoriasis)
    Papule/plaqueEpidermal acanthosis ± dermal infiltrate (psoriasis: epidermal hyperproliferation + neutrophils; LP: band-like lymphocytes at the dermo-epidermal junction)
    NoduleDeep dermal/subcutaneous infiltrate, tumour or granuloma
    Vesicle (spongiotic)Eczema: inflammatory fluid separates keratinocytes → intercellular oedema = spongiosis within the epidermis (quiz Q2) → microvesicles (most visible on palms/soles as pompholyx)
    Vesicle (viral)HSV/VZV infect keratinocytes → ballooning degeneration, multinucleate giant cells → intraepidermal vesicles
    Flaccid bulla/erosions — intraepidermal splitPemphigus: autoantibodies to desmogleins (desmosomes) → acantholysis → thin roof that breaks easily. Bullous impetigo/staph scalded skin: exfoliative toxin cleaves desmoglein-1 high in the epidermis (subcorneal)
    Tense bulla — subepidermal splitBullous pemphigoid: autoantibodies to BP180/BP230 (hemidesmosomes) → whole epidermis lifts off → thick roof, blisters stay intact. Friction/burns: mechanical/thermal separation
    PustuleNeutrophils migrate into epidermis (spongiform pustule of Kogoj in psoriasis) or follicle (folliculitis/acne)
    WhealMast-cell histamine → upper-dermal oedema, epidermis normal
    LichenificationChronic rubbing → acanthosis, hyperkeratosis, papillary dermal fibrosis
    Erosion vs ulcerErosion = epidermis lost above basement membrane (heals by re-epithelialisation, no scar); ulcer = through basement membrane into dermis (heals by scar)
    AtrophyThin epidermis ± loss of dermal collagen (steroids inhibit fibroblasts/keratinocytes)
    CystEpithelial-lined cavity in dermis

    Why does a blister develop? (Day 3 model answer, paraphrased): a blister forms when the "glue" between cells fails or cells are damaged, and tissue fluid fills the gap. The level of failure predicts the clinical picture: high (intraepidermal) splits give fragile flaccid blisters and erosions; low (subepidermal) splits give tense, often haemorrhagic, durable bullae.

    Functions to link (Days 3–4): barrier (physical, chemical — acid mantle, antimicrobial peptides, lipids; microbiological — commensal flora competes with pathogens; immunological — Langerhans cells), thermoregulation (eccrine sweat, dermal blood flow; hair traps heat), sensation, vitamin D synthesis, UV protection (melanin caps over nuclei), social/communication. Commensals rarely cause bacteraemia because the intact stratum corneum, acid pH, sebum fatty acids and innate immunity prevent invasion — breaches (cannulas, ulcers, eczema) raise risk.

    Ageing (Day 5): intrinsic (thinner dermis, fewer fibroblasts, slower turnover, flattened dermo-epidermal junction → fragility) vs extrinsic/photo-ageing (UV + smoking → solar elastosis, wrinkles, lentigines). Twin studies show smoking and sun exposure produce visibly older skin in the exposed twin.

    Fitzpatrick skin types I–VI

    • Melanocyte number is broadly similar across all phototypes (answer to Day 3 Q2c: type IV does not have more melanocytes than type I). Differences arise from melanosome size, number, packaging and distribution, the eumelanin (brown-black) : pheomelanin (red-yellow) ratio, and how efficiently melanin is transferred to keratinocytes.
    • Larger, singly dispersed eumelanin-rich melanosomes in darker skin absorb/scatter more UV → greater natural photoprotection (approx. SPF-equivalent differences), but also a more reactive melanocyte response to inflammation → post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH).
    • UVB converts 7-dehydrocholesterol in the epidermis to pre-vitamin D3 → liver (25-OH D) → kidney (1,25-(OH)2 D). Melanin competes for UVB, so darker skin needs longer exposure to make the same amount of vitamin D.

    Dermoscopy: why it adds value

    Surface reflection from the stratum corneum normally hides deeper structure. Immersion fluid or cross-polarisation removes that glare, so light penetrates and returns from deeper layers — each colour/pattern corresponds to pigment depth (black/brown = epidermis/junction; grey/blue = dermis) and vessel morphology.

    Clinical features

    Skin layers linked to lesion type

    • Thick-roofed tense bullae on flexural limbs/trunk in older adults → BP.
    • Mucosal erosions + flaccid blisters → pemphigus (refer urgently).
    • Itchy palmar/digital vesicles → pompholyx (spongiotic).

    Fitzpatrick skin types I–VI

    DomainLower phototypes (I–II)Higher phototypes (IV–VI)
    Skin cancer (melanoma, BCC, SCC)Highest risk — strict sun protection, skin checksLower UV risk but cancers often diagnosed later; acral lentiginous melanoma (palms/soles/nails) not UV-related
    Photo-ageingEarlier wrinkles, lentiginesRelative protection from photo-ageing; ageing may present as dyschromia, DPN, melasma
    Erythema recognitionRedness obviousErythema may look violaceous/grey/brown — underestimates eczema severity (EASI/SCORAD) and cellulitis
    PIH / hypopigmentationLess common, shorterCommon, long-lasting (months–years); major patient concern after acne, eczema, LP, procedures
    KeloidLess commonMore common (V–VI)
    Phototherapy / lasers / peelsLower starting UV doses (burn easily)Higher risk of dyspigmentation with lasers/peels — specialist parameters
    Vitamin DSynthesise more efficientlyHigher risk of deficiency at UK latitude

    Is phototype protective against ageing at higher latitude? (Day 5) — Partly: higher phototypes have more photoprotection and typically show fewer wrinkles and lentigines, but intrinsic ageing still occurs, and lifestyle (smoking, sunbeds) matters more than latitude alone. At UK latitude, darker phototypes gain photo-ageing protection but pay a vitamin D cost.

    Vitamin D at UK latitude (Day 4 table, paraphrased):

    • UK lies ~50–60°N: from roughly October to March UVB is too weak for meaningful cutaneous vitamin D synthesis in anyone.
    • People with darker skin (African, African-Caribbean, South Asian heritage), those who cover skin for cultural/religious reasons, the housebound/care-home residents, and those who use high-factor sunscreen constantly are at highest risk; UK South Asian populations consistently show high prevalence of deficiency (forum-cited studies, e.g. Darling; Correia et al. 2014).
    • UK advice (SACN 2016 / UK government / NICE PH56 & CKS vitamin D deficiency): everyone aged ≥1 year should consider 10 micrograms (400 units) vitamin D daily in autumn and winter; at-risk groups including people with dark skin should consider it all year round. Babies/young children have specific advice (Healthy Start). Check current guidance for treatment doses in confirmed deficiency (CKS/local formulary).

    Dermoscopy: why it adds value

    No — evidence supports real diagnostic value in trained hands:

    • Meta-analyses and a Cochrane review (Dinnes et al. 2018) show dermoscopy improves melanoma detection accuracy versus naked-eye examination alone, with better sensitivity without loss of specificity when used by trained clinicians.
    • Reduces unnecessary excisions of benign lesions (better benign:malignant excision ratio) and helps triage referrals.
    • Helps distinguish seborrhoeic keratosis (milia-like cysts, comedo-like openings, "brain-like" fissures), solar lentigo (homogeneous light-brown, moth-eaten border, fingerprint pattern) from lentigo maligna (asymmetric follicular pigmentation, rhomboidal structures, grey dots — forum Day 5 discussion), BCC (arborising vessels, blue-grey ovoid nests), dermatofibroma (central white patch, peripheral network), molluscum (central white polylobular structures with crown vessels), scabies ("delta-wing jet" mite), and nail/scalp conditions (trichoscopy).
    • Allows digital monitoring of atypical naevi and teledermatology image quality (UK referral pathways increasingly request dermoscopic images).
    4

    Diagnosis

    Skin layers linked to lesion type

    Histology (punch biopsy) and direct immunofluorescence (perilesional skin) are specialist tests for blistering disorders; Nikolsky sign (lateral pressure shears off epidermis) suggests intraepidermal split (pemphigus, SSSS, TEN). Pharmacists/IPs recognise and refer; they do not need to perform these.

    Fitzpatrick skin types I–VI

    Ask: "If you went out in strong sun for 30–45 minutes at the start of summer without protection, would you burn, tan, or both?" Skin colour alone is not the same as phototype — record both self-reported burning/tanning and observed skin colour. Recognise limitations: Fitzpatrick was designed for white skin types and lumps diverse darker skin into V–VI; alternative descriptive scales exist (e.g. Monk scale, individual typology angle) but Fitzpatrick remains the clinical standard.

    Dermoscopy: why it adds value

    Dermoscopy is an adjunct to history + naked-eye examination, not a replacement. Structured algorithms exist (e.g. ABCD rule, 7-point checklist, 3-point checklist, "chaos and clues") — beyond the scope of this primer.

    Differentials

    Skin layers linked to lesion type

    Flaccid / erodes easilyTense / persists
    Pemphigus vulgaris; bullous impetigo; SSSS; TENBullous pemphigoid; bullous drug reaction; friction blister; burns; insect-bite bullae; porphyria cutanea tarda

    Fitzpatrick skin types I–VI

    • PIH vs melasma vs lichenification-associated pigmentation vs drug-induced pigmentation.

    Dermoscopy: why it adds value

    Accuracy depends on training; untrained use may be no better (or worse) than naked eye. Featureless or amelanotic lesions remain challenging — biopsy if in doubt.

    6

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    Skin layers linked to lesion type

    Histology level guides urgency: widespread blistering, mucosal involvement, skin pain/fever, or Nikolsky-positive skin → emergency/same-day referral (possible SJS/TEN, pemphigus, SSSS). Localised friction blisters → leave roof intact, protect, monitor for infection.

    Fitzpatrick skin types I–VI

    • Sun protection counselling by phototype: SPF ≥30 with high UVA rating (4–5 stars), shade, clothing; avoid sunbeds — especially I–II.
    • PIH prevention: treat inflammation early and effectively (acne, eczema); avoid picking; daily broad-spectrum sunscreen (tinted iron-oxide sunscreens help visible-light-driven pigmentation); caution with irritant topicals (retinoids/benzoyl peroxide — start low, slow).
    • Vitamin D: recommend OTC 10 microgram daily supplementation per UK advice for at-risk groups; refer/test per CKS if symptoms of deficiency (bone pain, proximal weakness) or high-risk.
    • Severity assessment: in darker skin, use palpation, thickness, lichenification, and patient-reported symptoms (POEM) rather than redness alone.

    Dermoscopy: why it adds value

    Pharmacist/IPs without accredited dermoscopy training should not use dermoscopy to reassure about a suspicious pigmented lesion; refer per NICE NG12 (weighted 7-point checklist; any lesion of concern). With training, dermoscopic photos support teledermatology referrals.

    7

    Red flags & referral

    • Changing or atypical pigmented lesion, including acral and nail sites in darker skin types: NICE NG12 referral.
    • Severity under-estimated in skin of colour: look for thickening, scale, pigment change and symptoms rather than redness alone.
    8

    Prescriber pearls

    Skin layers linked to lesion type

    • Use histology to explain treatment logic: e.g. emollients restore stratum corneum lipids; potent steroids in lichenification target the thickened epidermis; why steroids cause atrophy (dermal collagen suppression).

    Fitzpatrick skin types I–VI

    • Patients with skin of colour often prioritise pigment change over the active rash — address it explicitly and set timelines (PIH fades over months with sun protection).
    • Keloid-prone (V–VI) patients: counsel before piercings/elective procedures; treat inflammatory acne promptly.
    • Don't assume darker skin "can't burn" or doesn't need skin checks — acral melanoma awareness.

    Dermoscopy: why it adds value

    • "When in doubt, cut it out (or refer)" — a "benign-looking" dermoscopic pattern in a changing lesion still warrants referral. Document dermoscopy findings and whether images were stored.
