    Skin surgery: consent, complications & danger zones

    What to discuss when consenting for minor skin surgery (scar, infection, bleeding, nerve injury, recurrence), the anatomical danger zones of the face and neck, and safe practice: room, prep, specimen labelling and the histology fail-safe.

    Overview

    Module 2 · Week 6consentdehiscencehaematoma

    • Spot it

      Consenting, or answering a patient’s questions about the risks of minor skin surgery.

    • Why

      Tension, site, infection, bleeding and nerve anatomy drive most complications.

    • Treat

      Montgomery consent covering material risks; know the danger zones (temporal and marginal mandibular branches of the facial nerve, accessory nerve); label specimens at the bedside; run a histology fail-safe log.

    • Never miss

      Nerve injury in danger zones; lost or unlabelled specimens; missed malignant histology without a fail-safe.

    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    What it is

    Valid consent in the UK is informed, voluntary and given by a person with capacity. Under Montgomery v Lanarkshire* (2015) and the GMC Decision making and consent (2020), clinicians must discuss material risks: those a reasonable person in the patient's position would consider significant, or that this particular patient would. They must also discuss reasonable alternatives, including doing nothing.

    What you’ll see

    • Aseptic non-touch technique (ANTT), sterile gloves for excisions, eye protection (splash/plume), and surgical masks.
    • Surgical smoke from electrosurgery contains particulates and may carry viable HPV. Use smoke evacuation for diathermy (NHS/HSE guidance) and FFP masks where indicated.
    • Chlorhexidine 0.5% in 70% alcohol (or 2% chlorhexidine in alcohol) or povidone-iodine (aqueous or alcoholic).
    • Avoid chlorhexidine near the eyes (keratitis) and middle ear (ototoxicity). Use aqueous povidone-iodine there.
    • Fire risk: alcohol-based preps must dry completely before electrosurgery or diathermy. Pooled alcohol in hair or drapes has caused burns (NPSA/MHRA alerts). Wait about 3 minutes, more on hairy skin.
    • Hair removal (if needed) by clipping, not shaving (NICE NG125).

    Clinical features

    Why complications happen

    Bleeding (vascular site, antithrombotics), infection (contamination, site, host), tension (dehiscence, stretched scar), genetics (keloid), and anatomy (superficial nerves).

    Room, skin preparation and specimen handling

    Room and PPE

    • Minor skin surgery in primary care takes place in a clean treatment room meeting CQC and local infection prevention standards: washable surfaces, good lighting, an adjustable couch, a sharps bin, clinical waste, resuscitation equipment (anaphylaxis kit, AED access), and hand hygiene facilities.
    • Aseptic non-touch technique (ANTT), sterile gloves for excisions, eye protection (splash/plume), and surgical masks.
    • Surgical smoke from electrosurgery contains particulates and may carry viable HPV. Use smoke evacuation for diathermy (NHS/HSE guidance) and FFP masks where indicated.

    Skin preparation

    • Chlorhexidine 0.5% in 70% alcohol (or 2% chlorhexidine in alcohol) or povidone-iodine (aqueous or alcoholic).
    • Avoid chlorhexidine near the eyes (keratitis) and middle ear (ototoxicity). Use aqueous povidone-iodine there.
    • Fire risk: alcohol-based preps must dry completely before electrosurgery or diathermy. Pooled alcohol in hair or drapes has caused burns (NPSA/MHRA alerts). Wait about 3 minutes, more on hairy skin.
    • Hair removal (if needed) by clipping, not shaving (NICE NG125).

    Specimen handling

    • Every excised specimen goes for histology, as NICE IOG and good practice require. Do not discard specimens, even "obvious" SKs or PGs.
    • Label the pot at the time with two patient identifiers, the site (left/right, precise location) and the date. Use the correct fixative (10% neutral-buffered formalin, with about 10× the specimen volume). Use an orientation suture (e.g. "suture at 12 o'clock" or "long suture = superior") for malignancy excisions, so involved margins can be located.
    • The request form must carry clinical details: the provisional diagnosis, previous histology, immunosuppression, and the question being asked.
    • Fail-safe: keep a minor surgery log of all specimens, then check that every result has been received, read, communicated to the patient and acted on. Unexpected malignancy needs referral to the skin cancer MDT. This is a frequent root cause of patient-safety incidents.

    How to audit

    Annual audit of histology concordance (clinical vs histological diagnosis), complete-excision rates for malignancies, infection rates, and patient satisfaction (IOG requirement for accredited GPs).

    Pitfalls

    Unlabelled or mislabelled pots, missing request forms, results filed without action, and specimens lost in transport.

    Differentials

    (post-op)

    Normal healing redness vs infection (spreading erythema, pain, pus, fever); haematoma vs seroma; contact dermatitis from dressings or antiseptics (itchy, square-edged rash) vs infection.

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    • Support consent with written information (BAD/BSDS/PCDS leaflets) and time to decide. Document the material risks discussed and the alternatives (e.g. for a painful DF: leave alone, analgesia, or excision).
    • For complicated sites (eyelid margin, nose, lips, ears, danger zones) or high-risk patients, refer to dermatological surgery or plastics.

    Room, skin preparation and specimen handling

    IPs can own the results-tracking fail-safe in a practice, and can check that unexpected malignant histology is referred within NG12 timeframes.

    Step 6 of 7 · 1 min

    Prescriber pearls

    • "Every cut leaves a scar. The aim is a neat scar in a natural line, but we cannot guarantee how your skin heals."

    • Use the photos of the wound journey and complications to make the consent conversation concrete.

    • Tell patients when to expect results (often 2–6 weeks) and to chase if they hear nothing. "No news is not good news."
