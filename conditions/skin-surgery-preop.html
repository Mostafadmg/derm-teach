    Skin surgery: pre-operative checks, anticoagulants & cardiac devices

    The medication and patient checks before minor skin surgery: allergies, anticoagulants and antiplatelets (BSDS 2023: continue most), diabetes, immunosuppression, cardiac devices, and why NICE CG64 does not recommend endocarditis prophylaxis.

    Overview

    Module 2 · Week 6pre-op assessmentwarfarin skin surgeryDOAC skin surgery

    • Spot it

      A patient booked for an excision, curettage or biopsy, especially on anticoagulants, antiplatelets or with a pacemaker or valve.

    • Why

      Bleeding, infection, poor healing and device interference are mostly predictable from the medicine list and history.

    • Treat

      Continue antiplatelets and warfarin (INR under 3.5) and, for most procedures, DOACs (BSDS 2023); no routine endocarditis prophylaxis (NICE CG64); bipolar diathermy or cautery near devices.

    • Never miss

      Stopping anticoagulants in high thrombotic-risk patients; stopping DAPT after a recent stent; unchecked INR.

    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    What it is

    A structured check that the procedure is indicated, funded, safe and wanted. It happens before any skin surgery, however minor.

    Antithrombotics (BSDS 2023)

    Guidance on whether to continue, adjust or (rarely) pause antithrombotics for dermatological surgery. The guiding principle is that thrombotic harm from stopping usually outweighs bleeding harm from continuing.

    What you’ll see

    • "Bridging" with LMWH for minor skin surgery is not needed and increases bleeding.
    • Patients often stop aspirin themselves "to be safe". Tell them not to.
    • If the procedure is at a site of established infection, treat the infection with an antibiotic that covers organisms causing endocarditis (CG64 principle).
    • Clean skin surgery (most minor procedures): no routine antibiotic prophylaxis (NICE NG125: do not use routinely for clean, non-prosthetic, uncomplicated surgery).
    • Electrosurgery current can interfere with devices: inhibited pacing, inappropriate shocks, or reprogramming.
    • Preferred: electrocautery (heated wire; no current through the patient) or bipolar diathermy (current confined between the forceps tips), in short bursts (<5 s), at the lowest power, away from the device (>15 cm).
    More detailfull notes · 5 min

    Clinical features

    Why it matters

    Most complications of minor skin surgery are predictable from patient factors: bleeding (antithrombotics), infection (site, diabetes), poor healing (smoking, steroids, the lower leg), bad scars (keloid tendency, site), and allergy.

    History, medication reconciliation (including OTC and herbal products), allergy history with the reaction type, and examination of the site and regional nodes (if the lesion could be malignant).

    Antithrombotics (BSDS 2023)

    Evidence

    Stopping warfarin or antiplatelets for skin surgery has been associated with strokes, emboli and deaths. Bleeding complications on continued therapy are mostly minor and manageable (pressure, haemostasis, dressings).

    Recommendations (teaching summary; check the full BSDS document)

    DrugBSDS 2023 approach (summary)
    Aspirin, clopidogrel and other antiplatelets (including DAPT)Continue. Stopping DAPT after a recent stent risks stent thrombosis
    WarfarinContinue. Check the INR before surgery (on the day or within ~72 h). Proceed if INR <3.5 for most procedures. For higher-bleeding-risk procedures (large flaps/grafts), aim for the lower end of the patient's therapeutic range, liaising with the anticoagulation clinic
    DOACs (apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban, dabigatran)Continue for most procedures. For high-bleeding-risk procedures in patients at lower thrombotic risk, consider omitting the DOAC 24 h before (48 h if eGFR <50, especially dabigatran) and restarting ~24 h after once haemostasis is secure. Timing relative to a once-daily dose (e.g. schedule surgery before the next dose) is a practical alternative
    LMWHIndividualise. Often omit the dose on the morning of surgery (specialist/anticoagulation team)
    Highest thrombotic risk (do not interrupt without specialist advice)Valvular AF / mechanical valves, VTE within 3 months, CHA₂DS₂-VASc ≥5, recent stroke/TIA, recent coronary stent
    Platelet countGenerally >50 × 10⁹/L for most skin procedures

    Case 1 (warfarin for AF; small excision of an arm DF): continue warfarin; check the INR (on the day, per course teaching, or within 72 h per local policy); proceed if <3.5. Meticulous haemostasis; pressure dressing.

    How to check

    INR from the anticoagulation clinic or point-of-care testing. Renal function (eGFR/CrCl) for DOAC timing. Recent FBC if there is a bleeding history.

    Pitfalls

    • "Bridging" with LMWH for minor skin surgery is not needed and increases bleeding.
    • Patients often stop aspirin themselves "to be safe". Tell them not to.

    Antibiotic prophylaxis and cardiac devices

    Infective endocarditis

    • NICE CG64 (updated 2016): antibiotic prophylaxis against infective endocarditis is not recommended routinely for people at risk (including those with prosthetic valves or previous endocarditis) undergoing dental, GI, GU, respiratory or other interventional procedures. This includes skin surgery.
    • If the procedure is at a site of established infection, treat the infection with an antibiotic that covers organisms causing endocarditis (CG64 principle).
    • The SDCEP/ESC positions differ for dentistry in high-risk patients (ESC 2023 still recommends prophylaxis for high-risk dental procedures), but NICE CG64 is the England standard. Individual cardiology advice can be sought for the highest-risk patients.

    Surgical site infection prophylaxis

    • Clean skin surgery (most minor procedures): no routine antibiotic prophylaxis (NICE NG125: do not use routinely for clean, non-prosthetic, uncomplicated surgery).
    • Specialists may consider prophylaxis in selected higher-risk situations: procedures below the knee, groin, wedge excisions of the lip or ear, nasal flaps and grafts, and inflamed or ulcerated lesions with clinical infection. The decision should be individual, ideally a single pre-operative dose rather than a week-long course.
    • The course "1 week of prophylaxis for ulcerated lesions" is not standard UK practice (stewardship; adverse effects; C. difficile). Treat established infection instead (see Aftercare: sutures, return to work and sport, infection and scars).

    Pacemakers and ICDs

    • Electrosurgery current can interfere with devices: inhibited pacing, inappropriate shocks, or reprogramming.
    • Preferred: electrocautery (heated wire; no current through the patient) or bipolar diathermy (current confined between the forceps tips), in short bursts (<5 s), at the lowest power, away from the device (>15 cm).
    • Avoid monopolar diathermy near the device. With an ICD, follow the BSDS cardiac devices flowchart: contact the cardiac physiology team about suspending tachytherapy if monopolar diathermy is essential, and have monitoring and a defibrillator available.
    • Ask about devices at pre-assessment (see Pre-operative assessment: patient factors).

    –9.5 Other cardiac points

    • Recent MI (course teaching: "defer for 6 months"): elective procedures are usually deferred after ACS, but a short procedure under LA is low risk. Timing is individual, and cancer surgery should not be delayed unnecessarily. Never stop DAPT after a stent without cardiology input.
    • Uncontrolled hypertension: check BP before surgery (adrenaline; bleeding).

    More detailfull notes · 3 min

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    Lead the medicines and allergy part of the assessment: produce a clear pre-op medicines plan, including the antithrombotic plan, which β-blocker the patient takes, and the allergy list with reaction type.

    Antithrombotics (BSDS 2023)

    • Write a clear peri-operative antithrombotic plan in the record and give it to the patient (date of surgery, doses to take or omit, restart time).
    • Post-operative analgesia: paracetamol first-line. Avoid NSAIDs (and aspirin as an analgesic) in anticoagulated patients.
    • Post-operative antibiotics in warfarin patients: many antibiotics raise the INR (clarithromycin, erythromycin, metronidazole, co-trimoxazole, fluoroquinolones, doxycycline) or lower it (rifampicin; flucloxacillin can reduce it). Check the INR within 3–5 days if starting an interacting antibiotic, and inform the anticoagulation service.
    • DOAC interactions: clarithromycin raises levels (P-gp/CYP3A4); rifampicin lowers them.

    Antibiotic prophylaxis and cardiac devices

    Answer the "do I need antibiotics, I have a heart valve?" question with CG64: no routine prophylaxis. Advise good oral hygiene and prompt treatment of infections, and tell the patient to seek medical advice for symptoms of endocarditis (fever, night sweats, new murmur, weight loss).

    Worked cases

    Case 1: 54-year-old, painful arm DF; warfarin for AF; latex allergy; 60 kg

    • Is removal indicated? Yes. It is symptomatic (painful), which meets most commissioning criteria. An elliptical excision along RSTL with histology (the DF diagnosis is confirmed and variants excluded).
    • Warfarin: continue; check the INR on the day (or within 72 h, per local policy); proceed if INR <3.5 (BSDS 2023); meticulous haemostasis, a pressure dressing, and paracetamol for pain (no NSAIDs); check the INR if an interacting antibiotic is started
    • Latex allergy: first on the list, a latex-free room and kit, an anaphylaxis kit ready (IM adrenaline 500 micrograms of 1 mg/ml), and allergy documented. Check the chlorhexidine allergy status too.
    • Consent (material risks): bleeding/haematoma (higher on warfarin); infection; scar (≈3× lesion length; hypertrophic/keloid on the arm/shoulder); wound dehiscence; numbness/nerve injury; recurrence; histology result

    Alternatives: leave it, or analgesia.

    • LA maximum (lidocaine with adrenaline, teaching): 7 mg/kg × 60 kg = 420 mg = 21 ml of 2% (the course "~20 ml"), within the BNF absolute maximum of 500 mg. Plain lidocaine: BNF maximum 200 mg (≈3 mg/kg = 180 mg = 9 ml of 2%). In practice, a few ml suffices.
    • Adrenaline: acceptable unless the patient is on a non-selective β-blocker such as propranolol or sotalol. Rate control in AF is often with bisoprolol, which is cardioselective and lower risk, so check which β-blocker they take. Use caution in Raynaud's.

    Case 2: 21-year-old builder/footballer, 9 mm bleeding PG on the posterior neck

    • Diagnosis: pyogenic granuloma (lobular capillary haemangioma). Exclude amelanotic melanoma, so histology is mandatory.
    • Treatment: curettage and cautery (shave/curette and cauterise the base) under LA, with the specimen to histology. Excision with layered closure is reserved for recurrence (deep absorbable plus surface nylon, as course teaching described).
    • Aftercare: keep dry and covered for 24–48 h; petroleum jelly; no football, heavy lifting or contact sport for ~2 weeks; 1–2 weeks of light duties (fit note with adjustments); neck sutures (if used) out at 7–10 days; return if bleeding or infection; recurrence is possible (up to 15%)
    • Anatomy: posterior triangle, so the spinal accessory nerve is superficial. Superficial C&C is safe; deeper excision needs care.

    Case 3: see the melanoma teledermatology case

    See the melanoma notes. In brief: Breslow 1.4 mm ulcerated = T2b, stage IIA if N0, ~93–94% 5-year MSS; WLE 2 cm; consider SLNB; no adjuvant at IIA.

    Prescriber pearls

    • Ask "Do you or your family form thick or raised scars?" Keloid-prone patients need to be told before surgery on high-risk sites.

    • Patients with valves may have been told "antibiotics before the dentist" in the past (pre-2008 practice). Explain the change and its basis (resistance and anaphylaxis risk outweighed unproven benefit).

    More detailfull notes · 2 min

    Prescriber pearls

    • Ask "Do you or your family form thick or raised scars?" Keloid-prone patients need to be told before surgery on high-risk sites.

    Antithrombotics (BSDS 2023)

    • Bleeding first aid after surgery: firm continuous pressure for 20 minutes by the clock with a clean pad, sitting upright, without peeking. If bleeding persists, or there is a rapidly expanding, tense, painful swelling (haematoma), seek same-day review.

    Antibiotic prophylaxis and cardiac devices

    • Patients with valves may have been told "antibiotics before the dentist" in the past (pre-2008 practice). Explain the change and its basis (resistance and anaphylaxis risk outweighed unproven benefit).
