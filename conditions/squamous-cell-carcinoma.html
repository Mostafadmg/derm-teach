    Lumps, lesions & skin cancer

    Cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC)

    Module 2 · Week 3

    A growing, tender, keratotic or ulcerated nodule on sun-damaged skin that can spread to lymph nodes, especially on the lip and ear. Know the precursors (AK, Bowen’s, actinic cheilitis) and refer on the suspected-cancer pathway.

    CoversSCCcSCClip SCCkeratoacanthomacutaneous hornMarjolin ulcerBowen’s diseaseactinic cheilitistransplant skin cancer
    Think of it when

    A tender, growing, crusted or keratotic nodule or non-healing ulcer on the face, lip, ear, scalp, hands or a chronic wound.

    What drives it

    Cumulative UV, immunosuppression (organ transplant), HPV, chronic wounds and scars; arises from AK or Bowen’s disease.

    Backbone of care

    Suspected cancer pathway (NG12 1.7.4); surgical excision first-line; check nodes; transplant patients need specialist surveillance.

    Never miss

    Lip and ear SCC (higher metastatic risk), SCC in a chronic ulcer or scar, rapid keratoacanthoma-like growth, immunosuppressed patients.

    What it is

    A malignant tumour of keratinocytes. It is the second commonest skin cancer, with real potential to metastasise (overall about 2–5%; higher for high-risk tumours). Lip and ear SCCs metastasise more often (lip around 10–15% in older series).

    SCC precursors and related lesions

    Lesions that sit on the UV → keratinocyte dysplasia → SCC pathway, plus clinical forms that conceal SCC.

    2

    How it’s caused

    • Cumulative UV (outdoor workers, as in Case 3), older age, fair skin.
    • Immunosuppression: organ transplant recipients have 65–250× the risk; CLL; HIV.
    • Drugs: azathioprine, voriconazole, hydrochlorothiazide, BRAF inhibitors (vemurafenib monotherapy: KA-type SCCs).
    • HPV.
    • Chronic wounds, scars and burns (Marjolin ulcer, ).
    • Radiation, arsenic, smoking (lip), lichen sclerosus/planus (genital and oral).
    • Genetic: xeroderma pigmentosum, recessive dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa, albinism.
    • Path: UV-signature TP53 mutations progress through AK or Bowen's to invasive SCC (see SCC precursors and related lesions).

    Clinical features

    • An indurated, tender keratotic nodule or plaque, or an ulcer with hard everted edges, growing over weeks to months, often bleeding.
    • Sun-exposed sites: scalp, ears, lower lip, the back of the hands, and the lower legs in women.
    • Lip SCC: usually the lower lip vermilion (more UV), often on a background of actinic cheilitis. Smoking or pipe use. Persistent ulcer or crust, induration.
    • Variants: well-differentiated/keratinising, cutaneous horn, verrucous carcinoma/carcinoma cuniculatum (plantar, slow, locally destructive; ), keratoacanthoma-type (the benign lesion notes see Cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC), including lip SCC), poorly differentiated, acantholytic, desmoplastic.

    SCC precursors and related lesions

    Signs that an AK or Bowen's has become SCC (refer under NG12 1.7.4):

    • induration/thickening
    • tenderness/pain
    • rapid growth
    • bleeding
    • ulceration
    • diameter >1 cm
    • failure to respond to treatment.
    4

    Diagnosis

    • Clinical + palpation of regional lymph nodes (pre-auricular, parotid, cervical, submental/submandibular for the lip; ).
    • Biopsy: excision biopsy is preferred where feasible. Otherwise an incisional/punch biopsy deep enough to assess depth.
    • Pathology report items: differentiation, thickness/depth, perineural and lymphovascular invasion, margins.
    • Staging: the AJCC 8th edition (head and neck cSCC) or UICC 8. The BAD 2020 risk categories (low, high, very high) guide management.
    • Imaging (specialist): ultrasound ± FNA of clinically suspicious nodes; CT/MRI for very high-risk tumours or perineural symptoms. Consider SLNB only in trials (not standard UK practice).

    SCC precursors and related lesions

    Clinical + dermoscopy (Bowen's: glomerular vessels in clusters + scale). Biopsy if there is doubt or atypia.

    5

    Differentials

    KA, BCC, AK (hypertrophic), Bowen's, irritated SK, viral wart, amelanotic melanoma, Merkel cell carcinoma (rapid red-violet nodule in the elderly), chancre (syphilis; lip), herpes labialis (it heals), aphthous ulcer, traumatic ulcer, and for the lip discoid lupus or lichen planus.

    SCC precursors and related lesions

    For Bowen's: psoriasis, eczema, superficial BCC, tinea (scrape), and extramammary Paget's (genital).

    6

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    Recognition and referral

    • NICE NG12 1.7.4: consider a suspected cancer pathway referral (appointment within 2 weeks) for suspected cSCC. Case 3 (a 6-week indurated bleeding lip ulcer in an outdoor worker) should be referred on that pathway. Make the referral within 1 working day of deciding.
    • Any lip or oral ulcer that persists for more than 3 weeks also meets NG12 head-and-neck/oral cancer criteria (suspected cancer pathway or urgent dental referral).
    • Do not: freeze, curette or treat a suspected SCC in primary care unless you are an accredited skin-surgery practitioner working to the IOG.

    Specialist management (BAD 2020; teaching)

    Risk groupPeripheral clinical margin (excision)Notes
    Low risk4 mmAim for ≥1 mm histological clearance
    High risk6 mmLip and ear are high-risk by site
    Very high risk10 mmConsider adjuvant radiotherapy; MDT
    • Surgery is first-line (the course answer). Lip: wedge or V-excision ± vermilionectomy for associated actinic cheilitis (maxillofacial/plastics).
    • Mohs: selected high-risk facial tumours.
    • Radiotherapy: primary treatment if surgery is unsuitable; adjuvant for close/positive margins, perineural invasion or nodal disease.
    • Nodal disease: neck dissection ± parotidectomy, then adjuvant RT.
    • Advanced/metastatic: cemiplimab (anti-PD-1; NICE TA802, 2022).
    • Follow-up: BAD suggests clinical review for high-risk tumours (e.g. 3–6-monthly for 2 years), plus education about self-examination of the scar and nodes.
    • Immunosuppressed/transplant patients: joint care with the transplant team. Specialists may switch to mTOR inhibitors (sirolimus) or use acitretin chemoprevention. Oral nicotinamide 500 mg twice daily reduced new keratinocyte cancers in immunocompetent high-risk patients (ONTRAC 2015), but showed no benefit in a 2023 trial in transplant recipients; it is a specialist recommendation.

    SIGN 140 / BAD high-risk features:

    • Site: lip, ear, non-sun-exposed sites, or within scars or chronic wounds.
    • Diameter >20 mm.
    • Thickness >4 mm or invasion beyond the subcutaneous fat.
    • Poor differentiation.
    • Perineural or lymphovascular invasion.
    • Recurrence.
    • Immunosuppression.

    SCC precursors and related lesions

    • AK: IP-manageable field therapy per the older skin pages notes see Worked cases (5-FU, diclofenac 3%, imiquimod, tirbanibulin; check the BNF/formulary).
    • Bowen's disease: treatment is usually a confirmed diagnosis + GP/dermatology plan. An IP may prescribe topical 5-FU 5% within a documented specialist or GP plan after diagnosis (e.g. once or twice daily for 3–4 weeks; local protocol).
    • Actinic cheilitis: refer for diagnosis. Topical options are specialist-initiated (lip reactions can be severe).
    7

    Red flags & referral

    • Suspected cSCC: suspected cancer pathway referral (NICE NG12 1.7.4).
    • Any lip or mouth ulcer persisting more than 3 weeks: suspected head-and-neck cancer pathway.
    • A non-healing area or new growth in a long-standing ulcer or scar: biopsy to exclude SCC (Marjolin ulcer).
    8

    Prescriber pearls

    • Review hydrochlorothiazide (MHRA) and voriconazole (photosensitivity; consider an alternative azole for long-term use in high-risk patients), and remind transplant patients of photoprotection.
    • Smoking cessation: lip/oral SCC risk and wound healing.
    • Lip sun protection: an SPF lip balm.
    • Teach patients to report a new lump in the scar or the neck.

    SCC precursors and related lesions

    • 5-FU: expect redness, crusting and soreness. Do not apply near the eyes or mucosa; wash hands after.
    • Topical 5-FU is minimally absorbed, but never use it with brivudine (fatal DPD inhibition), and take care over large areas in patients with known DPD deficiency.
    • Mark lesions on a body map for follow-up.
