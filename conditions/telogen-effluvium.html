    Telogen effluvium

    Diffuse shedding of club hairs 2–3 months after a trigger. Reassure, find reversible causes, and cross-link pattern hair loss rather than duplicating AGA photo sets.

    Also calledtelogen effluviumdiffuse hair sheddingclub hairspostpartum hair lossTE
    Related pagesAndrogenetic alopecia (male & female)Alopecia areataTrichotillomania

    Key facts

    • Telogen effluvium is diffuse shedding about 2–3 months after a trigger, when many follicles shift into telogen together.
    • Triggers: childbirth, febrile illness (including COVID-19), surgery, crash diets, iron deficiency, thyroid disease, new drugs.
    • Positive hair-pull test (more than 4–6 club hairs) in the acute phase; no scarring, no miniaturisation.
    • Check ferritin, thyroid function and full blood count if the trigger is unclear.
    • Reassure: regrowth over 6–12 months once the trigger has gone; chronic TE lasts longer than 6 months.

    Overview

    Telogen effluvium (TE) is non-scarring diffuse shedding of telogen (club) hairs. Acute TE follows a trigger by about 2–3 months. Chronic TE lasts beyond 6 months.

    Androgenetic (pattern) hair loss is the other common diffuse-looking thinning of mid-life — full UK IP detail and Module 6 Week 6 photos live on androgenetic alopecia. This page only sketches the DDx so Week 3 teaching stays joined up without duplicating that photo set.

    Epidemiology

    • Who
      • Common at any age, more often reported by women.
      • Postpartum shedding affects many women 2–4 months after delivery.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    • Classic triggers: high fever, COVID/flu-like illness, major surgery, postpartum (including when stopping breastfeeding), crash diets, haemorrhage, new drugs (retinoids, anticoagulants, antithyroid, beta-blockers, some anticonvulsants — check BNF), severe stress.
    • Iron deficiency, thyroid disease and other metabolic insults.
    • Chronic TE: ongoing trigger or idiopathic; overlap with female pattern loss is common.

    Risk factors and associations

    • Physiological stress, hormonal change, nutritional deficiency or drugs (retinoids, anticoagulants, beta-blockers, stopping the combined pill).

    Myths to address

    • "Shedding means I'll go bald": follicles are alive and will regrow.
    • "Hair supplements cure it": only replace a proven deficiency.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    A shock synchronises hair cycles so many hairs rest and then fall together.

    1. 1

      Normal cycle

      Normally about 85–90% of scalp hairs are growing (anagen) and 10–15% resting (telogen) at any time.

    2. 2

      Trigger

      A physiological shock pushes many anagen follicles into early telogen at once.

    3. 3

      Delayed shedding

      Telogen lasts about 3 months, so club hairs shed in large numbers 2–3 months after the trigger.

    4. 4

      Recovery

      New anagen hairs push out the old ones; short regrowing hairs at the hairline are a good sign.

    Histopathology

    • Biopsy only if unclear: normal follicle numbers with an increased proportion of telogen follicles; no miniaturisation or inflammation.

    Clinical features

    • Diffuse thinning; parted hair looks thinner; no discrete bald patch.
    • Increased daily shed; club hairs with a white bulb on the brush (photo teaching: D10).
    • Pull test: many club hairs come out easily when active.
    • Scalp skin looks normal; ostia preserved.

    Brief AGA contrast (cross-link)

    • Pattern loss is gradual miniaturisation over years (male temples/vertex; female crown with widened part, frontal hairline usually kept).
    • Management (minoxidil, male finasteride MHRA mood warnings, etc.) is covered on the androgenetic alopecia page, with its own photo gallery.

    Special groups

    • Postpartum
      • Shedding peaks 3–4 months after delivery and usually settles by 12 months.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    • History timed to a trigger 2–3 months earlier.
    • Examine for patches, scale, inflammation, pattern miniaturisation.
    • Bloods when indicated: FBC, ferritin, TFT (± others guided by history).
    • Child with a scaly patch → mycology (tinea), not TE.

    Key points

    • History timed to a trigger 2–3 months earlier.
    • Examine for patches, scale, inflammation, pattern miniaturisation.
    • Bloods when indicated: FBC, ferritin, TFT (± others guided by history).
    • Child with a scaly patch → mycology (tinea), not TE.

    Management

    • Reassure: acute TE usually regrows over 6–12 months once the trigger has passed; shedding can look alarming but follicles are alive.
    • Correct reversible causes (iron if deficient, thyroid replacement optimisation, review culprit drugs with the original prescriber).
    • Gentle hair care; avoid crash diets; camouflage if wanted.
    • Do not promise “hair vitamins” as evidence-based cure-alls.
    • If patterned miniaturisation coexists, manage as on the AGA page.
    • Refer if diagnostic doubt, progressive patterned loss with virilisation, scarring signs, or severe distress.

    Referral

    • Routine

      Virilisation or sudden severe FPHL → endocrine/tumour pathways.

    • Routine

      Patchy, scaly or shiny bald areas — not TE.

    • Urgent

      Kerion — urgent antifungal pathway.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Virilisation or sudden severe FPHL → endocrine/tumour pathways.
    • Patchy, scaly or shiny bald areas — not TE.
    • Kerion — urgent antifungal pathway.

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Anxiety; may unmask underlying androgenetic alopecia.

    Prognosis

    • Acute TE resolves in most within 6–12 months; chronic TE fluctuates over years but rarely causes baldness.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • Always ask “what happened 2–3 months ago?”

    • Postpartum shedding is physiological — counsel antenatally when possible.

    • Teaching grid: TE (D09) vs FPHL (D06) vs extensive AA — different time courses and trichoscopy.

    Self-check quiz

    5 questions · optional
    1. Q1When should you think of Telogen effluvium?Reveal

      Sudden increase in hairs in the brush or shower, diffuse thinning, no bald patches — often 2–3 months after illness, childbirth or a new drug.

    2. Q2What drives it?Reveal

      A stressor synchronises many anagen follicles into telogen; club hairs shed together weeks later.

    3. Q3What is the backbone of care?Reveal

      Identify the trigger, check thyroid/ferritin when indicated, reassure that acute TE usually settles in 6–12 months; treat coexistent pattern loss separately.

    4. Q4What must you never miss?Reveal

      Patchy loss, scale, or lost ostia are not TE — rethink AA, tinea or scarring alopecia.

    5. Q5Name a red flag that needs escalation.Reveal

      Virilisation or sudden severe FPHL → endocrine/tumour pathways.
