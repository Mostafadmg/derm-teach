    Infections — fungal

    Tinea: corporis, cruris & pedis

    Adult core · deepened with Module 5 Week 1

    Dermatophyte ‘ringworm’ of the body, groin and feet. Look for an asymmetrical scaly active edge, examine the feet, use a topical allylamine or azole past clearance, and never a steroid alone.

    Coversringwormtinea corporistinea crurisjock itchtinea pedisathlete's footdermatophytetinea incognitoterbinafinefungal infectionEpidermophytonTrichophyton interdigitale
    Think of it when

    An asymmetrical annular plaque with a red, scaly advancing edge and central clearing; macerated toe webs; a groin rash that spares the scrotum.

    What drives it

    Keratin-loving dermatophytes (mostly Trichophyton rubrum) spreading outwards in the stratum corneum.

    Backbone of care

    Keep dry, apply topical terbinafine or an imidazole until 1–2 weeks after clearance, and treat the feet and nails too.

    Never miss

    Tinea incognito (steroid-modified), kerion, Majocchi granuloma, and immunosuppression.

    What it is

    FormPlain definition
    Tinea corporis‘Ringworm’ of the body skin
    Tinea crurisDermatophyte infection of the groin (‘jock itch’)
    Tinea pedisAthlete’s foot: interdigital, moccasin or vesicular

    All three are caused by dermatophytes, most often Trichophyton rubrum. They are contagious, spreading by spores and fomites (towels, floors, clothing) and from animals.

    Tinea manuum and pedis on the palms and soles (Module 3 Week 6)

    • dermatophyte infection of the hand (tinea manuum — much less common) and foot (tinea pedis — very common). Usual organisms Trichophyton rubrum, T. interdigitale, Epidermophyton floccosum; zoophilic (Microsporum canis, T. verrucosum, T. erinacei) cause inflamed lesions.
    2

    How it’s caused

    Dermatophytes are keratin-loving fungi that invade the stratum corneum (and hair and nails). The infection spreads outwards in a ring, which produces the active edge with central clearing.

    Autoinoculation is common: infection spreads from the feet and nails to the groin and body.

    • Risk factors: occlusion, sweating, sport, shared towels and changing rooms.
    • Pets and farm animals: zoophilic species cause more inflamed, pustular lesions.

    Tinea cruris risk factors (Module 5)

    Hot, humid weather; sweating and sport; tight, occlusive clothing; diabetes; and immunosuppression. The usual organism is T. rubrum, with T. interdigitale and Epidermophyton floccosum also seen.

    Why steroids make tinea worse

    A topical steroid suppresses the local immune response that is holding the fungus back. The redness fades, but the dermatophyte spreads freely. The result is tinea incognito: a larger, less scaly, atypical rash that is easily mistaken for eczema.

    3

    Clinical features

    Tinea corporis

    One or more asymmetrical annular plaques with an active red, scaly, advancing edge and central clearing. Usually itchy.

    • Tinea incognito: altered by steroid, so less scaly and more widespread.
    • Majocchi granuloma: a deeper, follicular infection, often after shaving or steroid use.
    • Kerion-like inflammation: boggy and pustular, typical of zoophilic species.

    Tinea cruris

    Affects the inguinal folds, with or without the inner thighs. The scaly edge may be less obvious in moist folds. The scrotum is usually spared, whereas Candida often involves it.

    Tinea cruris often starts on one side or asymmetrically. It can spread to the thighs and buttocks, and sometimes has vesicles at the active edge. The scrotum is usually spared, but this is a teaching rule rather than an absolute one.

    Tinea pedis

    • Interdigital: maceration, peeling and fissures between the toes, especially the 4th–5th web space.
    • Moccasin: dry, hyperkeratotic scaling of the sole and side of the foot.
    • Vesicobullous: inflamed vesicles on the instep.

    Tinea manuum and pedis on the palms and soles (Module 3 Week 6)

    • Clues: "two feet–one hand" syndrome (hyperkeratotic scale on one palm + both soles); white scale accentuating creases (vs red lichenified creases in eczema); asymmetric; spread contiguous from palm to dorsum with a raised edge; nails (onychomycosis); toe-web maceration; moccasin scaling of soles. Risk: manual work, hyperhidrosis, pre-existing hand eczema, animal/soil contact.
    • Steroid trap: topical steroid alters appearance (tinea incognito) — scrape before treating any unexplained unilateral palm rash.
    4

    Diagnosis

    • Clinical pattern plus risk factors.
    • Take skin scrapings from the active edge for microscopy, with or without culture, if: the diagnosis is uncertain, treatment has failed, before starting oral antifungals, for extensive disease, or if immunocompromised.
    • Always examine the feet, nails and hands when the groin or body is involved.
    • Swab the folds if bacterial co-infection is suspected.

    Tinea manuum and pedis on the palms and soles (Module 3 Week 6)

    • skin scrapings (± nail clippings) for microscopy and culture.

    Differentials

    FormLook-alikesHow to tell apart
    Tinea corporisDiscoid eczema, psoriasis, herald patch (pityriasis rosea), granuloma annulareAsymmetry and a scaly active edge; mycology. Granuloma annulare has no scale
    Tinea crurisCandidal intertrigo, flexural psoriasis, erythrasmaMycology. Candida: satellites and scrotal involvement. Erythrasma: coral-red under Wood’s light. Psoriasis: sharper edge and other sites involved
    Tinea pedisEczema, psoriasis, contact dermatitisToe-web maceration; check the nails; often one-sided at first
    6

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    Self-care (all forms)

    • Keep the skin clean and dry, especially the toe webs and folds. Wear loose cotton clothing.
    • Do not share towels. Wash clothing and bedding hot.
    • Treat any foot or nail infection at the same time.
    • Children with body or groin tinea do not usually need to be kept off school (CKS).

    Tinea corporis/cruris: mild, localised

    • Topical terbinafine, or an imidazole (clotrimazole, miconazole, econazole). Continue for 1–2 weeks after the skin looks clear.
    • If very inflamed, add a mild steroid (e.g. hydrocortisone 1%) for a maximum of 7 days. Never use a steroid alone.

    Extensive or severe disease

    • Adults: oral terbinafine first line, ideally after sending scrapings. Alternatives are itraconazole and griseofulvin.
    • Children with extensive disease: refer to paediatric dermatology.

    Tinea pedis

    • Same topical classes. Allylamines (terbinafine) are often preferred for dermatophytes.
    • Advise on drying the toe webs and on footwear. Treat onychomycosis through its separate pathway.
    • Consider oral therapy if severe, if topicals fail, or if immunocompromised. Check LFTs and interactions for terbinafine and itraconazole.

    Tinea manuum and pedis on the palms and soles (Module 3 Week 6)

    • tinea pedis — topical terbinafine 1% (1–2 weeks) or an imidazole (clotrimazole/miconazole, ~4 weeks); foot hygiene, dry webs, change socks, treat shoes. Tinea manuum (hyperkeratotic palm) often needs oral terbinafine (adult 250 mg daily, typically 2–6 weeks) after mycology confirmation; check LFTs/drug interactions per BNF; treat feet/nails simultaneously to prevent re-infection. Refer if diagnosis uncertain or immunosuppressed.
    7

    Red flags & referral

    • Diagnostic doubt, or failure despite good adherence: take scrapings and reconsider.
    • Kerion or Majocchi granuloma, or suspected deep infection.
    • Immunocompromised patient with extensive or atypical disease.
    • Children needing oral treatment.
    • Occupational or institutional outbreak.
    • Tinea pedis in a person with diabetes or a history of cellulitis: fissured toe webs are an entry point for cellulitis.
    8

    Prescriber pearls

    • Always look at the feet in a ‘groin rash’.
    • A steroid–antifungal combination is a short bridge only (up to 7 days). Explain why steroid on its own is harmful.
    • Oral terbinafine: check interactions and LFTs if used for a prolonged period. Taste disturbance is uncommon but worth mentioning.
    • Azoles: watch for CYP interactions, especially with oral itraconazole and fluconazole.
    • OTC clotrimazole and terbinafine are widely available and are a good first pharmacy intervention for limited tinea.
    • Recurrent tinea cruris means an untreated reservoir: the feet, the nails, or another family member.
    • Groin rash in a person with diabetes: also think of Candida and secondary bacterial infection.
    • Thick, discoloured toenails with tinea pedis: see fungal nail infection and send clippings before oral treatment.
    • Scaly rings that fail antifungals: work through the annular rash differential.
