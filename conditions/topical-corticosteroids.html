    Therapeutics

    Topical corticosteroids: potency, quantities & safety

    How topical steroids work, the UK potency ladder, matching potency to age, site and severity, fingertip-unit quantities, adverse effects and when not to use them.

    Module 1 · Week 2 · 11 images

    Also coversTCSsteroid creampotency ladderfingertip unitFTUsteroid atrophytopical steroid withdrawalTSWhydrocortisoneclobetasol
    Think of it when

    Choosing, quantifying or reviewing a topical steroid for eczema, psoriasis or another inflammatory rash.

    What drives it

    Glucocorticoid-receptor effects switch off inflammatory genes, constrict vessels and slow cell turnover; the same effects thin skin with overuse.

    Backbone of care

    Lowest potency that works for the shortest time; potency by site and age (BNF); fingertip units; review rather than repeat; step down once controlled.

    Never miss

    Tinea incognito, steroid rosacea and perioral dermatitis, atrophy and striae, topical steroid withdrawal, and adrenal suppression with large amounts of potent steroid.

    01

    What it is

    Synthetic analogues of cortisol applied to the skin for their anti-inflammatory, immunosuppressive, anti-proliferative and vasoconstrictive effects. They are the mainstay for eczema/dermatitis flares. They are also used in psoriasis (with vitamin D), lichen planus, lichen sclerosus, discoid lupus, insect-bite reactions and many other inflammatory dermatoses.

    Systemic glucocorticoids covered on Case 1 (approximate anti-inflammatory potency vs hydrocortisone = 1): cortisone 0.8; prednisolone 4; methylprednisolone 5; dexamethasone and betamethasone ~25–30. Topical potency is a separate scale (see Topical corticosteroids). It depends on the molecule, its esterification (more lipophilic → better skin penetration), the concentration and the vehicle.

    03

    Clinical features

    How it works

    • Genomic (classical) pathway: the lipophilic steroid crosses the cell membrane and binds the cytoplasmic glucocorticoid receptor (GR), which is released from heat-shock proteins. The complex then moves into the nucleus: Transactivation: the complex binds glucocorticoid response elements and switches on anti-inflammatory genes. Examples: annexin-1 (lipocortin-1), which inhibits phospholipase A2 → less arachidonic acid → fewer prostaglandins and leukotrienes; also MKP-1, IκBα and IL-10; Transrepression: the GR "tethers" to the pro-inflammatory transcription factors NF-κB and AP-1 and blocks them. This switches off cytokines (IL-1, IL-2, IL-6, TNF-α), chemokines, adhesion molecules and COX-2, and probably drives most of the anti-inflammatory effect
    • Non-genomic (rapid) pathways: mostly, but not entirely. Rapid effects (seconds to minutes) occur through (a) membrane-bound GR, (b) cytosolic GR signalling that needs no gene transcription, and (c) at high concentrations, non-specific physicochemical membrane interactions that need no receptor at all. Vasoconstriction (the basis of the potency assay) appears within hours and is partly non-genomic.
    • Cell effects that matter in skin: inhibition of Langerhans cells, T cells, mast-cell mediator release and eosinophils; an anti-proliferative effect on keratinocytes (useful in psoriasis); and inhibition of fibroblast collagen and hyaluronic-acid synthesis. That last effect causes atrophy, striae and purpura; course teaching singled it out.
    • Potency testing: the McKenzie–Stoughton vasoconstrictor (skin-blanching) assay tracks clinical potency and underlies the BNF classes.

    Clinical uses & where it fits — the UK potency ladder

    UK practice uses four BNF classes. The US uses seven (I = super-potent → VII = least potent). The two systems do not line up product by product, so use UK classes for UK prescribing and teaching.

    Combination products take the potency of their steroid. Examples: steroid + antimicrobial (Fucibet, Fucidin H, Daktacort, Trimovate, Timodine, Canesten HC) and steroid + salicylic acid (Diprosalic). The course point: use them only when infection and inflammation genuinely coexist, and only briefly (e.g. fusidic acid for ≤1–2 weeks because of resistance), then continue the plain steroid. If there is no response, recheck the diagnosis.

    The vehicle matters:

    • Ointments are greasy and occlusive, with the fewest preservatives. Best for dry or lichenified skin and for eczema.
    • Creams suit weeping or hair-bearing skin and are more acceptable to patients, but their preservatives can cause contact allergy.
    • Lotions, scalp applications, foams and mousses are for hairy areas.
    • Occlusion (wet wraps, hydrocolloid, steroid tape such as fludroxycortide) raises effective potency.

    Regional absorption (approximate, forearm = 1): soles ~0.1, palms ~0.8, scalp ~3.5, face ~7–13, eyelids/genitals up to ~40. Thin, flexural and infant skin absorbs more, which is why the face, flexures and infants get mild potency.

    How to choose / assess

    • Match potency to severity, site and age. Use the lowest potency that works, for the shortest time. In practice, under-treatment from steroid phobia is commoner than harm: NICE CG57 (children): mild for mild eczema, moderate for moderate, potent for severe; Do not use potent steroids on the face/neck, or very potent steroids in children, without specialist advice.; On the face/neck, use moderate potency for only 3–5 days in severe flares; Apply once or twice daily; once daily is usually enough for potent preparations
    • Step down once the eczema is controlled. For frequently relapsing eczema, consider proactive "weekend" therapy (twice weekly to the usual flare sites) (NICE CG57 / CKS).
    • Assess before every re-issue: how often repeats are requested, the amount used (FTU arithmetic, see Topical corticosteroids), sites, signs of atrophy, alternative diagnoses (scabies, tinea, contact allergy), infection, and emollient adherence.
    • Signs of steroid overuse: Epidermal/dermal atrophy: shiny, thin, translucent skin with fine "cigarette-paper" wrinkling, visible veins and loss of skin markings; Telangiectasia (especially on the face), steroid rosacea, and perioral/periorificial dermatitis; Striae (irreversible; flexures, inner thighs, axillae; adolescents in growth spurts), purpura/easy bruising, stellate pseudoscars; Hypopigmentation (more visible in skin of colour), hypertrichosis, steroid acne/folliculitis; Delayed wound healing and ulceration. Infections masked or changed by the steroid (tinea incognita, crusted scabies, spread of HSV); Allergic contact dermatitis to the steroid or a preservative. Suspect it when eczema gets worse on treatment; Systemic effects (large areas, occlusion, very potent steroids, infants): HPA-axis suppression, iatrogenic Cushing's, growth suppression, hyperglycaemia. Periocular use → glaucoma, cataract; Grading scales exist (e.g. atrophy and telangiectasia scored 0–3 in trials — the "objective gradings" a classmate posted). In practice, record the sign, the site and the steroid history

    06

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    Pharmacist / IP

    Fingertip units (FTU) and quantities

    • 1 FTU is the ointment squeezed from a tube with a 5 mm nozzle, from the distal skin crease to the tip of an adult index finger. It weighs about 0.5 g and covers two adult palms with fingers together (~2% BSA). For children, still measure on an adult finger; fewer FTUs are needed per site.
    • Adult FTUs per application (Long & Finlay 1991; BAD/NES charts): face & neck 2.5; one arm 3; one hand (both sides) 1; one leg 6; one foot 2; front of trunk 7; back incl. buttocks 7. The whole adult body takes about 40 FTU (≈20 g) per application.
    • BNF suitable quantities (adult, twice daily for 1 week): face & neck 15–30 g; both hands 15–30 g; scalp 15–30 g; both arms 30–60 g; both legs 100 g; trunk 100 g; groins & genitalia 15–30 g. These are rounded teaching values — recheck the current BNF table.
    • Practical prescribing: state the name, potency, site, frequency, duration and number of FTUs on the label/instructions, not "use as directed". MHRA asks that packaging and counselling make the potency clear. Apply the emollient first and wait ~15–30 minutes before the steroid (or always use the same order). The gap and the quantity matter more than the order.

    MHRA Drug Safety Update (Sept 2021): topical steroid withdrawal (TSW) reactions

    • Rare but potentially severe reactions after long-term, continuous or inappropriate use of topical steroids, mainly moderate-to-high potency, typically on stopping.
    • Features: redness that may look like a flare but spreads beyond the treated area; burning or stinging worse than the original condition; peeling; oedema; oozing. The "red sleeve" sign (forearm erythema sparing the palms) and facial/eyelid redness are described.
    • Advice to prescribers and pharmacists: Use the lowest potency for the shortest time; Review patients on long-term use, and reduce gradually rather than stopping abruptly after prolonged use; If TSW is suspected, seek specialist advice rather than restarting the same potent steroid as if it were a "flare"; Report via Yellow Card, and counsel on potency and duration
    • TSW has to be told apart from a rebound flare, contact allergy and the underlying disease, which often needs dermatology.

    Tachyphylaxis

    • A reduced response with continuous use. In experimental models the vasoconstriction fades within days, but how much this matters clinically is debated. Poor adherence and a wrong diagnosis are commoner reasons that "the steroid stopped working".
    • What to do: Check adherence, quantity used and the diagnosis (tinea, scabies, contact allergy, infection); Use intermittent or pulsed regimens (e.g. potent once daily, then weekends only) or short rest periods; Step up briefly, then down; Or switch to a steroid-sparing agent (TCI; vitamin D in psoriasis; phototherapy)

    Steroid-phobic patients (Sophie's and Siddharth's experience)

    • Explore the patient's beliefs (online TSW content is common). Explain potency, FTU and duration, and that untreated eczema causes more lichenification and infection than correctly used TCS. Offer written plans, TCIs for face/flexures, and follow-up.

    Red flags / refer

    • Suspected TSW, contact allergy to a steroid (patch testing), or widespread atrophy/striae.
    • Adult eczema not controlled after 4 weeks of correct potent TCS + emollients, or children meeting the NICE CG57 referral criteria; facial eczema needing repeated moderate/potent steroid; diagnostic doubt.
    • Eczema herpeticum (same day), erythroderma, suspected HPA suppression or Cushingoid features.
    • Children who need very potent steroids, or potent steroids on the face/flexures.

    IP scope

    • Starting and continuing mild–potent TCS for common dermatoses, within competence and the local formulary, is routine IP practice.
    • Very potent steroids, steroids in infants, genital lichen sclerosus regimens and TCIs may be within competence after training, but follow local formulary/shared-care rules.
    • TCIs in under-2s and any long-term facial/eyelid plan are specialist decisions.

    Worked cases

    Case 1: moderate–severe eczema with steroid atrophy

    • Signs of overuse: thin, shiny, translucent skin; fine wrinkling; telangiectasia; purpura/bruising; striae; hypopigmentation; perioral dermatitis, steroid rosacea or acne; infection modified by the steroid (tinea incognita); delayed healing; TSW on stopping.
    • What to advise: potency and site rules; FTU quantity; duration and step-down; stinging on application; signs of infection and allergy; do not stop abruptly after long-term use (MHRA TSW); periocular risks (glaucoma/cataract); a steroid-sparing plan; emollients; fire risk from paraffin-soaked fabrics.
    • Alternative: a topical calcineurin inhibitor (tacrolimus 0.03%/0.1% ointment or pimecrolimus 1% cream). It blocks calcineurin/IL-2 T-cell activation without collagen loss, so there is no atrophy — suitable for face, eyelids, flexures and long-term proactive twice-weekly use. Counsel about initial burning and sun protection. Alongside it: generous emollients, and referral for phototherapy or systemic therapy if still uncontrolled.
    • Mechanism, types, potency: see Topical corticosteroids (four UK classes; vasoconstrictor assay).
    • All via GR? Mostly, via cytosolic GR (genomic transactivation/transrepression). There are also non-genomic effects via membrane GR, cytosolic GR signalling, at high concentration, non-specific membrane effects (see Topical corticosteroids).
    • Questions 6–8: see Vitamin D analogues, coal tar (vitamin D analogues; coal tar — Pott 1775, scrotal SCC; cohort data reassuring) and (phototherapy/PUVA). Tachyphylaxis: see Topical corticosteroids.

    Case 2: retinoids and steroid contraindications

    • Class/MoA/uses: see Retinoids. Topical retinoids in pregnancy: low absorption and no clear signal, but contraindicated in pregnancy and when planning (EMA 2018/MHRA). Inadvertent exposure → stop, reassure, contact UKTIS.
    • 8 contraindications: see Topical corticosteroids (untreated bacterial/fungal/viral infection, scabies, rosacea, perioral dermatitis, acne, ulcers/wounds; caution in widespread psoriasis, infants and allergy).
    • Combination creams: use briefly for genuinely infected eczema, then switch to the plain steroid; recheck the diagnosis if it does not settle.

    Case 3: 19-year-old with mild–moderate acne

    • Question 1: skin care + a 12-week fixed-combination topical (adapalene/BPO, tretinoin/clindamycin or BPO/clindamycin) per NICE NG198. Review at 12 weeks; maintain with a topical retinoid; screen mood; refer if failing or scarring. No antibiotic monotherapy; no minocycline.
    • Question 2: yes. COCs reduce inflammatory and comedonal acne (↑ SHBG, ↓ ovarian androgen) and match oral antibiotics at 6 months. The evidence is best for mild–moderate and hormonally influenced lower-face acne; benefit takes 3–6 months.
    • Question 3: if she wants contraception, start with a low-VTE (levonorgestrel) COC, checked against UKMEC. Move to drospirenone/desogestrel options if needed. Use co-cyprindiol only for moderate–severe acne after topicals + an oral antibiotic have failed, with VTE counselling and a stop plan (MHRA 2013). Mechanism as above, plus androgen-receptor blockade by cyproterone/drospirenone.
    • Acne fulminans: systemic, ulcerating, haemorrhagic acne. It needs dermatology within 24 h for urgent oral steroids to prevent scarring and manage the systemic illness; it is often triggered by isotretinoin.

    Case 4: 25-year-old with localised facial hirsutism

    • Question 1: most likely idiopathic/peripheral hirsutism (normal androgens, regular cycles). Check SHBG/FAI, consider HbA1c, ± mid-luteal progesterone to confirm ovulation. Then: weight loss; cosmetic methods, laser or electrolysis; eflornithine only if still obtainable (now discontinued in UK dm+d); a COC if distress persists; anti-androgens only via a specialist and with contraception; psychological support; red flags for referral (see Hirsutism case and the Ferriman–Gallwey score).
    • Question 2: mFG = 9 areas × 0–4, total 36. The traditional cut-off is ≥8 (≥4–6 in some ethnic groups). Use it to track response, not as the only basis for decisions (see Hirsutism case and the Ferriman–Gallwey score).

    Case 5: biologics

    See Biologics, JAK inhibitors and conventional systemics — the primary-care interface: targets (TNF, IL-12/23, IL-23, IL-17; IL-4Rα/IL-13; JAK); NICE eligibility (psoriasis: PASI ≥10 and DLQI >10 after a conventional systemic; AE: after systemic failure). The primary-care role: record the drug, check interactions, no live vaccines, recognise infections and AEs, and do shared care where it applies.

    Practice case: 6-year-old with AE not responding to 1% hydrocortisone

    • QA = 1, aqueous cream (SLS → irritation and barrier damage; MHRA 2013).
    • QB = 4, 250–500 g/week (NICE CG57).
    • QC = 3: 5 mm nozzle, from the distal crease to the tip of an adult index finger (≈0.5 g; covers two adult palms).
    • Extra teaching: a 6-year-old failing hydrocortisone 1% on the body probably needs a moderate steroid (e.g. clobetasone butyrate 0.05%) for 7–14 days during flares, adequate emollient quantities, and review for infection or an alternative diagnosis. Give fire-safety advice.
    07

    Red flags & referral

    Escalate
    • Rash worse or spreading on a steroid with scale at the edge: scrape for tinea incognito before going on.
    • Facial redness, burning and rebound after stopping a potent steroid: think steroid rosacea / topical steroid withdrawal (MHRA 2021).
    • Large quantities of potent or very potent steroid in a child or under occlusion: risk of adrenal suppression, so review and seek advice.
    08

    Prescriber pearls

    • "Right potency, right place, right amount, right duration — then step down."
    • Write the FTU count on the prescription (e.g. "apply 2.5 FTU to face once daily for 5 days then stop").
    • Inflamed skin may sting briefly with creams/TCIs. Persistent burning or worsening needs review (allergy, TSW, infection).
    • Eyelids: mild steroid for a few days only; TCI preferred for recurrent eyelid eczema. Consider ophthalmology if periocular steroid has been used long-term (IOP).
    • Skin of colour: atrophy and hypopigmentation are more conspicuous — warn patients.
    • Never sell or supply potent steroids (e.g. clobetasol creams) for skin lightening. Illegal cosmetic use causes severe atrophy, striae and Cushing's (relevant in community pharmacy).
    • Paraffin-based steroid preparations soaked into dressings/clothing are a fire hazard (the same MHRA 2018 warning as for emollients).
