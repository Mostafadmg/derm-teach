    Infantile haemangioma & port-wine stain

    Module 6 · Week 1

    A haemangioma is a tumour that grows fast, then involutes. A port-wine stain is a malformation that persists. Refer high-risk haemangiomas early, while propranolol works best.

    Coversinfantile haemangiomastrawberry naevusstrawberry markhaemangiomaport-wine staincapillary malformationSturge-WeberPHACEpropranololtimololvascular birthmark
    Think of it when

    A bright red raised ‘strawberry’ (or bluish deep) lesion appearing in the first weeks and growing fast, or a flat red-purple stain present at birth.

    What drives it

    Haemangioma: clonal GLUT-1-positive endothelial proliferation. Port-wine stain: dilated dermal capillaries.

    Backbone of care

    Most haemangiomas: watchful waiting with photos. High-risk ones: early specialist referral for propranolol.

    Never miss

    Periocular, airway or beard, lip, ulcerated, large facial segmental (PHACE), or 5 or more lesions (liver).

    What it is

    Infantile haemangioma (IH)Port-wine stain (capillary malformation)
    NatureBenign vascular tumourVascular malformation
    At birthAbsent or a faint precursor markPresent, flat red or purple
    CourseRapid growth (mostly in the first 1–3 months, largely complete by about 5 months), plateau, then involution over yearsGrows with the child; may darken or thicken; never involutes
    FrequencyAbout 4–10% of infantsAbout 0.3%
    2

    How it’s caused

    • IH: clonal endothelial proliferation, GLUT-1 positive. Risk factors: prematurity, low birth weight, female sex, multiple pregnancy, placental anomalies.
    • Port-wine stain: dilated dermal capillaries (often a GNAQ mosaic mutation).
    3

    Clinical features

    IH typeAppearance
    SuperficialBright red, raised, well-defined (‘strawberry’)
    DeepBluish, rubbery swelling under the skin
    MixedBoth

    Haemangiomas can occur anywhere, most often on the head and neck. 5 or more skin haemangiomas raise concern for liver haemangiomas. White surface change can herald ulceration.

    4

    Diagnosis

    • Usually clinical.
    • Ultrasound if deep, or uncertain against a malformation or soft-tissue mass. MRI for complex facial or segmental lesions.
    • Abdominal ultrasound if there are 5 or more skin lesions.
    5

    Differentials

    Look-alikeDistinguishing clues
    Port-wine stainPresent at birth, flat, grows with the child, no rapid tumour growth
    Congenital haemangiomaFully formed at birth; different natural history
    Venous or lymphatic malformationSoft, compressible, persistent; imaging differs
    Pyogenic granulomaLater onset; often a pedunculated bleeding papule
    6

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    Uncomplicated small haemangioma

    Active observation with photo documentation. Reassure parents about involution.

    Refer promptly (ideally by about 4 weeks of age if high risk)

    • Periocular (risk to vision and amblyopia)
    • Beard or airway distribution (risk of stridor)
    • Lip or perioral (feeding)
    • Nasal tip, large facial, or segmental (PHACE risk with large facial segmental lesions)
    • Perineal or flexural (ulceration)
    • Ulcerated, bleeding or rapidly enlarging
    • 5 or more skin lesions
    • Breast (girls) or large scalp lesions

    Specialist treatment

    • Oral propranolol is first line for problematic IH. It is started and supervised by a specialist, with heart rate, BP and glucose monitoring. Feed regularly, and hold doses if the baby is unwell or not feeding.
    • Topical timolol for selected small, thin, superficial lesions (off-label; NICE ESUOM47).
    • Wound care, analgesia and antibiotics for ulceration; laser or surgery for selected residual changes.

    Port-wine stain

    Reassure for small, uncomplicated stains. Pulsed dye laser is specialist, and earlier childhood referral is often preferred. Facial PWS in the V1 distribution: consider Sturge–Weber (neurology and ophthalmology for glaucoma and seizures). Limb PWS with overgrowth: consider Klippel–Trénaunay and related syndromes.

    7

    Red flags & referral

    • High-risk haemangioma sites (listed above): do not wait to see if it goes away.
    • Stridor or noisy breathing with a beard-area haemangioma: urgent.
    • Ulcerated or bleeding haemangioma.
    • Facial segmental port-wine stain with eye or neurological symptoms, or limb asymmetry.
    8

    Prescriber pearls

    • The rapid growth window is early, so late referral misses the easiest window for treatment.
    • A malformation is not a tumour, so be clear with parents whether it is a PWS or an IH.
    • IP role: recognise high-risk sites, explain that beta-blockers are specialist-supervised, and support dressings for ulceration under the specialist plan.
    • BAD patient leaflet and the Birthmark Support Group are useful for families.
