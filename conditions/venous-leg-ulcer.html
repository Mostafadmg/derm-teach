    Venous leg ulcer

    The commonest chronic leg ulcer: a shallow, sloping, exudative ulcer in the gaiter area. Compression after ABPI is the cornerstone. Colonised does not mean infected.

    Also calledvenous ulcerstasis ulcergravitational ulcerleg ulcercompressionABPI+3

    Key facts

    • Venous leg ulcers are the commonest chronic leg ulcers (about 70%), caused by venous hypertension.
    • Gaiter area, especially over the medial malleolus; shallow, sloping-edged, exudative ulcer with haemosiderin, venous eczema and lipodermatosclerosis.
    • Always check ABPI before high compression to exclude significant arterial disease.
    • Compression is the cornerstone; plus elevation, walking, wound care and treatment of venous eczema.
    • Refer if not healing after about 2 weeks of optimal care, atypical, mixed arterial disease or for venous intervention (NICE CG168).

    Overview

    A venous leg ulcer is full-thickness skin loss caused by chronic venous insufficiency (venous hypertension). It is the most common chronic leg ulcer. Other names are stasis ulcer and gravitational ulcer. Avoid calling it a ‘varicose ulcer’ if there are no varicose veins.

    Epidemiology

    • Who
      • Older adults, more women; prevalence rises steeply after 65.
      • Risk factors: varicose veins, previous DVT, obesity, immobility, multiple pregnancies, leg trauma.
    • Burden
      • Often chronic and recurrent: many recur within a year without ongoing compression.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    • Valve incompetence, previous DVT, obesity, immobility and calf-muscle pump failure cause venous hypertension.
    • This leads to oedema, haemosiderin deposition, inflammation and lipodermatosclerosis, with poor oxygen and nutrient delivery.
    • The skin then breaks down after minor trauma and fails to heal.

    Risk factors and associations

    • Calf-muscle pump failure (immobility, ankle stiffness, arthritis).
    • Post-thrombotic syndrome after DVT.

    Myths to address

    • "Swab every ulcer": all chronic ulcers grow bacteria; swab only if clinically infected.
    • "Compression is unsafe in the elderly": it is safe once ABPI shows adequate arterial supply.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    Persistently high pressure in the leg veins damages the skin from the inside.

    1. 1

      Valve failure

      Incompetent valves in superficial, perforating or deep veins (often after DVT) let blood reflux downwards.

    2. 2

      Venous hypertension

      Walking normally lowers ankle venous pressure; with reflux or pump failure it stays high (ambulatory venous hypertension).

    3. 3

      Capillary leak

      High pressure stretches capillaries; fluid, red cells (later haemosiderin) and fibrinogen leak into the tissues, causing oedema and brown staining.

    4. 4

      Inflammation and fibrosis

      White cells trapped in capillaries release enzymes and cytokines; the dermis and fat scar (lipodermatosclerosis) and oxygen delivery falls.

    5. 5

      Ulceration

      Minor trauma breaks the fragile skin, which cannot heal while venous pressure stays high: compression reverses this.

    Histopathology

    • Not needed routinely. Biopsy a non-healing or atypical ulcer (rolled edge, exuberant tissue) to exclude SCC (Marjolin ulcer) or BCC.

    Clinical features

    • Gaiter area, most commonly over the medial malleolus.
    • Shallow ulcer with sloping edges, often heavily exudative.
    • Surrounding skin: varicose veins, brown haemosiderin staining, venous eczema, atrophie blanche, and lipodermatosclerosis (the ‘inverted champagne bottle’ leg).
    • Dependent oedema, worse when the leg is down.
    • Pain is usually milder than with arterial ulcers, unless the ulcer is infected.

    Special groups

    • Mixed arterial–venous disease
      • ABPI below 0.8 needs reduced or no compression and vascular referral; diabetes can give a falsely high ABPI.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    • Clinical, with signs of venous disease.
    • ABPI (ankle–brachial pressure index) before any high compression, to exclude significant arterial disease.
    • Venous duplex if intervention is planned.
    • Swab only if there are clinical signs of infection.

    ABPI

    • (ankle–brachial pressure index) before any high compression, to exclude significant arterial disease.

    Key points

    • Clinical, with signs of venous disease.
    • Venous duplex if intervention is planned.
    • Swab only if there are clinical signs of infection.

    Management

    Based on NICE CG168 and local leg ulcer and tissue viability pathways.

    • Compression (multilayer bandaging or hosiery) is the cornerstone, once ABPI is satisfactory.
    • Leg elevation, walking and ankle exercises, weight management, and emollients.
    • Treat venous eczema with short courses of mild to potent topical steroid around (not in) the ulcer.
    • Wound care: moist healing and debridement by tissue viability. Do not use topical antibiotics routinely.
    • Infection: give systemic antibiotics only for spreading cellulitis or systemic signs. Colonisation is not infection.
    • Pentoxifylline is sometimes used as an adjunct, following specialist or local policy.

    Referral

    • Don’t

      Low or abnormal ABPI, absent pulses, rest pain or a cold foot: arterial disease, so do not compress. Refer to vascular.

    • Routine

      Spreading redness, fever or systemic upset: cellulitis or sepsis.

    • Routine

      Rolled, raised or irregular edge, bleeding, or failure to heal: biopsy to exclude SCC or BCC (Marjolin ulcer).

    • Routine

      Rapidly enlarging, very painful ulcer with a purple edge: suspect pyoderma gangrenosum.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Low or abnormal ABPI, absent pulses, rest pain or a cold foot: arterial disease, so do not compress. Refer to vascular.
    • Spreading redness, fever or systemic upset: cellulitis or sepsis.
    • Rolled, raised or irregular edge, bleeding, or failure to heal: biopsy to exclude SCC or BCC (Marjolin ulcer).
    • Rapidly enlarging, very painful ulcer with a purple edge: suspect pyoderma gangrenosum.

    Refer

    To vascular surgery, tissue viability or dermatology if: not healing after about 2 weeks of optimal care, atypical appearance, mixed arterial disease, or for venous intervention (NICE: refer for venous assessment).

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Cellulitis; contact allergy to dressings and topical treatments (common in leg ulcer patients).
    • Chronic pain, reduced mobility and isolation.
    • Malignant change in long-standing ulcers (rare).

    Prognosis

    • Most heal within months with good compression; recurrence is common, so lifelong compression hosiery is advised after healing.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • Never start high compression without a vascular assessment or ABPI.

    • Explain lifelong compression stockings after healing, because recurrence is common.

    • Good analgesia and exudate control improve adherence to compression.

    • Topical steroids on surrounding venous eczema help. Watch for contact allergy to dressings and topical products, which is common in leg ulcer patients.

    Self-check quiz

    5 questions · optional
    1. Q1When should you think of Venous leg ulcer?Reveal

      A shallow, sloping, wet ulcer over the medial malleolus with haemosiderin staining, venous eczema, varicose veins and oedema that is worse at the end of the day.

    2. Q2What drives it?Reveal

      Venous hypertension from incompetent valves, previous DVT or calf-pump failure, causing oedema, inflammation and skin breakdown.

    3. Q3What is the backbone of care?Reveal

      ABPI, then multilayer compression, elevation, walking, emollient, and lifelong stockings after healing.

    4. Q4What must you never miss?Reveal

      Arterial disease before compressing, and a non-healing or atypical ulcer (malignancy, PG, vasculitis).

    5. Q5Name a red flag that needs escalation.Reveal

      Low or abnormal ABPI, absent pulses, rest pain or a cold foot: arterial disease, so do not compress. Refer to vascular.
