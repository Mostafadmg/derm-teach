    Viral warts (HPV)

    Benign HPV-driven epidermal growths. Most clear on their own. Salicylic acid is the community first line, cryotherapy is clinic-based, and refer the immunosuppressed and anything atypical.

    Also calledwartsverrucaverrucaeplantar wartcommon wartplane warts+5
    Related pagesPerineal dermatosesSeborrhoeic keratosisCutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC)

    Key facts

    • Benign epidermal growths from human papillomavirus (HPV) infection of keratinocytes.
    • Very common in school-age children; spread by direct contact, wet floors and autoinoculation.
    • Common, plantar (verrucae), plane, filiform and periungual types; thrombosed capillaries (black dots) are typical.
    • Most clear spontaneously: half within 6 months in children, about 90% within 2 years; treat only if painful or distressing.
    • Options: salicylic acid paints and occlusion, cryotherapy; refer atypical, extensive, facial or immunosuppressed cases.

    Overview

    Viral warts (verrucae) are very common benign growths of the epidermis caused by cutaneous human papillomavirus (HPV). This page covers cutaneous warts. Anogenital warts belong to sexual health and are counselled separately.

    Epidemiology

    • Who
      • Up to a third of school-age children have warts at any time; less common in adults.
      • Meat and fish handlers (HPV-7 'butcher's warts'), people with eczema and the immunosuppressed (transplant recipients) are at higher risk.
    • Spread
      • Direct contact, swimming pools and changing rooms, shaving, nail biting and picking; incubation up to 12 months.

    Aetiology: causes and risk factors

    • HPV is a double-stranded DNA virus. Common cutaneous types are 1, 2, 3, 4, 10, 27, 29 and 57.
    • It infects basal keratinocytes, producing hyperkeratosis and new virus particles.
    • Spread is by skin-to-skin contact, fomites (swimming pools, changing rooms), and autoinoculation. A line of warts along a scratch is the pseudo-Koebner phenomenon.
    • Incubation takes weeks to months. The wart burden is higher with dermatitis or immunosuppression (transplant, HIV).

    Risk factors and associations

    • HPV types: 1 (plantar myrmecia), 2 and 4 (common and plantar), 3 and 10 (plane), 7 (butchers), 27 and 57.
    • Risk factors: barrier damage, moist skin, trauma, immunosuppression (e.g. azathioprine, ciclosporin, HIV).

    Myths to address

    • "Warts have roots": they are purely epidermal; aggressive digging causes scars.
    • "Children must stay out of swimming": not necessary; covering verrucae with a plaster or sock is enough.

    Pathophysiology and pathology

    HPV infects the basal layer through tiny breaks in the skin and hijacks keratinocyte growth.

    1. 1

      Entry through micro-trauma

      Virus reaches basal keratinocytes through small breaks; maceration and trauma help it enter.

    2. 2

      Viral genes drive proliferation

      Early viral proteins (E6, E7) push infected cells to keep dividing, producing acanthosis, papillomatosis and hyperkeratosis.

    3. 3

      Virus made at the surface

      New virus is assembled in upper layers (koilocytes) and shed in skin flakes, spreading to other sites (lines of warts from scratching).

    4. 4

      Capillaries and black dots

      Dermal papillae with dilated capillaries extend up into the wart; clotted vessels show as black dots, unlike corns.

    5. 5

      Immune clearance

      Cell-mediated immunity eventually recognises the virus and clears it, which is why most warts go without treatment.

    Histopathology

    • Papillomatosis, acanthosis and hyperkeratosis with parakeratosis over tips of papillae.
    • Koilocytes (vacuolated keratinocytes with shrunken nuclei) and large keratohyaline granules in the upper epidermis.
    • Biopsy only for atypical, persistent or bleeding lesions (exclude SCC in the immunosuppressed).

    Types to recognise

    • Common

      Rough hyperkeratotic papules with capillary dots. Site: Hands, knees, nails.

    • Plantar

      Inward-growing and painful, or ‘mosaic’ clusters. Site: Soles.

    • Plane

      Flat-topped, skin-coloured, numerous; spread by shaving. Site: Face, hands, shins.

    • Filiform

      Thin fronds on a narrow base. Site: Face.

    • Periungual

      Around or under the nail; nail dystrophy risk. Site: Nail folds.

    Special groups

    • Immunosuppressed
      • Warts are often numerous and resistant; any growing, painful or bleeding lesion in a transplant recipient needs urgent review (SCC).
    • Children
      • Watchful waiting is reasonable; avoid painful treatments in young children.

    Diagnosis and investigations

    • Clinical. Paring reveals pinpoint bleeding or black dots. Dermoscopy helps.
    • Plantar wart vs corn: a wart is tender when squeezed from the sides. A corn is tender on direct pressure, and the skin lines run through it.
    • Biopsy if SCC is a concern, especially in immunosuppressed patients.

    Look

    • Clinical diagnosis.
    • Paring shows pinpoint bleeding or black dots; dermoscopy helps.

    Squeeze test

    • Wart: tender when squeezed from the sides.
    • Corn: tender on direct pressure; skin lines run through.

    Who is at risk

    • Immunosuppression (transplant, HIV), dermatitis.
    • Diabetes or neuropathy changes treatment choice.

    Tests

    • Usually none.
    • Biopsy if SCC is a concern, especially if immunosuppressed.

    Management

    Many warts resolve without treatment: roughly half of childhood warts within 6 months, and most within 2 years. Treat if they are painful, disabling, in an immunosuppressed patient, or if the patient prefers.

    First line (community)

    • Salicylic acid paint, gel or plaster daily. Soak and pare (or file) first. Persist for 8–12 weeks. Protect the surrounding skin, and avoid in diabetes or neuropathy with poor sensation.
    • Cryotherapy (liquid nitrogen) is clinic-based and needs repeated sessions. It can blister and hurt, and may cause hypo- or hyperpigmentation (more so in skin of colour). Take care over superficial nerves.

    Second line / specialist (BAD guideline themes)

    Curettage or electrosurgery (scarring risk), radiofrequency cautery, CO₂ laser, intralesional bleomycin, contact immunotherapy (e.g. diphencyprone), microwave (plantar), and podophyllin or imiquimod (better evidence for anogenital than cutaneous warts).

    Caution

    Prescribing limits and pitfalls

    • Expectations: No treatment ‘kills’ HPV instantly; recurrence is common until immunity clears it.
    • Duct tape: Mixed evidence: optional add-on, not a cure.
    • Feet in diabetes: No self-treatment with salicylic acid or cryotherapy.

    Referral

    • Urgent

      Rapid growth, bleeding, ulceration or atypical look: consider SCC.

    • Routine

      Immunosuppressed with extensive or recalcitrant warts → dermatology.

    • Routine

      Extensive facial or periungual warts where destruction risks scarring or nail damage.

    • Don’t

      Diabetes, neuropathy or vascular disease: no self-treatment on the feet.

    Red flags and when to refer

    • Rapid growth, bleeding, ulceration or an atypical appearance: consider SCC and refer.
    • Immunosuppressed patient (transplant, HIV) with extensive or recalcitrant warts: refer to dermatology.
    • Extensive facial or periungual warts where destructive treatment risks scarring or nail damage.
    • Diabetes, peripheral neuropathy or vascular disease: avoid self-treatment with salicylic acid or cryotherapy on the feet.

    Complications and prognosis

    Complications

    • Pain on walking (plantar warts); nail dystrophy (periungual).
    • Spread to other sites and people; psychosocial distress.
    • Scarring from destructive treatment.
    • Rarely, malignant change (SCC) in immunosuppressed or epidermodysplasia verruciformis.

    Prognosis

    • Children: about 50% clear in 6 months and 90% in 2 years without treatment.
    • Adults' warts are more persistent and recur more often.

    Prescriber pearls (UK pharmacist IP)

    • No treatment ‘kills’ HPV instantly. Treatment removes virus-bearing skin, and recurrence is common until immunity clears that type.

    • Duct-tape occlusion has mixed evidence. It is an optional add-on, not a cure.

    • Periungual warts: avoid aggressive destruction near the nail matrix.

    • Advise flip-flops in communal showers, not sharing towels, and covering verrucae for swimming.
