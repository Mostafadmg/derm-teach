    Viral warts (HPV)

    Benign HPV-driven epidermal growths. Most clear on their own. Salicylic acid is the community first line, cryotherapy is clinic-based, and refer the immunosuppressed and anything atypical.

    Overview

    Very commonBenignCutaneous HPVMost clear without treatment

    • Spot it

      Rough papule with black dots that interrupts the skin lines: hands, feet, around nails.

    • Why

      HPV infects basal keratinocytes → hyperkeratosis. Spread by contact, fomites, autoinoculation.

    • Treat

      Reassure, or salicylic acid daily after paring for 8–12 weeks, ± cryotherapy.

    • Never miss

      A fast-growing or ulcerating ‘wart’, especially if immunosuppressed, may be SCC. Refer.

    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    What it is

    Viral warts (verrucae) are very common benign growths of the epidermis caused by cutaneous human papillomavirus (HPV). This page covers cutaneous warts. Anogenital warts belong to sexual health and are counselled separately.

    Start: Why it happens
    2

    Step 2 of 9 · 1 min

    Why it happens

    A virus that makes the top layers of skin pile up.

    • HPV (double-stranded DNA). Common cutaneous types: 1, 2, 3, 4, 10, 27, 29, 57.
    • Infects basal keratinocytes → hyperkeratosis and new virus.
    • Spread: skin contact, pools and changing rooms, autoinoculation (a line along a scratch: pseudo-Koebner).
    • Incubation weeks to months. More warts with dermatitis or immunosuppression.
    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    How it’s caused

    • HPV is a double-stranded DNA virus. Common cutaneous types are 1, 2, 3, 4, 10, 27, 29 and 57.
    • It infects basal keratinocytes, producing hyperkeratosis and new virus particles.
    • Spread is by skin-to-skin contact, fomites (swimming pools, changing rooms), and autoinoculation. A line of warts along a scratch is the pseudo-Koebner phenomenon.
    • Incubation takes weeks to months. The wart burden is higher with dermatitis or immunosuppression (transplant, HIV).
    Next: What you’ll see
    3

    Step 3 of 9 · 1 min

    What you’ll see

    Match the look to the site.

    • Rough, hyperkeratotic papule.
    • Black or red dots: thrombosed capillaries.
    • Breaks the normal skin lines (corns do not).
    • Often several; may line up along a scratch.

    Types to recognise

    • Common

      Rough hyperkeratotic papules with capillary dots.

      Hands, knees, nails

    • Plantar

      Inward-growing and painful, or ‘mosaic’ clusters.

      Soles

    • Plane

      Flat-topped, skin-coloured, numerous; spread by shaving.

      Face, hands, shins

    • Filiform

      Thin fronds on a narrow base.

      Face

    • Periungual

      Around or under the nail; nail dystrophy risk.

      Nail folds

    Step 4 of 9 · 1 min

    Confirm the diagnosis

    Look

    • Clinical diagnosis.
    • Paring shows pinpoint bleeding or black dots; dermoscopy helps.

    Squeeze test

    • Wart: tender when squeezed from the sides.
    • Corn: tender on direct pressure; skin lines run through.

    Who is at risk

    • Immunosuppression (transplant, HIV), dermatitis.
    • Diabetes or neuropathy changes treatment choice.

    Tests

    • Usually none.
    • Biopsy if SCC is a concern, especially if immunosuppressed.
    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    Diagnosis

    • Clinical. Paring reveals pinpoint bleeding or black dots. Dermoscopy helps.
    • Plantar wart vs corn: a wart is tender when squeezed from the sides. A corn is tender on direct pressure, and the skin lines run through it.
    • Biopsy if SCC is a concern, especially in immunosuppressed patients.

    Treat

    Many resolve alone: about half of childhood warts in 6 months, most within 2 years.

    Treatment ladder

    1. 1

      FoundationWatchful waiting

      • Treat only if painful, disabling, immunosuppressed, or the patient prefers.
      • Flip-flops in communal showers; no shared towels; cover verrucae for swimming.
    2. 2

      Step 1First line · community

      Salicylic acid paint, gel or plaster

      Daily, after soaking and paring

      8–12 weeks

      Protect surrounding skin. Avoid in diabetes or poor sensation.

    3. 3

      Step 2Clinic

      Cryotherapy (liquid nitrogen)

      Repeated sessions

      Clinic-based

      Blistering, pain, pigment change (more in skin of colour); mind superficial nerves.

    4. 4

      SpecialistSpecialist

      • Curettage or electrosurgery (scarring), radiofrequency, CO₂ laser.
      • Intralesional bleomycin, contact immunotherapy (diphencyprone), microwave (plantar).
      • Podophyllin or imiquimod: better evidence for anogenital warts.

    Safety limits

    Expectations

    No treatment ‘kills’ HPV instantly; recurrence is common until immunity clears it.

    Duct tape

    Mixed evidence: optional add-on, not a cure.

    Feet in diabetes

    No self-treatment with salicylic acid or cryotherapy.

    Doses as in the source notes. Always confirm in the current BNF and local formulary.

    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    Management (UK pharmacist / IP)

    Many warts resolve without treatment: roughly half of childhood warts within 6 months, and most within 2 years. Treat if they are painful, disabling, in an immunosuppressed patient, or if the patient prefers.

    First line (community)

    • Salicylic acid paint, gel or plaster daily. Soak and pare (or file) first. Persist for 8–12 weeks. Protect the surrounding skin, and avoid in diabetes or neuropathy with poor sensation.
    • Cryotherapy (liquid nitrogen) is clinic-based and needs repeated sessions. It can blister and hurt, and may cause hypo- or hyperpigmentation (more so in skin of colour). Take care over superficial nerves.

    Second line / specialist (BAD guideline themes)

    Curettage or electrosurgery (scarring risk), radiofrequency cautery, CO₂ laser, intralesional bleomycin, contact immunotherapy (e.g. diphencyprone), microwave (plantar), and podophyllin or imiquimod (better evidence for anogenital than cutaneous warts).

    Next: Red flags & referral
    7

    Step 7 of 9 · 1 min

    Red flags & referral

    • Urgent

      Rapid growth, bleeding, ulceration or atypical look: consider SCC.

    • Routine

      Immunosuppressed with extensive or recalcitrant warts → dermatology.

    • Routine

      Extensive facial or periungual warts where destruction risks scarring or nail damage.

    • Don’t

      Diabetes, neuropathy or vascular disease: no self-treatment on the feet.

    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    Red flags & referral

    • Rapid growth, bleeding, ulceration or an atypical appearance: consider SCC and refer.
    • Immunosuppressed patient (transplant, HIV) with extensive or recalcitrant warts: refer to dermatology.
    • Extensive facial or periungual warts where destructive treatment risks scarring or nail damage.
    • Diabetes, peripheral neuropathy or vascular disease: avoid self-treatment with salicylic acid or cryotherapy on the feet.
    Next: Prescriber pearls
    8

    Step 8 of 9 · 1 min

    Prescriber pearls

    • Set expectations: weeks of daily treatment; recurrence is common.

    • Periungual warts: avoid aggressive destruction near the nail matrix.

    • Prevention: flip-flops, own towel, cover verrucae for swimming.

    • Anogenital warts belong to sexual health and are counselled separately.

    More detailfull notes · 1 min

    Prescriber pearls

    • No treatment ‘kills’ HPV instantly. Treatment removes virus-bearing skin, and recurrence is common until immunity clears that type.
    • Duct-tape occlusion has mixed evidence. It is an optional add-on, not a cure.
    • Periungual warts: avoid aggressive destruction near the nail matrix.
    • Advise flip-flops in communal showers, not sharing towels, and covering verrucae for swimming.

    Step 9 of 9 · 1 min

    Quick self-check

    1. Q1How do you tell a plantar wart from a corn?Reveal

      Wart: tender when squeezed from the sides, breaks the skin lines, black dots. Corn: tender on direct pressure, skin lines run through.

    2. Q2What are the black dots in a wart?Reveal

      Thrombosed capillaries.

    3. Q3Community first-line treatment and duration?Reveal

      Salicylic acid daily after soaking and paring, for 8–12 weeks.

    4. Q4Who should not self-treat warts on the feet?Reveal

      People with diabetes, peripheral neuropathy or vascular disease.

    5. Q5When is a ‘wart’ a red flag?Reveal

      Rapid growth, bleeding, ulceration or atypical look, especially if immunosuppressed: consider SCC and refer.
